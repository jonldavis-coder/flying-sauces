Hawaiian Chili Pepper Water

Chile, garlic, and water: clean, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Chili pepper water is a Hawaiian table condiment of hot chiles steeped in water with garlic, sometimes ginger or vinegar. Small local chiles became part of the islands’ cooking after the plant’s global spread. A bottle of it sits on diner counters beside soy sauce. This is a simple infused version.

Cultural background

It is shaken onto saimin, loco moco, grilled fish, and rice. It is closer to a chile vinegar or a light infusion than to a cooked salsa.

Why it tastes this way

Small hot chiles steeped in water with garlic taste clean and hot, like a table vinegar. It is thin so it can be shaken onto noodles and rice. A cooked ketchup would be a different condiment.

How it is made

Smashed chiles and garlic sit in hot water and a little vinegar until the water tastes hot, then the bottle is refrigerated.

Authentic touch

Let it sit overnight. The water tastes mild the hour you mix it and hotter the next day.

How it is used

Shake it over noodles, rice plates, and grilled fish.

Field notes

  • It is meant to be thin enough to sprinkle, like a table vinegar.
  • The islands’ diner counters helped make it a daily sauce, not a special-occasion one.

Ingredients

  • 6 Hawaiian or other small hot chiles, smashed
  • 2 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 1 cup just-boiled water
  • 1 tablespoon white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Put the chiles and garlic in a clean jar.
  2. Pour on the hot water, vinegar, and salt.
  3. Cool, then refrigerate overnight.
  4. Taste the next day and add a pinch more salt if needed.
  5. Use within two weeks.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Some recipes add a knob of ginger.
  • A fully fermented hot sauce is a different project.

Pairings

  • saimin
  • grilled fish
  • rice plates
