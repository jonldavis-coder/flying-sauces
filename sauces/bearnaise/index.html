Béarnaise

Warm butter emulsion with shallot, vinegar, and tarragon: herbal and sharp.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Béarnaise is a daughter of hollandaise flavored with a reduction of shallot, vinegar, and tarragon. It was popular in Paris restaurants in the nineteenth century. The name refers to the Béarn region, though the sauce as served with steak is a Paris restaurant sauce more than a farmhouse one.

Cultural background

It is the classic French steak sauce beside peppercorn and bordelaise. The tarragon is not optional in the traditional version.

Why it tastes this way

It tastes herbal and sharp because a vinegar, shallot, and tarragon reduction seasons the same butter emulsion as hollandaise. Tarragon, not lemon, is the perfume.

How it is made

Shallot, pepper, and tarragon stems reduce with vinegar, then that reduction flavors an egg-and-butter emulsion.

Authentic touch

Strain the reduction so the shallot does not stay in threads, then stir chopped tarragon leaves in at the end so they stay green.

How it is used

Serve with grilled steak, roasted potatoes, or grilled fish. It should be warm, not hot.

Field notes

  • Béarnaise is hollandaise plus a tarragon reduction, not a separate emulsion method.
  • The Béarn name outran the sauce’s actual regional origin.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons white wine vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons dry white wine
  • 1 shallot, minced
  • 2 sprigs tarragon, stems and leaves separated
  • 3 egg yolks
  • 1/2 cup unsalted butter, melted
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the vinegar, wine, shallot, and tarragon stems until about a tablespoon remains. Strain and cool slightly.
  2. Whisk the yolks with the reduction over gentle heat until thick.
  3. Whisk in the warm butter in a stream.
  4. Chop the tarragon leaves and stir them in. Season with salt.
  5. Hold the sauce warm, not hot, and serve the same day.

Keep the yolks below a simmer. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Sauce Choron replaces some of the tarragon with tomato.
  • Sauce Paloise uses mint instead of tarragon.

Pairings

  • grilled steak
  • frites
  • grilled asparagus
  • sole
