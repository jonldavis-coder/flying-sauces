Tapenade

Black olive, caper, and anchovy: salty, briny, and rich.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Tapenade is the Provençal olive paste. The name comes from tapeno, the Provençal word for capers, which belong in the paste along with olives and anchovy. It was written up in the late nineteenth century and is now a standard southern French spread.

Cultural background

It is eaten on bread, with raw vegetables, and on fish. Green-olive versions exist. The black-olive paste is the usual one.

Why it tastes this way

Olives, capers, and anchovy taste salty and briny because those are preserved pantry foods. The name points at capers. Olive oil binds them. A coarse chop tastes of the pieces. A puree tastes muddier.

How it is made

Olives, capers, anchovy, and olive oil are chopped or briefly pulsed to a coarse paste.

Authentic touch

Chop rather than puree, and do not add salt until you taste. Olives and anchovy are already salty.

How it is used

Spread it on toast or serve it as a dip. A spoonful can season grilled fish.

Field notes

  • The name refers to capers, not to olives.
  • A smooth puree is less traditional than a coarse chop.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups pitted black olives
  • 2 tablespoons capers
  • 2 anchovy fillets
  • 1 small garlic clove
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Chop the olives, capers, anchovy, and garlic together until coarse.
  2. Work in the oil and lemon.
  3. Taste before adding salt. The olives and anchovy are already salty.
  4. Serve at room temperature.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Green tapenade uses unripe olives and is sharper.
  • Leaving out the anchovy makes a different, meatless olive paste.

Pairings

  • toasted bread
  • raw fennel
  • grilled fish
