Vinegar, mustard, and oil: sharp, salty, and fresh.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1/2 cup

History

A vinaigrette is oil and vinegar, seasoned and often held together for a minute by mustard. French cooking made the ratio and the mustard emulsion a standard. It is a temporary emulsion unless mustard or another emulsifier is doing real work.

Cultural background

It is the default sauce for green salad in France, tossed at the last moment so the leaves stay crisp. Countless regional oils and vinegars change the flavor without changing the method.

Why it tastes this way

Sharpness is the vinegar, body is the oil, and mustard is what holds them together for a minute. Without mustard it separates, which is normal for a temporary emulsion. The vinegar you pick is the main flavor.

How it is made

Salt dissolves in vinegar, mustard joins them, and oil is whisked in.

Authentic touch

Dissolve the salt in the vinegar before the oil goes in. Salt will not dissolve well in oil.

How it is used

Dress green salads, lentil salads, and warm vegetables just before serving.

Field notes

  • Without mustard, a vinaigrette separates within minutes. That is normal.
  • The usual French starting ratio is about three parts oil to one part vinegar, adjusted to the vinegar’s strength.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil or olive oil
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir the vinegar, mustard, and salt until the salt dissolves.
  2. Whisk in the oil until the sauce looks lightly creamy.
  3. Add pepper.
  4. Toss with salad immediately.
  5. Whisk again if it separates.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A shallot and herbs make a sauce ravigote-like dressing.
  • Walnut oil or red wine vinegar changes the character without a new method.

Pairings

  • green salad
  • lentils
  • leeks vinaigrette
  • tomatoes
