Corned Beef Mustard Sauce

The next field stop is a nearby sauce on the same map.

Mustard, vinegar, and a little sugar: sharp, tangy, and warm.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3/4 cup

History

In Ireland, boiled bacon is the older salted-pork joint, often served with parsley sauce. Corned beef became the St. Patrick’s Day plate in the United States, where beef brisket was easier to buy than Irish bacon. A sharp mustard sauce is what many Irish-American tables put on that beef. Mustard also appears with cold meat in Ireland. This sauce is the sharp one for the corned-beef plate. It has no documented medieval author.

Cultural background

The beef is salty and fatty. Mustard and vinegar cut that salt. Sugar keeps the vinegar from tasting only sour. It is a table sauce, mixed and warmed, not a long gravy.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard is hot in the nose because of its own compounds, not because of chile. Vinegar keeps the sauce thin and sour. Sugar rounds the acid so it can sit on salty beef. Butter, if you use it, carries the mustard and softens the sharpness. A boil makes prepared mustard taste harsh, which is why the sauce is only warmed.

How it is made

Mustard, vinegar, and sugar are warmed with a little butter until they come together.

Authentic touch

Warm the sauce. Do not boil it. Serve it with corned beef, and remember that the older Irish joint is bacon, which more often takes parsley sauce.

How it is used

Spoon it over sliced corned beef, or serve it cold on a sandwich.

Field notes

  • Corned beef and cabbage is the Irish-American plate. Boiled bacon is the older Irish one.
  • The heat is mustard, not chile.

Ingredients

  • 1/3 cup prepared mustard
  • 2 tablespoons cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir the mustard, vinegar, sugar, and salt in a small pan.
  2. Add the butter.
  3. Warm over low heat until the butter melts and the sauce is smooth.
  4. Do not boil.
  5. Serve warm with corned beef.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Horseradish in place of some of the mustard is a sharper American version.
  • Leave out the butter if you want it dairy-free. The sauce will be thinner.

Pairings

  • corned beef
  • cabbage
  • potatoes
  • rye bread
