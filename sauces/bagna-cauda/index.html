Bagna Cauda

Anchovy, garlic, and butter or oil: hot, salty, and mellow.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Bagna cauda, a hot bath, is the Piedmontese dip of garlic, anchovy, and olive oil or butter, kept warm and eaten with raw and cooked vegetables. It is autumn and winter food of the vineyards, associated with the new wine. The garlic is cooked slowly so it loses its raw bite.

Cultural background

Cardoons are traditional when they are in season. Cabbage, peppers, and bread are the practical companions. It is a communal pot, not a pasta sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Garlic cooked slowly in milk or oil loses its raw burn and tastes sweet. Anchovy melts into that fat, so the dip tastes savory and mellow, kept warm so it stays fluid.

How it is made

Garlic simmers very gently in milk or oil until soft, then anchovy and butter or oil melt into a smooth dip.

Authentic touch

Cook the garlic until it mashes easily. Raw garlic in this dip is a mistake. Keep the pot warm, not frying.

How it is used

Keep it warm and dip vegetables and bread into it.

Field notes

  • The garlic is cooked until sweet. Raw garlic would be a different dip.
  • It is a warm dip for vegetables, which is why the pot stays on a low flame.

Ingredients

  • 1 head garlic, cloves peeled and sliced
  • 1/2 cup milk
  • 8 anchovy fillets
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • 1/3 cup olive oil

Steps

  1. Simmer the garlic in the milk until very soft, about 15 minutes. Drain it.
  2. Mash the garlic with the anchovy over low heat.
  3. Stir in the butter and oil until smooth. Do not fry it.
  4. Keep the sauce barely warm.
  5. Dip vegetables into it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • All olive oil, without butter, is a common version.
  • Cream is a richer restaurant finish and not required.

Pairings

  • cardoons
  • bell peppers
  • cabbage
  • bread
