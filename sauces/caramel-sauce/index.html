Caramel Sauce

Browned sugar and cream: bitter-sweet, buttery, and pourable.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Caramel is sugar cooked until it browns. A sauce is that caramel loosened with cream and butter. French pastry uses it constantly. The method is older than any one restaurant dessert. Salted caramel as a named fashion is more recent. Salt in the sauce is optional and traditional in some Breton cooking.

Cultural background

It is a dessert sauce for ice cream, apples, and cakes. Dulce de leche is milk cooked down, a different method and a different sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar cooked to amber tastes bitter-sweet. Cream and butter stop the cooking and make it pourable. Go darker than amber and it tastes burnt, not deeper.

How it is made

Sugar is cooked dry or with a little water to an amber caramel, then cream is added carefully and butter finishes it.

Authentic touch

Take the sugar off the heat before the cream goes in, and add warm cream slowly. Cold cream in a ripping-hot pan can seize and spit.

How it is used

Pour it warm over ice cream, baked apples, or cake.

Field notes

  • Steam erupts when cold cream hits hot caramel. Add it slowly.
  • The color should be amber. Black sugar is burnt, not deeper caramel.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup sugar
  • 2 tablespoons water
  • 1/2 cup heavy cream, warmed
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Cook the sugar and water without stirring until amber.
  2. Take the pan off the heat and whisk in the warm cream slowly.
  3. Whisk in the butter and salt.
  4. Cool until pourable.
  5. Reheat gently if it firms up in the refrigerator.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Salted caramel adds about 1/2 teaspoon of salt.
  • Butterscotch starts with brown sugar and is cooked less far.

Pairings

  • vanilla ice cream
  • baked apples
  • bread pudding
