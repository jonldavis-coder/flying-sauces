Cuban Salsa Criolla

Raw onion, garlic, and sour orange: sharp, crunchy, and bright.

History

Cuban salsa criolla is a raw onion sauce, sharpened with sour orange or vinegar and loosened with oil. It is served with fried fish and with roast pork. It is chunkier than mojo criollo, which is pounded garlic and oil. It is also not the Argentine salsa criolla of onion, tomato, and vinegar. The shared name is Spanish for a creole sauce. The recipes are different, and this page is the Cuban one.

Cultural background

Fish fries and pork roasts in Cuba put a bowl of this on the table so each person can add the acid. It is eaten the day it is made. Onion that sits for days turns harsh and watery.

Why it tastes this way

Raw onion is sharp because its sulfur compounds have not been cooked away. Sour orange, or lime, salts that bite and adds acid. Oil carries the garlic onto the fish. Salt draws water out of the onion, which is why the sauce turns juicy after a short rest instead of tasting dry.

How it is made

Onion, garlic, sour orange, oil, and cumin are stirred and rested. Nothing is cooked.

Authentic touch

Slice the onion thin, salt it, and let it stand 10 minutes before you add the acid. Keep it chunky. Blending it makes a mojo, not this salsa.

How it is used

Spoon it over fried fish or roast pork the day you make it.

Field notes

  • The name is shared with other countries. The Cuban sauce is onion, acid, and oil.
  • It is raw. Mojo criollo is the cooked or warmed garlic sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 large white onion, thinly sliced
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/4 cup sour orange juice, or lime juice
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cumin
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Salt the onion and let it stand 10 minutes.
  2. Stir in the garlic, juice, oil, and cumin.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Rest it 15 minutes so the onion softens slightly.
  5. Serve the same day with fish or pork.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • White vinegar can stand in for sour orange.
  • A little diced bell pepper is common. Tomato is the Argentine version, not the usual Cuban one.

Pairings

  • fried fish
  • roast pork
  • boiled yuca
