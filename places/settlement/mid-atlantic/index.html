Who settled the flavor

Mid-Atlantic

Dutch, German, Italian, and the sauces of the coastal cities.

The Mid-Atlantic was Dutch before it was English. New Netherland’s towns sat on the Hudson in the seventeenth century. That Dutch layer is real, and this guide does not invent a Dutch sauce to represent it. The German-speaking settlers later called Pennsylvania Dutch are a different migration. They were not from the Netherlands. Their wilted-lettuce dressing is the hot bacon dressing in this guide: bacon fat, vinegar, and sugar, poured on while it still steams.

Italian immigrants in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries built a Sunday gravy tradition in New York, Philadelphia, and other eastern cities: a long tomato sauce with meat, served over pasta. Vodka sauce is a later Italian-American restaurant sauce. Its exact first kitchen is disputed, so this guide does not award an inventor. Both are immigrant sauces. They are not colonial Dutch food.

Russian dressing is an American salad dressing from the early twentieth century. The name is not proof of a Russian origin, and this chapter does not assign it to one people. It is on the eastern table, beside the gravies that do have a clear immigrant line.
