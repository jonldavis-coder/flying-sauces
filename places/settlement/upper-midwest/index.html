Who settled the flavor

Upper Midwest and the North

German dairy, Scandinavian preserves, and Greek chili parlors.

German immigrants built dairies and breweries in Wisconsin. Beer cheese is that meeting: sharp cheddar melted with lager, mustard, and garlic. It is a dip and a sauce for pretzels and burgers, not a French mornay. Scandinavian immigrants brought their own preserves. Lingonberry sauce in this guide is the Swedish berry sauce. Upper Midwest tables still serve it with meatballs and pancakes because those families kept the practice. The berry and the recipe are Swedish. They were not invented in Minnesota.

Detroit coney sauce is a different migration. Greek and Macedonian lunch counters in Detroit serve a fine, saucy beef chili on a hot dog, with mustard and onion, and without beans in the pot. Cincinnati chili is the same broad history: Macedonian and Greek parlors, warm spices, spaghetti underneath. Empress Chili in 1922 is the usual Cincinnati account. Neither sauce is German, and neither is Scandinavian. Calling them a northern European gravy is a mistake.

Thousand Island dressing comes from the St. Lawrence River hotels at the northern edge of this map, a mayonnaise dressing with pickle and pepper. It is an American hotel sauce, and its inventor stories disagree, so none is repeated here.
