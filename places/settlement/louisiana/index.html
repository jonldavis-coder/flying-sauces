Who settled the flavor

Louisiana

French, Acadian, Spanish, West African, Caribbean, and Native kitchens, and the gravies they still share.

Louisiana’s sauces sit on more than one settlement. French colonists brought roux and a named sauce culture. After the British expelled the Acadians from Canada in 1755, many of those families reached Louisiana over the following years. Their descendants’ cooking is the Cajun stream: a dark roux, the celery-onion-pepper trinity, and gravies built for rice. Spanish rule, from the 1760s until the Louisiana Purchase in 1803, is part of the same colonial stack. None of these arrivals cooked in an empty country. Native nations, including Choctaw communities, were already there.

Enslaved West Africans and their descendants shaped the pot. Forced labor is the history, and so is skill: okra, rice knowledge, and pepper sauces are part of how Creole food tastes. Caribbean trade added another path for pepper and citrus. Creole, in the useful New Orleans sense, drew on French, Spanish, African, Caribbean, and later immigrant kitchens, often with butter and tomato. Cajun, in the useful southwest Louisiana sense, leaned on the roux. The labels help a reader. They are not a wall. Families crossed them.

In this guide the Louisiana sauces are remoulade, a brown roux gravy, Creole sauce, and the fermented pepper sauce. Remoulade is the cold, mustard-and-mayonnaise cousin of the French sauce, served with seafood. The brown gravy is the long-cooked roux. They are written as Louisiana sauces, not as a single people’s exclusive recipe.
