Who settled the flavor

Carolinas and the Deep South

Vinegar, mustard, and pepper, from more than one people.

English and Scottish settlers brought a taste for vinegar to the Carolinas. The eastern North Carolina whole-hog sauce is vinegar, salt, and pepper, thin on purpose. Lexington, farther west, adds a little tomato. Those are two sauces, and the pages keep them apart.

Enslaved West Africans and their descendants shaped the pits and the pepper. Barbecue in the Deep South is not a story that can be told as English hobby cooking. The labor was forced. The skill in the pit, the pepper, and the chopped meat is part of African American history. Nashville hot oil and Mississippi comeback sauce are later, specific sauces, written on their own pages rather than folded into one mythic Southern sauce.

South Carolina’s mustard sauce, yellow and sharp, is the Midlands style. The usual account ties it to German settlement in that part of the state. It is not the vinegar sauce of the North Carolina coast, and it is not the sweet tomato sauce of Kansas City. Alabama white sauce is mayonnaise, vinegar, and pepper, a twentieth-century Decatur style, not a colonial recipe. Memphis is a thinner tomato barbecue sauce. The map of the Deep South is these splits.
