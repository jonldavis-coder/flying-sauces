Adjika

Fresh chile, garlic, and herbs: hot, salty, and green or red.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Adjika is a chile and herb paste of Abkhazia and western Georgia. Fresh hot chiles, garlic, salt, and herbs such as cilantro and blue fenugreek are pounded together. Red and green versions depend on the chile. It is both a condiment and a seasoning for stews. Dry spice blends sold under the same name are a different product.

Cultural background

A little seasons a pot of beans or a grilled meat. It is salty enough to keep in the refrigerator as a paste. Megrelian cooking uses it heavily.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh chile, garlic, herbs, and salt taste hot and green or red because the paste is raw. Salt is both flavor and a way to keep it. A dry spice mix sold as adjika is a different product.

How it is made

Chiles, garlic, herbs, and salt are pounded or blended to a coarse paste. It is not cooked.

Authentic touch. Pound it coarse and salt it well. Use it by the spoonful. It is a seasoning paste, not a gravy.

How it is used

Serve a small spoonful with grilled meat, or stir a little into beans.

Field notes

  • The fresh paste and the dry spice mix sold as adjika are not the same thing.
  • Salt is part of how the paste is kept.

Ingredients

  • 6 fresh red chiles, stemmed
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 cup cilantro and parsley
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon white wine vinegar

Steps

  1. Blend or pound the chiles, garlic, herbs, and salt to a coarse paste.
  2. Stir in the vinegar.
  3. Taste. It should be very hot and salty.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Use by the spoonful.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green adjika uses unripe chiles and more herbs.
  • A cooked tomato adjika is a related sauce and a different texture.

Ají (Ecuador)

Tree tomato, hot chile, onion, and cilantro: tart, hot, and fresh.

15 min prep · 2 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Ecuadorian table ají is a raw or barely blended sauce of hot chile, tree tomato (tamarillo), onion, cilantro, and lime. It is served with hornado, llapingachos, and other highland plates. It is not Peruvian ají amarillo sauce, which is built on the yellow ají pepper and is often cooked. Tree tomato is the tart fruit that makes the Ecuadorian table sauce taste like itself.

Cultural background

A bowl sits on the table and each person spoons it on. The heat depends on the chile and on how many seeds are left in. The fruit keeps it from being only fire.

Why it tastes this way

Tree tomato is sharply tart, more than a ripe red tomato, which is why this ají tastes sour even before the lime. The chile is the heat and a little fruit of its own. Onion and cilantro stay raw, so the sauce tastes fresh. Salt and a spoon of water loosen it enough to drip off a spoon onto potatoes or pork.

How it is made

Tree tomato, chile, onion, cilantro, lime, and salt are blended with a little water until spoonable, not smooth as a puree.

Authentic touch. Use tree tomatoes if you can. Peel them after a short blanch. An ordinary red tomato is a different sauce. Leave the chile seeds in if you want the heat the table expects.

How it is used

Spoon it over hornado, potato cakes, or grilled meat. Make it the day you eat it.

Field notes

  • Tree tomato, not roma tomato, is the fruit in the usual highland ají.
  • Peruvian ají amarillo is a different chile sauce.

Ingredients

  • 4 tree tomatoes (tamarillos)
  • 1 or 2 hot red or orange chiles
  • 1/2 white onion, chopped
  • 1/2 cup cilantro leaves
  • Juice of 1 lime
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup water

Steps

  1. Score the tree tomatoes, cover them with boiling water for 1 minute, then peel them.
  2. Chop the chiles. Keep the seeds for more heat.
  3. Blend the tomatoes, chiles, onion, cilantro, lime, salt, and water until spoonable but not glassy.
  4. Taste for salt.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hotter chile makes a hotter bowl. The fruit stays the same.
  • Some tables add a little oil. The fruit and chile are the sauce.

Ají Amarillo Sauce

Yellow ají, onion, garlic, and oil: fruity, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Ají amarillo is the yellow chile at the center of much Peruvian cooking. A simple cooked sauce of the chile, onion, garlic, and oil is a base for stews and a table sauce. The chile is fruity rather than only hot. Pastes sold in jars are the practical way to cook it outside the Andes.

Cultural background

Peruvian kitchens use ají amarillo in causa, papa a la huancaína’s cousin sauces, and everyday saltados. This is the plain cooked chile sauce, not the cheese sauce huancaína.

Why it tastes this way

Ají amarillo tastes fruity as well as hot. Frying the paste with onion and garlic cooks off the raw edge and leaves that fruit in a savory sauce.

How it is made

Onion and garlic soften in oil, then ají paste or soaked dried ají is fried and loosened with water.

Authentic touch. Fry the paste in oil before you add water. Skipping the fry leaves a raw, dusty taste.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled chicken, potatoes, or rice, or stir a spoonful into a stew.

Field notes

  • Ají amarillo tastes fruity. Heat is only part of the point.
  • Jarred paste is a legitimate ingredient when fresh pods are unavailable.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/4 cup ají amarillo paste
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the garlic and ají paste and fry 2 minutes.
  3. Stir in the water and salt and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. Add the vinegar.
  5. Cool slightly and use as a spooning sauce.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Crema de ají blends the chile with mayonnaise and is milder.
  • Huancaína adds cheese and crackers and is a different sauce.

Ají Colombiano

Chile, scallion, and cilantro in vinegar: fresh and hot.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Ají is the Colombian fresh chile sauce, often with scallion, cilantro, and vinegar or lime, served alongside hogao rather than instead of it. The chile varies by region. It is a table hot sauce, not the cooked tomato base.

Cultural background

A bowl of aji sits on the table so each person heats their own plate. Hogao is the savory base. Ají is the heat.

Why it tastes this way

Raw chile and vinegar taste sharp and hot. Scallion and cilantro keep it fresh. It is thin enough to spoon, not a paste.

How it is made

Chile, scallion, cilantro, vinegar, and salt are chopped together.

Authentic touch. Chop it and dress it with vinegar. Serve it the same day beside the meal, not cooked into the beans.

How it is used

Spoon it on empanadas, grilled meat, or beans.

Field notes

  • Ají and hogao do different jobs on the same plate.
  • The heat depends on the local chile.

Ingredients

  • 4 fresh hot chiles, minced
  • 4 scallions, minced
  • 1/2 cup cilantro
  • 2 tablespoons vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Stir everything together.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A blended ají is more liquid.
  • Lime instead of vinegar is brighter.

Arrabbiata

Tomato, garlic, and dried chile: hot, sharp, and bright.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Sugo all’arrabbiata is a Roman tomato sauce whose name means angry, from the chile. Garlic, dried chile, tomato, and olive oil are the core. Pecorino is often added at the table and is not cooked into the sauce. It is a twentieth-century Roman trattoria sauce with older hot-tomato roots.

Cultural background

It is pasta sauce, usually for penne. The anger is chile heat, not a long list of spices.

Why it tastes this way

Dried chile bloomed in oil with garlic is the heat. Tomato stays bright because the simmer is short. The anger in the name is that chile, not a long spice list.

How it is made

Chile and garlic bloom in oil, tomato simmers briefly, and the sauce stays loose enough to coat pasta.

Authentic touch. Bloom the chile flakes in the oil before the tomato so the heat perfumes the fat. Add cheese at the table if you want it, not in the pot.

How it is used

Toss with penne. Add pecorino at the table if you want it.

Field notes

  • Arrabbiata means angry, and the anger is dried chile.
  • Cheese is a topping, not a required ingredient in the pot.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 1 can (28 ounces) tomatoes, crushed
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 12 ounces penne

Steps

  1. Warm the oil, garlic, and chile until the garlic softens.
  2. Add the tomatoes and salt.
  3. Simmer 15 minutes.
  4. Toss with boiled penne and pasta water.
  5. Serve with pecorino if you like.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Marinara without chile is the mild relative.
  • A spoon of chile oil at the end makes it hotter without a longer cook.

  • penne
  • pecorino
  • broccoli

Ata Din Din

Fried tomato and pepper: hot, savory, and thick.

15 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

Ata din din is the Yoruba fried pepper sauce: blended tomato, pepper, and onion fried in oil until the raw taste is gone and the sauce turns deep red. It is a base for eggs, boiled yam, and rice, and a cousin of the longer Nigerian stews. Palm oil or vegetable oil is used depending on the cook.

Cultural background

It is everyday sauce, hotter or milder with the pepper chosen. The fry is what separates it from a fresh pepper blend.

Why it tastes this way

Blended tomato and pepper fried in oil taste sweet and deep once the raw edge is gone and the oil separates. The fry is the difference from a fresh pepper blend.

How it is made

Tomato, pepper, and onion are blended, then fried in oil until thick and sweet.

Authentic touch. Fry the puree, stirring, until the color darkens and the oil shows. Pale sauce still tastes raw.

How it is used

Spoon it over rice, boiled yam, or eggs.

Field notes

  • Din din refers to the fried sauce, not a raw salsa.
  • The sauce is done when the oil separates and the raw pepper smell is gone.

Ingredients

  • 4 tomatoes
  • 2 red bell peppers
  • 1 scotch bonnet
  • 1 onion
  • 1/3 cup oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup meat or vegetable stock

Steps

  1. Blend the tomatoes, peppers, chile, and onion.
  2. Heat the oil and fry the puree, stirring, until it darkens and the oil shows, about 25 minutes.
  3. Add the stock and salt and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. The sauce should be thick.
  5. Serve with rice or yam.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Palm oil makes a different, more traditional pot for some cooks.
  • Stock instead of water makes it a light stew.

Awaze

Berbere, wine or water, and oil: hot, spiced, and thick.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1/2 cup

History

Awaze is an Ethiopian dipping and marinade paste made by stirring berbere with tej, wine, or water and sometimes oil. Berbere itself is the chile-and-spice blend. Awaze is what happens when that blend becomes a sauce. It is served with kitfo in some meals and used to marinate meat.

Cultural background

The heat and the spice list live in the berbere. This page does not pretend a teaspoon of chili powder is berbere. Use a berbere you trust.

Why it tastes this way

Berbere already holds chile, garlic, and warm spices. Loosened with wine or water, it tastes like that blend in sauce form. A plain chili powder will not.

How it is made

Berbere is stirred with water or dry red wine and a little oil into a thick dip.

Authentic touch. Start from a berbere you trust. Stir it to a thick paste. This page does not rebuild berbere from a guess.

How it is used

Use it as a dip for bread or a marinade for meat.

Field notes

  • Awaze is berbere loosened into a sauce. Berbere alone is the dry or paste spice.
  • Mitmita is a different, often hotter Ethiopian chile blend.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons berbere
  • 2 tablespoons water or dry red wine
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir the berbere with the water or wine.
  2. Add the oil and salt.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. The paste should be thick enough to cling to bread.
  5. Use it the same day as a dip, or as a marinade you then cook.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wine version is sharper.
  • More oil makes it a looser spooning sauce.

Buffalo Sauce

Cayenne hot sauce and butter: sharp, hot, and rich.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Buffalo sauce is the coating for the chicken wings associated with the Anchor Bar in Buffalo, New York, which was serving them by 1964. The sauce is cayenne hot sauce and butter. It is not a sweet glaze. Blue cheese or a celery stick is the usual side, not the sauce itself.

Cultural background

The wings are fried naked and tossed in the sauce afterward so the crust stays briefly crisp and the vinegar stays bright. More butter makes it milder.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar-based cayenne sauce is thin and sharp. Butter mellows it and helps it cling. Sugar is not the point, which is why it does not taste like a barbecue glaze.

How it is made

Butter and cayenne hot sauce are melted together.

Authentic touch. Melt butter into hot sauce, do not boil it hard, and toss just-fried wings. Serve them at once.

How it is used

Toss fried wings in the sauce.

Field notes

  • The Anchor Bar’s 1964 wings are the usual starting point of the story.
  • The sauce is butter and hot sauce, tossed on after frying.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup cayenne hot sauce
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • Pinch of black pepper

Steps

  1. Melt the butter with the hot sauce over low heat.
  2. Stir in the garlic powder and pepper.
  3. Toss with hot fried wings.
  4. Serve immediately.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More butter makes a milder sauce.
  • A sweet buffalo sauce is a later twist.

Pairings and uses

  • Wings
  • Dipping
  • Meat
  • chicken wings
  • celery
  • blue cheese dressing

Chicago Giardiniera

Pickled vegetables in oil, with hot chile: sharp, crunchy, and hot.

20 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 12 · about 4 cups

History

Chicago giardiniera is the oil-packed hot relish on an Italian beef sandwich. Cauliflower, carrot, celery, and hot chiles sit in vinegar and then in oil, with oregano. Italian giardiniera is an older pickle of garden vegetables in vinegar. The Chicago sandwich version is the local one: hotter, and dressed with oil so it soaks the bread. Sport peppers are traditional. Serranos are a common stand-in when sport peppers are not in the jar.

Cultural background

The beef is salty and wet. The relish is the crunch and the heat. A mild vinegar pickle without oil will not taste like the sandwich.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar pickles the vegetables, so they taste sour and stay crisp. Oil afterwards coats them and carries the chile heat into the bread. Oregano is the herbal note. The chile, not black pepper, is the burn. A short boil of the brine, poured over raw vegetables, is why they stay firm instead of cooking into a soft stew.

How it is made

Vegetables are covered with a hot vinegar brine, cooled, and then dressed with olive oil and oregano.

Authentic touch. Pack the vegetables in the hot brine, cool them, then cover them with oil. Give them a day if you can. Serrano is a stand-in, not the same pod as a sport pepper.

How it is used

Spoon it on Italian beef, or on sausages. It is a relish, not a cooked sauce.

Field notes

  • The oil is what makes the Chicago sandwich relish different from a plain vinegar pickle.
  • It is Italian-American Chicago cooking, hotter than a mild garden giardiniera.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cauliflower florets
  • 1 carrot, sliced
  • 2 celery stalks, sliced
  • 4 serrano chiles, sliced
  • 2 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1 1/2 cups white vinegar
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/2 cup olive oil

Steps

  1. Pack the cauliflower, carrot, celery, chiles, and garlic in a clean jar.
  2. Boil the vinegar, water, and salt.
  3. Pour the hot brine over the vegetables and cool to room temperature.
  4. Stir in the oregano and olive oil.
  5. Refrigerate at least a few hours, preferably a day, before serving.

Refrigerate. The vegetables want at least a few hours in the brine, and a day is better. Use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A vinegar-only jar is closer to older Italian giardiniera and milder on the bread.
  • Sport peppers, if you can buy them, are the Chicago chile.

  • Meat
  • Italian beef
  • sausages
  • crusty bread

Chipotle in Adobo

Smoked jalapeño, vinegar, and tomato: smoky, sour, and hot.

10 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Chipotle is a smoked ripe jalapeño. Packed in adobo, it sits in a sauce of vinegar, tomato, garlic, and spices. Mexican adobo here means that chile sauce, not Filipino vinegar-soy adobo. Canned chipotles are the pantry form of this preserve.

Cultural background

A spoon of the sauce seasons mayo, beans, and braises. The smoke is from the chile, so liquid smoke is unnecessary if the chipotles are real.

Why it tastes this way

Smoke-drying the jalapeño is why chipotle tastes smoky rather than green and fresh. Vinegar and tomato make a tart sauce around the fruit. The seeds and veins carry extra heat.

How it is made

Smoked chiles simmer in vinegar, tomato, garlic, and spices until soft.

Authentic touch. Simmer dried chipotles or use smoked jalapeños until they are soft in the vinegar-tomato sauce. Taste before adding salt.

How it is used

Use the chiles and the sauce in marinades, beans, or a spoon on tacos.

Field notes

  • Chipotle means a smoked chile, usually jalapeño.
  • Mexican adobo and Filipino adobo are not the same word in the kitchen.

Ingredients

  • 8 dried chipotle chiles or canned smoked jalapeños
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/4 cup vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil

Steps

  1. Simmer the chiles in water 15 minutes if they are dry.
  2. Blend half of them with vinegar, tomato, garlic, oregano, salt, and oil.
  3. Return the puree and whole chiles to the pot and simmer 10 minutes.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Filipino adobo is a different sauce.
  • Morita chiles are a fruitier smoked jalapeño.

  • mayonnaise for a chipotle crema

Cincinnati Chili Sauce

Fine beef, cinnamon, cocoa, and chile: savory, sweet-spiced, and thin.

15 min prep · 1 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 cups

History

Cincinnati chili is a sauce of finely ground beef and warm spices served over spaghetti. The usual account traces the style to Macedonian and Greek immigrant chili parlors, including Empress Chili, opened in 1922 by Tom and John Kiradjieff. It is not a German sauce, and it is not a Texas bowl of chili. Beans, if they appear, are a topping on a four-way or five-way, not the body of the sauce. Cheese and chopped onion are toppings too.

Cultural background

A two-way is spaghetti and chili. A three-way adds cheese. The sauce is thin because it has to coat noodles. Cinnamon, allspice or clove, and a little cocoa are the spice cabinet of those parlors.

Why it tastes this way

The beef is simmered until it breaks into a fine sauce, so it tastes savory without chunks. Chile is mild. Cinnamon and cocoa taste sweet and bitter, which is why people who expect only cumin think this sauce is spiced like a stew from the eastern Mediterranean. Tomato and a long simmer make it loose enough for spaghetti. Worcestershire, where it is used, adds anchovy salt.

How it is made

Ground beef simmers in water or stock with tomato, chile, cinnamon, cocoa, and other spices until it is a thin sauce.

Louisiana-Style Hot Sauce

Cayenne and vinegar: thin, sharp, and hot.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 20 · about 1 cup

History

Louisiana hot sauce is aged or simmered cayenne in vinegar and salt. The McIlhenny family on Avery Island began selling Tabasco sauce, made from tabasco peppers, in 1868, and that bottle defined the style for many eaters. This recipe is a weeknight cayenne-vinegar sauce in the Louisiana style. It is not the aged barrel formula of any brand.

Cultural background

It is on the table for oysters, greens, eggs, and beans. Thinness is correct. A thick chile paste is a different family.

Why it tastes this way

Cayenne and vinegar, simmered and strained, taste thin, sharp, and hot. Thickness is not the goal. The vinegar is why it seasons oysters and greens without covering them.

How it is made

Cayenne, garlic, vinegar, and salt simmer, then the sauce is blended and strained.

Authentic touch. Strain it if you want it to pour. Leave it a day in the refrigerator. The flavor settles.

How it is used

Shake it on oysters, greens, eggs, or rice and beans.

Field notes

  • The style is thin and vinegar-forward. Thickness is not a flaw to correct.
  • A brand name on a bottle is not the name of the whole style.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup fresh red cayenne peppers, stemmed
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 3/4 cup distilled white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Simmer the peppers, garlic, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend until smooth.
  3. Strain if you want it pourable from a bottle.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate. The flavor settles after a day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fermented cayenne mash is the commercial path and takes months.
  • Crystal-style sauces lean on cayenne. Tabasco-style sauces use tabasco peppers. Both are Louisiana vinegar sauces.

Mapo Sauce

Doubanjiang, fermented black bean, and Sichuan pepper: hot, numbing, and savory.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Mapo tofu is a Chengdu dish whose sauce is doubanjiang, fermented chile-bean paste, with ground meat, fermented black beans, and Sichuan pepper. The name is tied to a pockmarked woman’s tofu shop in Qing-era Chengdu in local food lore. The sauce is the spicy gravy the tofu braises in, eaten with rice.

Cultural background

Doubanjiang does the heavy lifting. Chile oil alone will not taste like mapo. The Sichuan pepper is the numbing layer on top of the heat.

Why it tastes this way

Doubanjiang, fermented chile-bean paste, tastes savory and hot. Sichuan pepper numbs. Fermented black beans add depth. Chile oil alone will not taste like mapo.

How it is made

Ground meat fries with doubanjiang and black beans, stock and tofu simmer, and a starch slurry thickens the sauce.

Authentic touch. Fry the doubanjiang until the oil turns red, then simmer the tofu gently so it does not break apart.

How it is used

Serve it over rice with the tofu.

Field notes

  • Pixian doubanjiang is the paste Sichuan cooks mean for this sauce.
  • Mala, hot and numbing, needs both chile and Sichuan pepper.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 4 ounces ground pork
  • 2 tablespoons doubanjiang
  • 1 teaspoon fermented black beans, rinsed
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 cup stock
  • 14 ounces soft tofu, cubed
  • 1 teaspoon cornstarch mixed with 1 tablespoon water
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground Sichuan pepper

Steps

  1. Fry the pork in the oil.
  2. Add the doubanjiang, black beans, and garlic and fry until the oil turns red.
  3. Add the stock and tofu and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. Stir in the cornstarch slurry.
  5. Finish with Sichuan pepper and serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A meat-free version uses more doubanjiang and mushroom stock and is less classic.
  • Chile oil on top is a finish, not the whole sauce.

Ripe red chile, garlic, vinegar, and sugar taste hot and tangy. Thai styles are often looser and seedier than the smooth California bottle. Garlic is part of the flavor, not an extra.

How it is made

Ripe red chiles and garlic simmer with vinegar and sugar, then the sauce is blended and simmered again.

Authentic touch. Simmer, blend, and simmer again. Leave it a day. This is a home sauce in the Thai style, not a brand copy.

How it is used

Spoon it on noodles, seafood, or rice.

Field notes

  • The name is a town in Thailand, Si Racha.
  • Thai sriracha and the famous California bottle are related and not the same sauce.

Ingredients

  • 8 ounces ripe red chiles, stemmed
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 3 tablespoons vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Simmer the chiles, garlic, sugar, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend to a coarse sauce.
  3. Simmer 5 minutes more.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The smooth American bottled style is a related sauce with a different texture.
  • A seed-in texture is closer to many Thai bottles.

Thai Green Curry

Green chile, cilantro root or stem, and coconut milk: hot, herbal, and rich.

25 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Kaeng khiao wan, Thai green curry, is a central Thai coconut curry. The paste is fresh green chile, shallot, garlic, galangal, lemongrass, cilantro root or stems, shrimp paste, and spices. The color comes from the fresh chile and herbs, not from food dye. It is hotter than many restaurant versions outside Thailand.

Cultural background

Chicken or beef simmers in the curry and is eaten with rice. Thai basil goes in at the end. The paste is fried in coconut cream before the rest of the liquid is added.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh green chile and herbs taste bright and hot. Coconut milk is rich. Shrimp paste, if you use it, adds a savory depth. Frying the paste in coconut cream before the rest of the liquid cooks off the raw spice taste.

How it is made

A fresh green paste is fried in coconut cream, then coconut milk and meat simmer, and basil finishes the pot.

Authentic touch. Fry the paste until it smells fragrant and the oil separates. Then add the rest of the coconut milk.

How it is used

Serve it with rice.

Field notes

  • The green is the chile and the herbs. It is not a spinach curry.
  • Shrimp paste is traditional in the paste, which is why this page is not vegetarian.

Ingredients

  • 6 green bird’s eye chiles
  • 2 shallots
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon chopped lemongrass
  • 1 teaspoon chopped galangal
  • 1/4 cup cilantro stems
  • 1/2 teaspoon shrimp paste
  • 1 can (14 ounces) coconut milk
  • 1 tablespoon fish sauce
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 pound chicken thighs
  • 1/2 cup Thai basil

Steps

  1. Pound or blend the chiles, shallot, garlic, lemongrass, galangal, cilantro, and shrimp paste.
  2. Fry the paste in a spoon of the thick coconut cream until fragrant.
  3. Add the chicken and the rest of the coconut milk, fish sauce, and sugar.
  4. Simmer until the chicken is cooked, about 20 minutes.
  5. Stir in the basil and serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red curry uses dried red chiles and is a different paste.
  • A milder paste uses fewer bird’s eye chiles.

History

Kaeng phet, Thai red curry, is built on a paste of dried red chiles, shallot, garlic, galangal, lemongrass, and shrimp paste. It is a central Thai curry, often with chicken, beef, or duck and with bamboo or Thai eggplant. The dried chile makes it redder and deeper than green curry.

Cultural background

It is weeknight and restaurant food, eaten with rice. The paste must be fried before the coconut milk is fully added, or the sauce tastes raw.

Why it tastes this way

Dried red chiles taste deeper and slightly smokier than fresh green ones. Coconut milk softens the heat. The paste still has to be fried or the sauce tastes raw.

How it is made

Soaked dried chiles are pounded with aromatics, fried in coconut cream, and simmered with meat and the rest of the coconut milk.

Authentic touch. Soak the dried chiles, pound the paste, and fry it in coconut cream before you add the meat.

How it is used

Serve it with rice.

Field notes

  • Red curry’s color comes from dried chiles, not from tomato.
  • Frying the paste is the step that wakes the spices.

Ingredients

Hot peppers, garlic, and vinegar simmer and are blended with salt.

Vindaloo

Vinegar, garlic, and hot chile: sharp, hot, and savory.

20 min prep · 1 hr 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Goan vindaloo comes from the Portuguese carne de vinha d’alhos, meat in wine and garlic, adapted in Goa with vinegar, dried chiles, and local spices. The British curry-house vindaloo that is only a very hot tomato gravy is a different dish. This is the Goan vinegar and chile stew sauce.

Cultural background

Pork is the classic meat because of Goa’s Catholic cooking. The vinegar is the point. It should taste sour as well as hot.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar, garlic, and dried chile taste sour and hot because Goan cooks adapted a Portuguese wine-and-garlic marinade to vinegar and local chiles. A tomato gravy with extra chili is a different, British restaurant dish.

How it is made

A paste of chile, garlic, ginger, spices, and vinegar marinates pork, then the meat simmers in that sauce.

Authentic touch. Use enough vinegar that the sauce still tastes sour when the meat is tender. The sour is the point.

How it is used

Serve the pork and sauce with rice.

Field notes

  • Vindaloo is a Goan reading of a Portuguese wine-and-garlic marinade.
  • Vinegar is as important as the chile.

Ingredients

  • 6 dried red chiles, soaked
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon ginger
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 1 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/4 cup vinegar
  • 1 pound pork shoulder, cubed
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Blend the chiles, garlic, ginger, cumin, turmeric, and vinegar into a paste.
  2. Coat the pork and rest 30 minutes.
  3. Fry the onion in the oil, add the pork and paste, and brown.
  4. Add the salt and water and simmer until the pork is tender, about 50 minutes.
  5. The sauce should be thick and sour.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Potato in the pot is common in some Goan homes.
  • The British restaurant version that is only extra chili in a tomato gravy is not this sauce.

Xnipec

Habenero, onion, and sour orange: very hot, sharp, and fresh.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Xnipec is the Yucatecan raw salsa of habanero, onion, and sour orange or lime. The name is often translated as dog’s nose, a joke about the heat making a nose run. It is a table salsa for grilled meat and tacos, not a cooked habanero hot sauce.

Cultural background

Yucatecan meals put this beside cochinita and grilled fish. The onion is soaked so it is sharp but not harsh. The chile is used with respect. A little seasons a lot.

Why it tastes this way

Raw habanero is fruity and extremely hot. Sour orange is sharper than lemon. Soaking the onion pulls out some of its raw sulfur so the chile and citrus can lead.

How it is made

Onion, tomato, habanero, and sour orange are chopped and salted. Nothing is cooked.

Authentic touch. Rinse the diced onion in cold water. Mince the habanero and add it to taste. Use sour orange or lime. Serve it the same day.

How it is used

Spoon a little on tacos or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • The heat is raw habanero. Start with less than you think.
  • Sour orange is the Yucatecan acid.

Ingredients

  • 1 white onion, finely diced and rinsed
  • 2 tomatoes, diced
  • 1 habanero, minced
  • 1/4 cup sour orange or lime juice
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Rinse the onion and drain it.
  2. Toss with tomato, a little habanero, cilantro, juice, and salt.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. Add more habanero only if you want more heat.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More tomato makes it juicier.
  • A cooked habanero salsa is a different, rounder sauce.

XO Sauce

Dried scallop, shrimp, ham, and chile in oil: savory, smoky, and rich.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

XO sauce is a Hong Kong condiment that emerged in hotel kitchens in the 1980s. The name borrows the prestige of XO cognac and the sauce contains no cognac. It is dried seafood, often scallop and shrimp, with chile, garlic, and sometimes cured ham, fried in oil. Specific restaurant credits vary, so this page keeps the place and the decade.

Cultural background

A spoonful finishes noodles, vegetables, and fried rice. It is a luxury pantry sauce because of the dried seafood, not because of alcohol.

Why it tastes this way

Dried scallop and shrimp fried in chile oil taste savory and rich. The name refers to a luxury grade of cognac and the sauce contains none. The dried seafood is the flavor.

How it is made

Soaked dried seafood and chile are fried slowly in oil with garlic until the bits are crisp and fragrant.

Authentic touch. Soak and chop the seafood, then fry it slowly until fragrant. Do not expect cognac in the taste.

How it is used

Spoon it onto noodles or stir-fried vegetables.

Field notes

  • XO sauce does not contain cognac. The name is a luxury reference.
  • It became a Hong Kong sauce in the 1980s.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup dried scallops, soaked and finely chopped
  • 1/4 cup dried shrimp, soaked and chopped
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 shallots, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chile flakes
  • 1/2 cup neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce

Steps

  1. Drain and chop the soaked seafood.
  2. Fry the shallot and garlic in the oil until soft.
  3. Add the seafood and chile and fry on low until fragrant and slightly crisp, about 10 minutes.
  4. Stir in the soy.
  5. Cool and refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Zhug, also spelled skhug, is the Yemeni cilantro and chile paste. Green zhug is herbs and green chile. Red zhug uses hotter red chile. Cardamom, cumin, and garlic are typical. Yemeni Jews brought it into wider Israeli cooking in the twentieth century. It is a condiment, not a cooked salsa.

Cultural background

It is spooned onto falafel, grilled meat, and breakfast eggs. A little is enough. The cardamom is the spice that keeps it from being a generic green hot sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro and hot chile taste bright. Cardamom and cumin are why it does not taste like a generic green hot sauce. It is uncooked, so the herbs stay raw.

How it is made

Cilantro, chile, garlic, and spices are pounded or blended with olive oil and salt.

Authentic touch. Pound or blend it the day you eat it, and use cardamom, not only chile.

How it is used

Serve a spoonful with grilled meat, eggs, or falafel.

Field notes

  • Cardamom is a usual spice in zhug, which is a quick way to tell it from many other green hot sauces.
