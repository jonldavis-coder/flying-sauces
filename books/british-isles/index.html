Mint Sauce

Fresh mint, vinegar, and a little sugar: sharp, cool, and sweet.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1/2 cup

History

English mint sauce is chopped mint with vinegar and sugar, served with roast lamb. It is a table sauce, not a jelly, though mint jelly later became a substitute. The pairing of lamb and mint in England is documented in cookbooks by the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Cultural background

It is Sunday roast food. The mint should be fresh spearmint. A vinegar-heavy sauce cuts the fat of the lamb. Mint jelly is sweeter and cooked.

Why it tastes this way

Chopped mint in vinegar, with a little sugar, tastes cool and sharp against fatty lamb. It is uncooked. Mint jelly is sweeter and cooked, and it is a different condiment.

How it is made

Mint is chopped with sugar and covered with hot vinegar so the leaves soften.

Authentic touch. Use spearmint, chop it fine, and cover it with hot vinegar. It should taste more sharp than sweet.

How it is used

Serve it cold or cool with roast lamb.

Field notes

  • Mint sauce is sharp. Mint jelly is a different condiment.
  • Spearmint, not peppermint, is the usual herb.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup finely chopped mint leaves
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/4 cup boiling water
  • 1/4 cup white wine vinegar
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir the mint and sugar with the boiling water.
  2. Add the vinegar and salt.
  3. Rest at least 20 minutes.
  4. Taste. It should be more sharp than sweet.
  5. Serve with lamb.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mint jelly is the cooked, sweeter cousin.
  • A spoon of boiling water before the vinegar softens the leaves.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • roast lamb
  • new potatoes
  • peas
  • Vegetarian

Bread Sauce

Milk, bread crumbs, onion, and clove: mild, peppery, and thick.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

English bread sauce is medieval in idea: bread thickened a sauce long before flour roux was standard. The version served with roast chicken, turkey, and game is milk infused with onion and clove, thickened with fresh crumbs, and finished with butter. It is a Christmas and Sunday roast sauce.

Cultural background

It sits beside cranberry sauce and gravy on a British Christmas plate, and beside roast chicken the rest of the year. The texture should be soft, not gluey.

Why it tastes this way

Milk infused with onion and clove, thickened with bread, tastes mild and spiced. Bread was a thickener before roux was common. The clove must perfume the milk and then come out, or the sauce tastes bitter.

How it is made

Milk is infused with onion and clove, the aromatics are removed, and bread crumbs thicken the milk. Butter finishes it.

Authentic touch. Steep the clove-studded onion and remove it. Stir in fresh crumbs and stop when the sauce is soft, not gluey.

How it is used

Serve warm with roast poultry or game.

Field notes

  • Bread sauce is older in spirit than roux-thickened gravies.
  • The clove should perfume the milk and then come out, or the sauce tastes bitter.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups whole milk
  • 1 small onion, peeled and studded with 3 cloves
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 1 cup fresh white bread crumbs
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • Salt
  • White pepper

Steps

  1. Heat the milk with the onion, cloves, and bay until steaming. Steep 10 minutes off the heat.
  2. Remove the onion and bay.
  3. Stir in the crumbs and simmer until thick, about 5 minutes.
  4. Stir in the butter, salt, and pepper.
  5. Serve warm.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A bay leaf can join the clove.
  • Cream at the end makes it richer.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • roast chicken
  • roast turkey
  • game birds
  • Vegetarian

Cumberland Sauce

Red currant, port, orange, and mustard: sweet, sharp, and spiced.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Cumberland sauce is an English cold sauce for cold meats and game, made from red currant jelly, port, orange and lemon zest, and mustard. It is named for Cumberland, and nineteenth-century cookbooks give versions of it. It is a cousin of the sweet-sour fruit sauces of northern Europe.

Cultural background

It is Boxing Day and buffet food, with ham, cold game, and pâté. It is served cold, which sets it apart from a hot roast gravy.

Why it tastes this way

Red currant, port, orange zest, and mustard taste sweet, sour, and spiced. It is served cold with cold meat, so the flavors are set, not simmered on the plate. Blanching the zest removes bitterness.

How it is made

Zest is blanched, then simmered with port and jelly, and mustard is stirred in off the heat.

Authentic touch. Blanch the zest, then chill the sauce fully. It is not a hot gravy.

How it is used

Chill it and serve it with cold ham, venison, or pork pie.

Field notes

  • Cumberland sauce is served cold, with cold meat.
  • The zest is blanched so the sauce is not bitterly pithy.

Ingredients

  • Zest of 1 orange and 1 lemon, cut in fine strips
  • 1/2 cup port
  • 1/2 cup red currant jelly
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard
  • 1 tablespoon orange juice
  • Pinch of cayenne

Steps

  1. Boil the zest in water 2 minutes and drain it.
  2. Simmer the zest with the port and jelly until the jelly melts.
  3. Stir in the mustard, orange juice, and cayenne off the heat.
  4. Cool completely.
  5. Serve cold with ham or game.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red currant jelly is traditional. Cranberry jelly is a different sauce.
  • A spoon of ginger is used in some household recipes.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • cold ham
  • game
  • pâté

Horseradish Sauce

Fresh horseradish and cream: hot, sharp, and cool.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Creamed horseradish is the English sauce for roast beef and smoked fish. Horseradish has been used as a sharp accompaniment in northern Europe for centuries. The English Sunday-roast version folds grated root into whipped or thick cream with vinegar and a little sugar. The heat is the root, not chile, so this page marks it mild on the chili scale.

Cultural background

It is Sunday lunch food beside roast beef, and breakfast food beside smoked trout and mackerel. Prepared horseradish in a jar is the practical grate.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh horseradish is hot in a different way from chile. Cream carries that heat and softens it for roast beef. The burn fades as the root sits, so freshly grated root tastes louder.

How it is made

Grated horseradish is folded into cream with vinegar, salt, and a pinch of sugar.

Authentic touch. Grate it just before you fold it into cream. Jarred horseradish is milder. Add vinegar so the heat does not disappear into the dairy.

How it is used

Serve it cold with roast beef or smoked fish.

Field notes

  • The burn is in the fresh root and fades as it sits.
  • Chili heat and horseradish heat are different. This guide’s heat scale is for chiles.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup heavy cream
  • 3 tablespoons finely grated horseradish
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar
  • 1/4 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Whip the cream to soft peaks, or use thick cream and skip the whip.
  2. Fold in the horseradish, vinegar, sugar, and salt.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. Taste. Add more vinegar if it is dull.
  5. Serve cold.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Apfelkren, the Austrian apple-horseradish sauce, is a different recipe.
  • A yogurt base is sharper and less classic.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • Seafood
  • roast beef
  • smoked mackerel
  • boiled beets
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Worcestershire Sauce

Anchovy, vinegar, molasses, and tamarind: salty, sour, and deep.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 24 · about 1 cup

History

Worcestershire sauce was first sold by the chemists Lea and Perrins in Worcester in the 1830s. It is a fermented anchovy and vegetable sauce with vinegar, molasses, tamarind, and spices. The exact commercial formula is a trade secret. Tales about a forgotten barrel are company lore and are not repeated here as fact. This recipe is a simmered kitchen version of the flavor, not the aged original.

Cultural background

British cooking uses it in Welsh rarebit and with grilled meats. It also became a standard seasoning in cocktails and in kitchens far from Worcester. It is not a vegan sauce, because of the anchovy.

Why it tastes this way

Anchovy, vinegar, molasses, and tamarind taste salty, sour, and deep. The commercial sauce is fermented. This simmer captures the same family of flavors without the long age, so it will not taste identical to the bottle.

How it is made

Anchovy, onion, vinegar, molasses, tamarind, and spices simmer, then the sauce is strained. The result is a pantry approximation.

Authentic touch. Use it by the teaspoon. Reduce the simmered sauce until it is sharp. A weak mix tastes like sweet vinegar.

How it is used

Splash it into meat sauces, rarebit, or a marinade. A teaspoon is usually enough.

Field notes

  • Lea and Perrins of Worcester began selling the sauce in the 1830s.
  • The simmered home version is not the fermented factory sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup white vinegar
  • 1/4 cup molasses
  • 2 tablespoons tamarind paste
  • 4 anchovy fillets
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 garlic clove
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground clove
  • 1/4 teaspoon chili powder
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients 25 minutes.
  2. Blend and strain.
  3. Cool.
  4. Taste. It should be sharply savory, not sweet.
  5. Refrigerate. This is a kitchen version, not aged Worcestershire.

Refrigerate the strained sauce and use it within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The commercial sauce is fermented and aged. This simmered version is only a kitchen echo.
  • Soy sauce and anchovy together can stand in for a splash, and that is a shortcut, not this recipe.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Marinades
  • grilled steak
  • Welsh rarebit
  • Bloody Mary
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Apple Sauce

Cooked apple, lightly sharp: soft, fruity, and mild.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

British apple sauce for roast pork is cooked tart apples, lightly sweetened or not at all. It is a standard part of a pork roast in England. Bramley apples are the usual cooking apple there. Any tart apple works. This is a savory sauce, not a sweet applesauce meant only for breakfast.

Cultural background

The acidity cuts the fat of pork. Some cooks add a clove or a strip of lemon. Sugar is optional and should not turn it into a dessert.

Why it tastes this way

Tart cooking apples, simmered with little or no sugar, taste sharp against fatty pork. A sweet cinnamon applesauce is a dessert and fights the meat less usefully.

How it is made

Tart apples are simmered with a splash of water until they collapse, then beaten smooth.

Authentic touch. Use a tart apple and add sugar only if the fruit is harsh. Keep it slightly sharp.

How it is used

Serve it warm or cold with roast pork.

Field notes

  • Bramley is the classic British cooking apple for this sauce.
  • It is supposed to be tart. A very sweet sauce fights the pork less well.

Ingredients

  • 4 tart apples, peeled and chopped
  • 2 tablespoons water
  • 1 teaspoon sugar, optional
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the apples and water, covered, until soft.
  2. Mash or blend them.
  3. Add sugar only if the apples are harsh, and add the salt.
  4. Serve warm or cold.
  5. Refrigerate leftovers.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A sage leaf honors the pork herb.
  • Cinnamon sugar makes it a dessert sauce instead.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • roast pork
  • pork chops
  • sausages
  • Vegetarian

Onion Gravy

Slow onion and brown stock: sweet, dark, and savory.

10 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Onion gravy is the British sauce of slowly cooked onions, flour, and stock, served with sausages, toad-in-the-hole, and mash. It is weeknight food and pub food. The onions have to cook long enough to sweeten. A pale, rushed onion gravy tastes raw.

Cultural background

Bangers and mash are the plate most people mean. The same gravy belongs with liver and with a pie. Worcestershire is a common splash and adds anchovy if you use it. This version stays with stock.

Why it tastes this way

Onions cooked until deep gold taste sweet. Flour and stock turn that sweetness into a brown gravy for sausages. Rushed, pale onions taste raw and the gravy tastes floury.

How it is made

Onions cook slowly in fat until brown, flour is stirred in, and stock simmers the gravy thick.

Authentic touch. Give the onions the time to brown. That browning is the flavor. Deglaze with stock only after they are golden.

How it is used

Pour it over sausages and mashed potatoes.

Field notes

  • The onions need time. Browning them is the flavor.
  • It is a sausage gravy in the British sense, not the American milk-and-sausage gravy.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter or oil
  • 3 onions, thinly sliced
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 2 1/2 cups beef stock
  • 1 teaspoon vinegar
  • Salt
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Cook the onions in the fat over medium-low heat until deep golden, about 25 minutes.
  2. Stir in the flour for 1 minute.
  3. Add the stock and simmer 10 minutes.
  4. Add the vinegar, salt, and pepper.
  5. Serve with sausages.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of mustard sharpens it.
  • Beer can replace part of the stock.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • sausages
  • mashed potatoes
  • toad-in-the-hole

Piccalilli

Mustard, turmeric, vinegar, and pickled vegetables: sharp, yellow, and crunchy.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 12 · about 4 cups

History

British piccalilli is a mustard pickle of cauliflower, onion, and other vegetables in a turmeric-yellow vinegar sauce. English cookbooks have versions from the eighteenth century onward, adapted from Indian pickles into a British accompaniment for cold meat and cheese. It is not a single Indian achar. This is a refrigerator pickle, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

Cultural background

It is ploughman’s lunch food, with cheddar and ham. The sauce should be thick enough to cling to the vegetables and sharp enough to notice.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard and turmeric in vinegar taste sharp and yellow. The vegetables stay slightly crisp because they are salted first and cooked briefly. It is a British reading of Indian pickles, built for cold meat and cheese.

How it is made

Salted vegetables are simmered briefly in a vinegar sauce thickened with mustard and a little flour.

Authentic touch. Salt and rinse the vegetables so they stay crisp and not watery. Cook the mustard sauce until the flour taste is gone, then add the vegetables only briefly.

How it is used

Eat it cold with ham, pork pie, or cheese.

Field notes

  • The yellow is turmeric, the same way American mustard is yellow.
  • British piccalilli is a local reading of Indian pickles, not a copy of one achar.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 small cauliflower, in florets
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 cup cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 tablespoon mustard powder
  • 1 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Toss the vegetables with the salt, rest 30 minutes, rinse, and drain.
  2. Simmer the vinegar and sugar.
  3. Whisk the mustard, turmeric, flour, and water, and stir that into the vinegar.
  4. Simmer until thick.
  5. Stir in the vegetables, cook 2 minutes, cool, and refrigerate.

Refrigerate and eat within two weeks. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green beans or cucumber can join the cauliflower.
  • A hotter English mustard powder makes it fiercer.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Meat
  • cheddar
  • ham
  • pork pie
  • Vegetarian

Marie Rose Sauce

Mayonnaise, tomato, and a little Worcestershire: creamy, sweet, and savory.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Marie Rose sauce is the British prawn-cocktail sauce: mayonnaise, tomato ketchup, lemon, and often a dash of Worcestershire. Prawn cocktail became a British restaurant standard in the 1960s and 1970s. Stories about who named the sauce disagree, so this page does not pick an inventor. It is close to American cocktail sauce only in purpose. The British one is mayonnaise-based.

Cultural background

It is starter food, on lettuce with cold prawns, and a sandwich spread. The color is pink from the ketchup.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise and ketchup taste creamy and slightly sweet. A dash of Worcestershire adds savor from anchovy. It is the British prawn-cocktail sauce, not the American ketchup-and-horseradish cocktail sauce.

How it is made

Mayonnaise and ketchup are stirred with lemon and a small dash of Worcestershire.

Authentic touch. Keep the ketchup to a spoon or two. Too much and it tastes like ketchup. Chill it.

How it is used

Spoon it over cold prawns or use it in a prawn sandwich.

Field notes

  • British prawn cocktail sauce is a mayonnaise sauce. American cocktail sauce is ketchup and horseradish.
  • The naming stories conflict, so none is treated as fact here.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup mayonnaise
  • 2 tablespoons ketchup
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon Worcestershire sauce
  • Pinch of black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients together.
  2. Taste for lemon.
  3. Chill.
  4. Spoon over cold prawns.
  5. Use within a few days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few drops of brandy show up in restaurant versions.
  • American cocktail sauce skips the mayonnaise and uses horseradish.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • cold prawns
  • lettuce
  • brown bread
  • Nut-free

Chip Shop Curry Sauce

Mild curry, onion, and a light thickener: savory, slightly sweet, and smooth.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 2 cups

History

British chip-shop curry sauce is a mild, thick spiced gravy sold on chips, especially in the north of England and in Scotland. It grew up beside fish and chips in the twentieth century, separate from Indian restaurant curries and from Chinese takeaway sauces. It is a chip-shop sauce, not a curry paste.

Cultural background

It is ladled over chips or into a pie. The spice level is gentle on purpose. Onion and curry powder are the flavor, not a long list of whole spices.

Why it tastes this way

Mild curry powder, onion, and a light thickener taste savory and slightly sweet, not hot. It was made to go on fried potatoes, so it is a gravy, not a long meat curry.

How it is made

Onion softens, curry powder is fried briefly, and stock and a little flour or cornflour make a smooth gravy.

Authentic touch. Fry the curry powder in the onion fat for a minute so it does not taste of raw powder. Keep it pourable.

How it is used

Pour it over chips, or serve it with a pie.

Field notes

  • Chip-shop curry sauce is its own British category, milder than most restaurant curries.
  • It is a gravy for fried potatoes, not a simmered meat curry.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 1 tablespoon curry powder
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 2 cups vegetable stock
  • 1 teaspoon tomato paste
  • 1/2 teaspoon sugar
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the oil until soft.
  2. Stir in the curry powder and flour for 1 minute.
  3. Add the stock and tomato paste and simmer 10 minutes.
  4. Stir in the sugar and salt.
  5. Blend if you want it as smooth as a chip shop’s.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A teaspoon of mango chutney makes it sweeter, which some shops do.
  • Raising the cayenne makes it less like a chip-shop sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • chips
  • meat pie
  • fried fish
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Coronation Chicken Dressing

Mayonnaise, curry powder, and apricot or mango: creamy, spiced, and sweet.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Coronation chicken was created for the 1953 coronation of Elizabeth II by Constance Spry and Rosemary Hume. The original sauce for the cold chicken included mayonnaise, curry, and a reduced apricot and tomato mixture. Later British versions simplified it into a curry-mayonnaise dressing with mango chutney. This is that simpler dressing, named for the occasion.

Cultural background

It became a sandwich-shop and buffet standard in Britain. It is a cold sauce for cold chicken, not a hot curry.

Why it tastes this way

Cold mayonnaise, curry powder, and chutney taste creamy, spiced, and sweet because the 1953 chicken dish was a cold buffet sauce, not a hot curry. The spice is bloomed so the powder does not taste raw.

How it is made

Mayonnaise is stirred with curry powder, chutney, and lemon.

Authentic touch. Stir the curry powder with a spoon of water before it meets the mayonnaise. Fold it through cold chicken, not hot.

How it is used

Fold it through cold chicken for sandwiches or a salad.

Field notes

  • The dish was made for the 1953 coronation banquet’s catering, not invented by a later supermarket.
  • Curry powder in a cold mayonnaise is the British reading of the idea.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 1 tablespoon mango chutney, chopped
  • 2 teaspoons curry powder
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon water
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Stir the curry powder with the water to make a paste.
  2. Mix it with the mayonnaise, chutney, and lemon.
  3. Salt to taste.
  4. Fold through cold cooked chicken.
  5. Serve cold.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The 1953 sauce used a cooked apricot reduction and was more elaborate.
  • Yogurt lightens the mayonnaise.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Salads
  • Meat
  • cold chicken
  • sandwiches
  • lettuce
  • Vegetarian

Lemon Curd

Lemon, egg, and butter: tart, rich, and thick.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Lemon curd is a British preserve and dessert sauce of lemon juice and zest, egg, sugar, and butter, cooked until thick. It has been a tea-table spread since the nineteenth century. The zest is the perfume. The juice is the tartness.

Cultural background

It is spread on bread, filled into tarts, and spooned on cakes. It should be tart. A low heat keeps the egg from scrambling.

Why it tastes this way

Lemon juice is sour and the zest holds the aromatic oils. Egg and butter thicken it into a custard. Boiling scrambles the egg and the curd tastes grainy.

How it is made

Lemon, sugar, egg, and butter thicken over gentle heat.

Authentic touch. Cook it over barely simmering water, stirring, and strain it. Use zest and juice. Stop when it coats a spoon.

How it is used

Spread it or spoon it on cake and fruit.

Field notes

  • Zest is the perfume. Juice alone tastes flatter.
  • Nineteenth-century British tables already treated it as a preserve.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon lemon zest
  • 3/4 cup sugar
  • 3 eggs
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Whisk the juice, zest, sugar, eggs, and salt in a heatproof bowl.
  2. Set it over simmering water and stir until thick.
  3. Take it off and stir in the butter.
  4. Strain.
  5. Cool and refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a week. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lime or passion fruit curd uses the same method.
  • A microwave curd is faster and easier to overcook.

Pairings and uses

  • Desserts
  • scones
  • sponge cake
  • berries
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Tikka Masala Sauce

Tomato, cream, and spices: mildly hot, sweet, and rich.

15 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Chicken tikka masala is a British restaurant dish: grilled spiced chicken in a tomato and cream sauce. Several UK origin stories circulate, including a Glasgow claim, and they do not agree. This page does not name an inventor. The sauce is a later, creamier cousin of North Indian makhani, built for the British curry house.

Cultural background

It is the defining British curry-house gravy, milder and often sweeter than restaurant curries in India. The chicken is cooked separately and then simmered in the sauce.

Why it tastes this way

A British restaurant sauce of tomato, spice, and cream tastes milder and often sweeter than many curries in India. The chicken is grilled first, so the sauce tastes of spice and tomato, not of boiled meat.

How it is made

Onion, tomato, and spices simmer, the sauce is blended, and cream finishes it.

Authentic touch. Grill or sear the chicken before it simmers in the sauce. Do not claim a single inventor. The stories disagree.

How it is used

Simmer grilled chicken tikka in the sauce and serve with rice or naan.

Field notes

  • The dish is British restaurant cooking. The origin stories disagree, so none is treated as fact.
  • The chicken is usually grilled first, then sauced.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 tablespoon garam masala
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 2 cups tomato puree
  • 1/2 cup cream
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion, garlic, and ginger in the oil.
  2. Add the spices and tomato and simmer 20 minutes.
  3. Blend smooth.
  4. Stir in the cream, salt, and sugar.
  5. Simmer grilled chicken in the sauce 10 minutes.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Makhani is the Delhi butter sauce and is close, not identical.
  • A spicier house version raises the chili.

Pairings and uses

  • Curries
  • Rice
  • Meat
  • Grilling
  • grilled chicken
  • rice
  • naan
  • Nut-free
