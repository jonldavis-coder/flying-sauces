Béchamel

Milk, butter, and a white roux: smooth, lightly nutty, and savory.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Béchamel is one of the five French mother sauces in the repertoire associated with Auguste Escoffier. The name is tied to Louis de Béchamel, a financier at the court of Louis XIV, though the sauce of flour-thickened milk is older than that dedication. By the nineteenth century it was a standard base for gratins and for daughter sauces such as Mornay.

Cultural background

In French cooking it is a pantry technique as much as a sauce: the white sauce that carries cheese, nutmeg, or onion. Italian besciamella and Greek béchamel in pastitsio are close relatives with their own seasoning.

Why it tastes this way

The mild, milky taste is the liquid, not a spice. A white roux only cooks long enough to lose raw flour, so the sauce stays pale and sweet from milk instead of tasting toasted or meaty like a brown sauce.

How it is made

A white roux of butter and flour is whisked with warm milk and simmered until it coats a spoon.

Authentic touch. Add the milk warm and whisk constantly. Cold milk and a hot roux lump, and those lumps taste of raw starch.

How it is used

Use it under a gratin, in lasagna, or as the base of a cheese sauce for cauliflower, croque monsieur, or creamed spinach.

Field notes

  • Escoffier’s codification made béchamel a mother sauce, not a single restaurant recipe.
  • The Italian besciamella in lasagna alla bolognese is the same technique with a lighter hand on the nutmeg.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 2 cups whole milk, warmed
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • Pinch of white pepper
  • Pinch of nutmeg

Steps

  1. Melt the butter in a saucepan over medium heat.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook 1 to 2 minutes without browning.
  3. Whisk in the warm milk a little at a time until smooth.
  4. Simmer, whisking, until the sauce coats a spoon, about 8 minutes.
  5. Season with salt, white pepper, and nutmeg.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mornay stirs in Gruyère and Parmesan.
  • Soubise blends in a sweated onion puree.
  • A pinch of nutmeg is traditional; a bay leaf in the milk is optional.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Vegetables
  • Eggs
  • lasagna
  • cauliflower gratin
  • croque monsieur
  • creamed spinach
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Velouté

Light stock and a blond roux: silky, savory, and pale.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Velouté is the stock-based white mother sauce of the French repertoire. Carême and later Escoffier distinguished it from béchamel by the liquid: chicken, veal, or fish stock instead of milk. Sauce suprême, a cream-finished chicken velouté, is the best-known daughter.

Cultural background

French sauce cookery treats velouté as a blank savory canvas. The stock decides the sauce: fish velouté for poached fish, chicken velouté for vegetables and poultry.

Why it tastes this way

It tastes of the stock you choose because stock, not milk, is the liquid. A blond roux keeps the flavor light. Chicken stock tastes of poultry. Fish stock tastes of the sea. They are not interchangeable.

How it is made

A blond roux is loosened with hot stock and simmered until nappé, then strained.

Authentic touch. Simmer and skim. The long, gentle cook is what removes the raw-flour taste without browning the sauce.

How it is used

Spoon it over poached chicken, steamed vegetables, or fish. Reduce it further with cream for sauce suprême.

Field notes

  • The name means velvety, which describes the texture once the starch has cooked out.
  • A fish velouté and a chicken velouté are the same method and should not be swapped casually.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 2 1/2 cups hot chicken stock
  • Salt
  • White pepper

Steps

  1. Melt the butter and stir in the flour. Cook the roux to a pale blond, about 2 minutes.
  2. Whisk in the hot stock until smooth.
  3. Simmer 15 minutes, skimming, until the sauce coats a spoon.
  4. Strain, then season with salt and white pepper.
  5. Keep it warm over the lowest heat, with a film of stock or butter on top if you are not using it at once.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sauce suprême finishes chicken velouté with cream and a little butter.
  • Sauce vin blanc adds a white-wine reduction and fish stock.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Seafood
  • Vegetables
  • poached chicken
  • steamed sole
  • asparagus
  • veal blanquette

  • Nut-free

Espagnole

Brown stock, browned roux, and tomato: deep, roasted, and savory.

20 min prep · 1 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Espagnole is the brown mother sauce. The name is French for Spanish, a label that stuck in nineteenth-century French kitchens and does not mean the sauce is from Spain. Escoffier built it from brown stock, a brown roux, mirepoix, and tomato, then used it as the path to demi-glace.

Cultural background

It belongs to the professional French sauce pot: a base kept warm and reduced, not usually a table sauce by itself. Home cooks meet it mostly through demi-glace and pan sauces that borrow its flavor.

Why it tastes this way

The dark, roasted taste comes from a brown roux and browned vegetables, then brown stock. Tomato is a seasoning here, not the main flavor, which is why it does not taste like marinara.

How it is made

A brown roux thickens brown stock, with a browned mirepoix and tomato, then the sauce is skimmed and strained.

Authentic touch. Let the roux reach a true brown and a toasted smell before the stock goes in. A pale roux will not give you this sauce.

How it is used

Use it as the base of demi-glace or a pan sauce for steak. It is too plain and thick to be the whole point of a plate.

Field notes

  • The Spanish name is a French kitchen label, not a Spanish recipe.
  • A properly cooked brown roux thickens less than a white roux and tastes more toasted.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter or neutral oil
  • 1 small onion, diced
  • 1 small carrot, diced
  • 1 celery stalk, diced
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 3 cups hot brown beef stock
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 1 bay leaf
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Brown the onion, carrot, and celery in the fat.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook until the roux is brown and smells toasted, 4 to 6 minutes.
  3. Whisk in the stock and tomato paste. Add the bay leaf.
  4. Simmer 45 minutes, skimming the surface.
  5. Strain, season, and use as a base or reduce further.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Demi-glace reduces espagnole with more brown stock.
  • Sauce Robert adds mustard and onion to a brown sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • pan sauces for steak
  • braised beef
  • demi-glace
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Sauce Tomate

Tomato cooked with stock, aromatics, and a little roux: round and savory rather than bright.

15 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

Sauce tomate is the tomato mother sauce of the French system, distinct from quick Italian marinara. Classic versions in the Escoffier lineage use salt pork or bacon, a roux, stock, and a long simmer. Tomato had already entered French cooking in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries before it was fixed as a mother sauce.

Cultural background

French tomato sauce is a cooked sauce for meat, eggs, and filled pasta. It is thicker and more savory than a raw salsa and less herbal than many Italian sughi.

Why it tastes this way

French sauce tomate tastes cooked and savory because the tomato simmers with pork fat, stock, and a little flour. The long cook turns sharp tomato into a round sauce. That is different from a quick Italian marinara.

How it is made

A small roux and rendered pork cook with tomato and stock until the sauce is thick and sweet-savory.

Authentic touch. Simmer until the raw tomato edge is gone. If it still tastes like the can, it needs more time, not more salt.

How it is used

Serve with grilled meats, eggs, stuffed vegetables, or as a base under a cheese gratin.

Field notes

  • In the French mother-sauce list, tomato sauce sits beside the roux sauces and hollandaise.
  • A long simmer is the point: the sauce should taste cooked, not like fresh tomato water.

Ingredients

  • 2 ounces salt pork or bacon, diced
  • 1 small onion, diced
  • 1 tablespoon all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1 can (28 ounces) whole tomatoes, crushed by hand
  • 1 cup beef or chicken stock
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Render the pork and cook the onion in the fat until soft.
  2. Stir in the flour and tomato paste and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes, stock, bay leaf, and sugar.
  4. Simmer 40 minutes until thick.
  5. Remove the bay, season with salt, and blend if you want it smooth.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the pork and use oil and vegetable stock for a different, meatless sauce.
  • Italian marinara is a separate, quicker sauce and is not this mother sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Meat
  • Vegetables
  • grilled pork
  • eggs
  • stuffed cabbage
  • gnocchi
  • Nut-free

Hollandaise

Butter emulsified with egg yolk and lemon: rich, tart, and warm.

10 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Hollandaise is the emulsion mother sauce: egg yolks holding butter, sharpened with lemon or a vinegar reduction. The name points at Holland and at butter, and the sauce was established in French cookbooks well before Escoffier listed it among the mothers. It is the parent of béarnaise and of sauce mousseline.

Cultural background

It is the classic sauce for eggs Benedict in American brunch cooking and for asparagus and poached fish in French cooking. It is made to order because it does not hold well.

Why it tastes this way

The flavor is butter, held by egg yolk and sharpened with lemon. Warmth keeps the emulsion glossy. Too much heat scrambles the yolk and the sauce tastes eggy and grainy instead of smooth.

How it is made

Yolks and a little water or reduction are warmed while melted butter is whisked in to form a stable emulsion.

Authentic touch. Whisk the butter in off a hard boil. If it tightens, a teaspoon of warm water brings it back. Do not try to save a scrambled yolk.

How it is used

Spoon it over poached eggs, asparagus, or poached fish as soon as it is made.

Field notes

  • Hollandaise is an emulsion, like mayonnaise, but the fat is warm butter rather than oil.
  • If it gets too hot, the yolk scrambles and the emulsion breaks.

Ingredients

  • 3 egg yolks
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon cold water
  • 1/2 cup unsalted butter, melted and warm
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Pinch of cayenne

Steps

  1. Whisk the yolks, lemon juice, and water in a heatproof bowl.
  2. Set the bowl over barely simmering water and whisk until the yolks thicken slightly.
  3. Take the bowl off the heat and whisk in the warm butter in a thin stream.
  4. Season with salt and cayenne.
  5. Serve at once. If it tightens, whisk in a teaspoon of warm water.

The yolks stay under a simmer. Use the sauce the day it is made and keep it warm, not hot. Pasteurized yolks are the safer choice if you need to hold it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Béarnaise uses a tarragon and shallot reduction.
  • Sauce mousseline folds in whipped cream.
  • Maltaise uses orange juice instead of lemon.

Pairings and uses

  • Eggs
  • Vegetables
  • Seafood
  • eggs Benedict
  • asparagus
  • poached salmon
  • artichokes
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Mayonnaise

Oil emulsified with egg yolk and acid: thick, neutral, and rich.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Mayonnaise is a cold emulsion of oil, egg yolk, and vinegar or lemon. French cookbooks of the early nineteenth century already treat it as a standard sauce. A story that ties the name to Mahón in Menorca after a French victory in 1756 is often repeated and not settled, so this page keeps the documented part: it is a French sauce technique with a disputed name origin.

Cultural background

From French tables it became a global sandwich and salad sauce. Belgian frites are often eaten with mayonnaise. Japanese Kewpie mayonnaise is a later, seasoned style with a different recipe.

Why it tastes this way

The neutral richness is oil suspended in yolk and acid. Mustard helps the emulsion and adds a small bite. The oil you choose is the flavor: a strong olive oil tastes loud, a neutral oil tastes clean.

How it is made

Oil is whisked drop by drop into yolk, mustard, and acid until a thick emulsion forms, then the rest of the oil can go in faster.

Authentic touch. Start the oil drop by drop. Once the sauce thickens, you can stream it. Dumping the oil in at the start is how mayonnaise breaks.

How it is used

Spread it on sandwiches, serve it with cold seafood or fries, or use it as the base of rémoulade and aïoli-style garlic mayonnaise.

Field notes

  • Mustard helps the emulsion and is why many home recipes include a spoon of Dijon.
  • The Menorca naming story is popular and not proven, so it is not treated here as fact.

Ingredients

  • 1 egg yolk
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice or white wine vinegar
  • 1 cup neutral oil or light olive oil
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Whisk the yolk, mustard, lemon juice, and salt until smooth.
  2. Whisk in the oil a few drops at a time until the mixture thickens.
  3. Add the remaining oil in a thin stream, whisking steadily.
  4. Adjust the salt and acid. Whisk in a teaspoon of water if it is stiffer than you want.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a few days.

This uses raw yolk. Refrigerate it and use it within a few days, or use a pasteurized yolk.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Aïoli, in the egg-added modern sense, is garlic mayonnaise.
  • Rémoulade stirs in herbs, capers, and cornichons.
  • Japanese-style mayonnaise is seasoned with vinegar, sugar, and often MSG, and is a separate product.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Seafood
  • Salads
  • frites
  • cold lobster
  • egg salad
  • sandwiches
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free

Béarnaise

Warm butter emulsion with shallot, vinegar, and tarragon: herbal and sharp.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Béarnaise is a daughter of hollandaise flavored with a reduction of shallot, vinegar, and tarragon. It was popular in Paris restaurants in the nineteenth century. The name refers to the Béarn region, though the sauce as served with steak is a Paris restaurant sauce more than a farmhouse one.

Cultural background

It is the classic French steak sauce beside peppercorn and bordelaise. The tarragon is not optional in the traditional version.

Why it tastes this way

It tastes herbal and sharp because a vinegar, shallot, and tarragon reduction seasons the same butter emulsion as hollandaise. Tarragon, not lemon, is the perfume.

How it is made

Shallot, pepper, and tarragon stems reduce with vinegar, then that reduction flavors an egg-and-butter emulsion.

Authentic touch. Strain the reduction so the shallot does not stay in threads, then stir chopped tarragon leaves in at the end so they stay green.

How it is used

Serve with grilled steak, roasted potatoes, or grilled fish. It should be warm, not hot.

Field notes

  • Béarnaise is hollandaise plus a tarragon reduction, not a separate emulsion method.
  • The Béarn name outran the sauce’s actual regional origin.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons white wine vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons dry white wine
  • 1 shallot, minced
  • 2 sprigs tarragon, stems and leaves separated
  • 3 egg yolks
  • 1/2 cup unsalted butter, melted
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the vinegar, wine, shallot, and tarragon stems until about a tablespoon remains. Strain and cool slightly.
  2. Whisk the yolks with the reduction over gentle heat until thick.
  3. Whisk in the warm butter in a stream.
  4. Chop the tarragon leaves and stir them in. Season with salt.
  5. Hold the sauce warm, not hot, and serve the same day.

Keep the yolks below a simmer. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sauce Choron replaces some of the tarragon with tomato.
  • Sauce Paloise uses mint instead of tarragon.

Pairings and uses

  • Grilling
  • Vegetables
  • grilled steak
  • frites
  • grilled asparagus

Beurre Blanc

White wine, shallot, and butter: sharp, creamy, and pale.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Beurre blanc is the butter sauce of the Loire, especially associated with Nantes and with fish from the river and the Atlantic. It is an emulsion of cold butter into a sharp reduction of shallot and white wine or vinegar. It does not use a roux or, in the classic version, egg.

Cultural background

Loire cooking pairs it with pike and other river fish. Restaurant kitchens later put it on almost any poached or grilled fish.

Why it tastes this way

The sauce tastes of white wine and shallot because that reduction is the only liquid. Cold butter emulsifies into it if the pan stays below a boil. Boiling drives the butter out and the sauce tastes greasy.

How it is made

A shallot and acid reduction is taken off the boil and mounted with cold butter, whisked in piece by piece.

Authentic touch. Whisk in cold butter a few cubes at a time, off a boil. A boil separates it.

How it is used

Spoon it over poached or grilled fish, shellfish, or asparagus.

Field notes

  • Classic beurre blanc has no egg yolk. The butter itself forms the emulsion if the pan is not too hot.
  • It is a Loire sauce that became an international fish-restaurant sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 shallot, minced
  • 1/4 cup white wine vinegar
  • 1/4 cup dry white wine
  • 8 tablespoons cold unsalted butter, cubed
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the shallot, vinegar, and wine until only about 2 tablespoons of liquid remain.
  2. Drop the heat to low. Whisk in the butter a few cubes at a time.
  3. Do not let the sauce boil once the butter goes in.
  4. Season with salt and serve at once.
  5. If it separates, whisk in a spoon of cold water off the heat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Beurre rouge uses red wine instead of white.
  • A splash of cream makes the emulsion more stable and less classic.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Vegetables
  • pike
  • sole
  • scallops
  • asparagus

Demi-Glace

Brown stock reduced with espagnole: glossy, concentrated, and savory.

15 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Demi-glace, in the Escoffier system, is espagnole combined with brown stock and reduced by about half until it is glossy and nappé. The name means half-glaze. A restaurant pan sauce that reduces a purchased stock is related in flavor and is not the full classic preparation.

Cultural background

It is a kitchen staple of French sauce work, used by the spoonful to finish meat sauces. It is not usually eaten by itself.

Why it tastes this way

The deep, glossy taste is reduction: water leaves, and browned stock concentrates. A little tomato and a brown roux add body. It tastes meaty because the liquid is brown stock, not because of a spice mix.

How it is made

Espagnole and brown stock simmer together until the sauce is reduced, shiny, and thick enough to coat a spoon. This home version builds a shorter brown sauce and reduces it.

Authentic touch. Reduce until it coats a spoon. If it tastes thin, it is not done. Salt only at the end, because reduction concentrates salt.

How it is used

Whisk a spoonful into pan juices for steak, short ribs, or a roast. A little goes a long way.

Field notes

  • Half-glaze refers to the reduction, not to a glaze made of sugar.
  • A boxed stock reduced with butter is a useful shortcut and a different sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 carrot, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1/2 cup dry red wine
  • 4 cups unsalted beef stock
  • 1 tablespoon flour mixed with 1 tablespoon soft butter
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Brown the onion and carrot in the oil. Stir in the tomato paste and cook until it darkens.
  2. Add the wine and reduce by half.
  3. Add the stock and simmer until reduced to about 1 1/2 cups.
  4. Whisk in bits of the butter-flour paste and simmer until the sauce coats a spoon.
  5. Strain and season. The result is a home demi-glace, shorter than the classical reduction.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

History

Ragù alla Bolognese is the meat sauce of Bologna, served with tagliatelle or used in lasagna. In 1982 the Accademia Italiana della Cucina deposited a recipe with the Bologna Chamber of Commerce: beef, pancetta, soffritto, wine, milk, and a modest amount of tomato. Cultural background

In Bologna it is a meat sauce first. Tomato seasons it. Milk is part of the traditional pot, not a modern shortcut. Wide fresh egg pasta is the usual partner, not spaghetti.

Why it tastes this way

A slow meat cook with soffritto, wine, milk, and only a little tomato tastes savory and soft, not sharply tomatoed. Milk rounds the meat. The registered Bologna approach keeps tomato as a seasoning.

How it is made

A soffritto and ground or finely chopped meat brown slowly, then wine, milk, and tomato simmer until the sauce is thick and soft.

Authentic touch. Cook the meat gently after it loses its raw color. A hard crust dries it. Give it the long simmer, and toss it with tagliatelle, not a huge amount of sauce.

How it is used

Toss it with tagliatelle, or layer it in lasagna with béchamel.

Field notes

  • A 1982 registered Bolognese recipe includes milk and only a little tomato.
  • Tagliatelle, not spaghetti, is the local pasta.

Carbonara

Guanciale, egg, pecorino, and black pepper: rich, salty, and sharp.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pasta portions

History

Carbonara is a Roman pasta sauce of guanciale, egg, pecorino Romano, and black pepper. Cream is not part of the Roman version. Stories that tie the dish to American rations in the 1940s are disputed, so this page does not repeat them as fact. The method is an emulsion of egg and cheese with hot pasta and rendered pork fat.

Cultural background

It is one of the four famous Roman pasta sauces, with cacio e pepe, gricia, and amatriciana. Guanciale, cured pork jowl, is the meat. Pancetta is the usual substitute.

Why it tastes this way

Cured pork fat, egg, and pecorino emulsify with hot pasta. There is no cream. The gloss is egg and cheese set by residual heat. Too much heat scrambles the egg and the sauce tastes dry.

How it is made

Guanciale renders, pasta is tossed with it off the heat, and beaten egg and pecorino cook in the residual heat into a cream.

Authentic touch. Take the pan off the heat before the egg goes in. Toss until glossy. Guanciale, or pancetta if that is what you can buy, should be rendered, not boiled.

How it is used

Eat it at once from the pan. It does not reheat well.

Field notes

  • Roman carbonara has no cream. The creaminess is egg, cheese, and pasta water.
  • The egg should thicken, not scramble. The pan has to be off the boil.

Ingredients

  • 4 ounces guanciale or pancetta, diced
  • 12 ounces spaghetti
  • 3 egg yolks and 1 whole egg
  • 3/4 cup finely grated pecorino Romano
  • 1 teaspoon coarse black pepper
  • Salt for the pasta water

Steps

  1. Render the guanciale until the fat is liquid and the pieces are golden. Turn off the heat.
  2. Boil the pasta. Mix the eggs, cheese, and pepper in a bowl.
  3. Drain the pasta, reserving water, and toss it with the guanciale off the heat.
  4. Stir in the egg mixture and a splash of pasta water until glossy.
  5. Serve immediately with more pepper.

The egg is cooked only by the hot pasta. Use fresh eggs and eat the dish at once.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Gricia is the same idea without egg.
  • A cream carbonara is a later, non-Roman version.

History

Cacio e pepe is the Roman pasta sauce of pecorino Romano and black pepper, emulsified with pasta water. It has no egg, no cream, and no guanciale. Cultural background

The cheese must be finely grated or it clumps. The pepper is toasted in a dry pan in many Roman kitchens so it smells fragrant before the pasta arrives.

Why it tastes this way

Pecorino and black pepper, emulsified with starchy water, taste sharp and creamy without cream. The cheese is salty, so the pasta water should be only lightly salted. Coarse pepper, toasted, is the spice.

How it is made

Toasted pepper and very finely grated pecorino form a paste with hot pasta water, then the pasta is tossed in until the sauce turns creamy.

Authentic touch. Grate the cheese very fine and make a paste with hot water before the pasta goes in. Coarse shreds clump into strings.

How it is used

Serve it at once in warm bowls. It sets as it cools.

Field notes

  • There is no cream in the sauce. Starch from the pasta water is what makes it look creamy.

  4. Toss the drained pasta with the pepper and the cheese paste, adding water until creamy.

Tomato, baked beans, pepper, and curry spice: tangy, sweet, and hot.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 4 cups

History

Chakalaka is a South African vegetable relish associated with township cooking and with braais. It typically includes onion, pepper, tomato, carrot, baked beans, and curry spices. It does not have a single inventor on record. Commercial jars made it a national pantry item.

Cultural background

It is served cold or warm beside pap, braaied meat, and bread. The spice level is a household choice.

Why it tastes this way

Baked beans, tomato, pepper, and curry spices taste like a braai relish because the vegetables are cooked down with the spices, not left raw. Curry powder is the spice trail in this South African pot.

How it is made

Aromatics and vegetables fry with curry powder, tomato, and beans until thick.

Authentic touch. Simmer until thick enough to sit on pap or bread. A watery pot tastes stewed, not like chakalaka.

How it is used

Spoon it next to grilled meat, pap, or onto bread.

Field notes

  • Chakalaka is a relish, usually eaten beside starch and meat rather than as a glaze.
  • Baked beans are a usual ingredient, which surprises people who expect only fresh vegetables.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 1 carrot, grated
  • 1 tablespoon curry powder
  • 1 can (14 ounces) diced tomatoes
  • 1 can (14 ounces) baked beans
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Fry the onion, pepper, and carrot in the oil until soft.
  2. Stir in the curry powder and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes and beans.
  4. Simmer 20 minutes until thick.
  5. Salt and cool.

Refrigerate and eat within five days. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add cabbage or apple.
  • A hotter chakalaka uses fresh bird’s eye chile.

History

Ata din din is the Yoruba fried pepper sauce: blended tomato, pepper, and onion fried in oil until the raw taste is gone and the sauce turns deep red. It is a base for eggs, boiled yam, and rice, and a cousin of the longer Nigerian stews. Palm oil or vegetable oil is used depending on the cook.

How it is made

Tomato, pepper, and onion are blended, then fried in oil until thick and sweet.

Egusi is the West African sauce-stew thickened with ground melon seeds, cooked across southern Nigeria and also in Benin and Ghana. How it is made

A pepper base is fried, ground egusi is cooked in the oil, then stock, meat, and greens simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook the egusi in the sauce until it no longer tastes raw and pasty. Then add the greens.

How it is used

Serve it with pounded yam or another swallow.

Field notes

  • Egusi is a melon seed, not a peanut and not pumpkin in every market sense.

Hummus

History

Hummus is a Levantine chickpea and tahini dip eaten across Lebanon, Syria, Palestine, Jordan, and Israel. Medieval Arabic cookbooks already describe chickpea dishes. The modern whipped form with tahini and lemon is the everyday mezze sauce. Exclusive national origin claims are newer than the dish.

Cultural background

It is breakfast, mezze, and a sandwich spread. Olive oil and paprika or whole chickpeas on top are presentation, not a different recipe. The map counts it in each Levantine country listed because it is a shared staple.

Why it tastes this way

Chickpeas, tahini, lemon, and garlic are a shared Levantine balance. Tahini makes it nutty, lemon makes it bright, and ice water makes it light instead of pasty. Olive oil on top is finish, not the body.

How it is made

Cooked chickpeas are blended with tahini, lemon, garlic, and ice water until smooth.

Authentic touch. Blend tahini and lemon first until pale, then add chickpeas. Ice water, added while blending, loosens the texture.

How it is used

Eat it with flatbread, as a base under stewed chickpeas, or in a sandwich.

Field notes

  • The dish is shared across the Levant. Pinning it to one capital would be inaccurate.
  • Ice water, added while blending, makes the texture lighter.

Ingredients

  • 1/3 cup tahini
  • 1 lemon, juiced
  • 1 small garlic clove
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons ice water
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil

Steps

  1. Blend the tahini, lemon, garlic, and salt until pale.
  2. Add the chickpeas and blend.
  3. Add ice water until the hummus is smooth and soft.
  4. Taste for salt and lemon.
  5. Spread it and finish with olive oil.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

History

Toum is the Lebanese garlic sauce served with grilled chicken, especially shawarma and rotisserie chicken. Garlic, oil, lemon, and salt are whipped into a white emulsion. Egg is not required. The garlic itself holds the oil if it is added slowly.

Cultural background

It is the sauce in a chicken shawarma wrap and on the plate with toum-painted grilled chicken. A little goes a long way.

Why it tastes this way

Raw garlic emulsified with oil tastes intensely of garlic because there is no cooking to soften it and often no egg to dilute it. Lemon keeps the emulsion bright and white.

How it is made

Garlic and salt are blended, then oil is added in a thin stream with lemon until the sauce turns fluffy and white.

How it is used

Serve it with grilled chicken or as a sandwich spread.

Field notes

  • Toum can emulsify without egg. The garlic is the emulsifier.
  • It is meant to be very garlicky. A mild toum is under-seasoned.

Ingredients

Falafel sandwiches are unfinished without it. The sauce lightens in color as the water goes in, which surprises people the first time.

Why it tastes this way

Sesame paste tastes bitter and thick until lemon and cold water loosen it and turn it pale. That water is why a good tahini sauce tastes light and tangy instead of like a spoon of paste.

How it is made

Tahini, lemon, and garlic are stirred, then cold water is added until the sauce loosens and turns pale.

How it is used

Drizzle it on falafel, roasted cauliflower, or fish.

Field notes

  • Tahini seizes and looks broken when lemon hits it. Water brings it back.
  • Hummus contains tahini sauce’s ingredients plus chickpeas.

Ingredients

Steps

  2. Add the water a spoon at a time until pale and pourable.

How it is made

Roasted peppers and toasted walnuts are blended with pomegranate molasses, bread crumbs, and oil.

Serve it with warm bread or next to grilled vegetables.

Khoresh-e fesenjan is the Persian stew of walnuts and pomegranate, classically with duck or chicken, served with rice. It is associated with the Caspian provinces, especially Gilan. How it is made

Ground walnuts simmer in stock until creamy, pomegranate molasses is added, and chicken or duck cooks in the sauce.

How it is used

Serve it over chelo, Persian rice, with the poultry that simmered in it.

  • The walnuts have to cook long enough to lose their raw flavor.

Sugar dissolves in lime juice and water, then fish sauce, garlic, and chile are stirred in. Nothing is cooked.

  • Nước mắm is the ingredient. Nước chấm is the dipping sauce made from it.

Why it tastes this way

How it is made

Soy, grated pear, garlic, sugar, and sesame are mixed and left on the meat before a fast grill.

Authentic touch. Slice the beef thin and do not skip the pear or another ripe fruit if you want the familiar sweetness and texture.

How it is used

Grill the marinated beef and serve it with rice and lettuce. Boil unused marinade if it touched raw meat and you want to brush it on at the end.

  • Bulgogi is a soy marinade. It is not a chile paste.

A fresh green paste is fried in coconut cream, then coconut milk and meat simmer, and basil finishes the pot.

A spiced paste is fried, then coconut milk, meat, potato, peanut, tamarind, and sugar simmer until the meat is soft.

Pork is the classic meat because of Goa’s Catholic cooking. The vinegar is the point. It should taste sour as well as hot.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar, garlic, and dried chile taste sour and hot because Goan cooks adapted a Portuguese wine-and-garlic marinade to vinegar and local chiles. A tomato gravy with extra chili is a different, British restaurant dish.

How it is made

A paste of chile, garlic, ginger, spices, and vinegar marinates pork, then the meat simmers in that sauce.

Authentic touch. Use enough vinegar that the sauce still tastes sour when the meat is tender. The sour is the point.

How it is used

Serve the pork and sauce with rice.

Field notes

  • Vindaloo is a Goan reading of a Portuguese wine-and-garlic marinade.
  • Vinegar is as important as the chile.

Ingredients

  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon ginger
  • 1 teaspoon cumin

  4. Add the salt and water and simmer until the pork is tender, about 50 minutes.

Mole Poblano

Dried chiles, nuts, seeds, spices, and a little chocolate: deep, mildly hot, and complex.

45 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 6 cups

History

Mole poblano is the thick chile sauce of Puebla, served with turkey or chicken. It uses several dried chiles, a fried puree, nuts and seeds, spices, and a small amount of chocolate as a seasoning, not as a dessert. Picturesque stories about a convent inventing it for a visiting bishop are legends, not reliable history, and they are left off this page.

Cultural background

In Puebla and in Mexican celebrations more broadly, mole poblano is feast food. It is one mole among many. Oaxacan mole negro, mole verde, and pipián are different sauces.

Why it tastes this way

The layered taste is fried chiles, nuts, seeds, and spices, with a little chocolate as one seasoning among many. Puebla’s dried chiles and the fry in fat are why it tastes toasted and deep rather than like a chocolate drink.

How it is made

Dried chiles are fried and soaked, then blended with toasted nuts, seeds, spices, tomato or tomatillo, and chocolate, and the puree is fried in fat and thinned with stock.

Authentic touch. Fry the blended puree in fat until it darkens. That fry, not extra chocolate, is what makes mole taste cooked.

How it is used

Serve with poached or roasted turkey or chicken, sesame seeds on top, and rice or tortillas.

Field notes

  • Chocolate in mole poblano is one ingredient among many, used the way a spice is used.
  • Convent-invention tales are widely told and not treated here as fact.

Ingredients

  • 4 dried ancho chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 3 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 dried pasilla chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 tablespoons lard or neutral oil
  • 1/4 cup almonds
  • 2 tablespoons peanuts
  • 2 tablespoons sesame seeds
  • 1/4 cup raisins
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 2 tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 cups chicken stock, plus more to thin
  • 1 ounce Mexican chocolate, chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Toast the chiles in a dry pan until fragrant, soak them in hot water 20 minutes, and drain.
  2. Fry the nuts, sesame, and raisins in the fat and remove them. Fry the onion and tomato.
  3. Blend the chiles, fried ingredients, cinnamon, and 1 cup stock until smooth.
  4. Fry the puree in the remaining fat, stirring, for about 10 minutes.
  5. Add the rest of the stock and the chocolate. Simmer 40 minutes, stirring, until thick. Season with salt and thin with stock if needed.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mole negro of Oaxaca uses chilhuacle chiles and a darker, smokier method.
  • The chocolate is a seasoning. Adding a large amount turns it into a different sauce.

Authentic touch. Use plenty of black pepper and keep it cold. The pepper is the heat. Chile is optional.

How it is used

Toss it with smoked chicken, or serve it as a dip for smoked meats.

Field notes

  • The sauce is white because the base is mayonnaise, not because it is mild in flavor.
  • It was built for chicken in a state better known, from the outside, for pork and tomato sauces.

  • 1 tablespoon black pepper

  2. Stir in the pepper, salt, sugar, and cayenne.
  3. Rest it 15 minutes so the pepper blooms.
  4. Taste and add vinegar if you want it sharper.
  5. Refrigerate.

This is a mayonnaise sauce. Keep it cold.

Eastern North Carolina Barbecue Sauce

Vinegar, salt, and chile: thin, sharp, and hot.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Eastern North Carolina whole-hog barbecue is dressed with a thin sauce of vinegar and pepper, not a sweet tomato glaze. The style is tied to the eastern part of the state and to pit-cooked pork. It is one of the oldest documented American barbecue sauces still cooked in public.

Cultural background

The sauce is a mop and a table sauce. It cuts the fat of chopped pork and soaks into the meat. Lexington, farther west, adds a little tomato. That is a different sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Whole-hog pork is fatty. Thin vinegar and pepper cut that fat and soak into chopped meat. There is no tomato or sugar body, so the sauce tastes sharp and hot instead of sweet and thick.

How it is made

Cayenne or chile flakes and salt dissolve in cider vinegar. There is no thickener.

Authentic touch. Do not thicken it. If it coats a spoon like ketchup, it has become a different region’s sauce.

How it is used

Dress chopped or pulled pork, and put more on the table.

Field notes

  • The sauce is supposed to be thin. Thickness is a different barbecue region.
  • It is a defining split in North Carolina barbecue, east versus Lexington.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir everything until the salt and sugar dissolve.
  2. Let it sit 10 minutes.
  3. Taste and add more cayenne if you want a hotter mop.
  4. Use it on chopped pork.
  5. Keep the rest refrigerated.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lexington dip adds ketchup or tomato to a vinegar base.
  • Some pits use both red pepper and black pepper.

Kansas City Barbecue Sauce

Tomato, molasses, vinegar, and smoke: thick, sweet, and tangy.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Kansas City barbecue became known for a thick, sweet, tomato-and-molasses sauce in the twentieth century, alongside a broad menu of smoked meats. Henry Perry is widely treated as an early figure in the city’s barbecue scene in the early 1900s. The thick commercial style associated with later restaurants is the one most people mean by Kansas City sauce.

Cultural background

It is a finishing and table sauce more than a thin mop. Burnt ends and ribs in Kansas City are closely tied to this flavor.

Why it tastes this way

Molasses and tomato make it thick and sweet, and vinegar keeps it from being only sweet. That balance suits ribs and burnt ends, which are already smoky. The sauce is a glaze and a table sauce, not a thin mop.

How it is made

Ketchup, molasses, vinegar, and spices simmer until the sauce is thick and glossy.

Authentic touch. Simmer until glossy, then brush it on late. Sugar burns if it spends the whole cook on the fire.

How it is used

Brush it on ribs and brisket near the end of cooking, and serve more alongside.

Field notes

  • Kansas City sauce is thick and sweet on purpose, unlike eastern Carolina vinegar sauce.
  • Molasses is the usual deep sweetener, not only white sugar.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup ketchup
  • 1/4 cup molasses
  • 1/4 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients in a saucepan.
  2. Simmer 20 minutes, stirring, until glossy.
  3. Thin with a splash of water if it is tighter than you want.
  4. Cool before storing.
  5. Refrigerate and use within two weeks.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Memphis sauce is usually thinner and more vinegar-forward.
  • Liquid smoke is optional. Real smoke on the meat matters more.

Cranberry Sauce

Cranberry and sugar: tart, bright, and jammy.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Cranberry sauce is the tart fruit sauce of the American Thanksgiving table, served with roast turkey. Cranberries grow in northern bogs. Sugar balances their sharpness. A jellied slice and a whole-berry sauce are both traditional. This is the whole-berry simmer.

Cultural background

Turkey is rich and mild. The sauce is sour enough to cut it. Orange peel is a common perfume and not required.

Why it tastes this way

Cranberries are intensely tart. Sugar and a short simmer pop the berries and make a loose jam. Too much sugar turns it into candy and it stops cutting the turkey.

How it is made

Cranberries, sugar, and water simmer until the berries burst.

Authentic touch. Simmer until most berries burst and the sauce thickens. Keep it tart. Chill it before dinner.

How it is used

Serve it cold or cool with roast turkey.

Field notes

  • The berries should still taste tart.
  • Whole-berry sauce and the sliced jelly are both old American forms.

Ingredients

  • 12 ounces cranberries
  • 3/4 cup sugar
  • 3/4 cup water
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the cranberries, sugar, water, and salt.
  2. Cook until most berries burst, about 10 minutes.
  3. Cool. It will thicken.
  4. Serve with turkey.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Orange zest is a common addition.
  • A jellied loaf is the molded version.

Louisiana Rémoulade

Mayonnaise, Creole mustard, and paprika: spicy, tangy, and pink.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Louisiana rémoulade is a spicy mayonnaise sauce for cold shrimp and fried seafood, seasoned with mustard, paprika, and often horseradish and hot sauce. It is sharper and redder than French rémoulade, which is herbs and capers. Creole mustard is the local grainy mustard.

Cultural background

Shrimp rémoulade is a New Orleans starter. The sauce should taste of mustard and paprika, not only of mayonnaise. A chill lets it thicken.

Why it tastes this way

Creole mustard is sharp. Paprika and cayenne add color and heat. Lemon and a little horseradish keep it bright. The French herb rémoulade is a cousin with a quieter spice list.

How it is made

Mayonnaise, mustard, paprika, and lemon are stirred together.

Authentic touch. Use a grainy Creole or whole-grain mustard and paprika. Chill the sauce before you dress cold shrimp.

How it is used

Spoon it over cold shrimp or use it as a fried-seafood dip.

Field notes

  • New Orleans rémoulade is a spiced mayonnaise, not the French herb sauce.
  • Paprika is the color.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 2 tablespoons whole-grain mustard
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon prepared horseradish
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon hot sauce
  • 1 scallion, minced
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir everything together.
  2. Chill 30 minutes.
  3. Taste for mustard and heat.
  4. Serve with shrimp.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • French rémoulade is herbier and less red.
  • Horseradish makes it sharper.

Samoan Miti

Coconut cream, onion, and lime: cool, rich, and sharp.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Miti is a Samoan coconut sauce, typically coconut cream with onion and salt, often with lime or lemon, served with cooked taro, breadfruit, or palusami. Related coconut sauces appear elsewhere in Polynesia. This is the fresh coconut-cream version, not a cooked curry.

Cultural background

It is a cool sauce next to hot starches from the umu or the pot. The onion is chopped fine so each bite of taro picks it up.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut cream is rich and cool. Onion and lime cut it so the sauce tastes bright against hot taro or fish. It is not cooked, so the coconut stays sweet instead of oily.

How it is made

Coconut cream is stirred with minced onion, lime, and salt. It is not cooked.

Authentic touch. Salt it and serve it cool. Heat is for the taro, not for the miti.

How it is used

Spoon it over hot taro, breadfruit, or grilled fish.

Field notes

  • Miti means the coconut sauce, not a spice paste.
  • It is meant to be cool against hot taro.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup coconut cream
  • 2 tablespoons minced onion
  • 1 tablespoon lime juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir the coconut cream, onion, lime, and salt.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Serve cool.
  5. Use the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A green-onion-only version is common.
  • Heating the coconut cream makes a different, thicker sauce.

Hawaiian Chili Pepper Water

Chile, garlic, and water: clean, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Chili pepper water is a Hawaiian table condiment of hot chiles steeped in water with garlic, sometimes ginger or vinegar. Small local chiles became part of the islands’ cooking after the plant’s global spread. A bottle of it sits on diner counters beside soy sauce. This is a simple infused version.

Cultural background

It is shaken onto saimin, loco moco, grilled fish, and rice. It is closer to a chile vinegar or a light infusion than to a cooked salsa.

Why it tastes this way

Small hot chiles steeped in water with garlic taste clean and hot, like a table vinegar. It is thin so it can be shaken onto noodles and rice. A cooked ketchup would be a different condiment.

How it is made

Smashed chiles and garlic sit in hot water and a little vinegar until the water tastes hot, then the bottle is refrigerated.

Authentic touch. Let it sit overnight. The water tastes mild the hour you mix it and hotter the next day.

How it is used

Shake it over noodles, rice plates, and grilled fish.

Field notes

  • It is meant to be thin enough to sprinkle, like a table vinegar.
  • The islands’ diner counters helped make it a daily sauce, not a special-occasion one.

Ingredients

  • 6 Hawaiian or other small hot chiles, smashed
  • 2 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 1 cup just-boiled water
  • 1 tablespoon white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Put the chiles and garlic in a clean jar.
  2. Pour on the hot water, vinegar, and salt.
  3. Cool, then refrigerate overnight.
  4. Taste the next day and add a pinch more salt if needed.
  5. Use within two weeks.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some recipes add a knob of ginger.
  • A fully fermented hot sauce is a different project.

Feijoa Chutney

Feijoa, onion, vinegar, and ginger: fragrant, sweet, and sharp.

20 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3 cups

History

Feijoa, a South American fruit grown widely in New Zealand gardens, became a home-kitchen chutney fruit there in the twentieth century. The chutney follows the British cooked-chutney pattern of fruit, onion, vinegar, sugar, and spice, with feijoa’s perfume in place of mango or apple. It is a New Zealand household preserve, not an older Māori sauce.

Cultural background

Jars of it are made when the fruit drops in autumn and are eaten with cold meat and cheese. This version is a refrigerator chutney, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Feijoa’s perfume is the fruit. Vinegar, ginger, and sugar are the British-style chutney method that New Zealand home cooks used on a local glut. The result is fragrant, sweet, and sharp.

How it is made

Chopped feijoa, onion, vinegar, sugar, and ginger simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook it until a spoon leaves a trail. A thin chutney tastes like stewed fruit, not a preserve.

How it is used

Serve it with cold lamb, cheese, or a meat sandwich.

Field notes

  • Feijoa is also called pineapple guava, and it is not native to New Zealand.
  • The chutney style is the British cooked chutney adapted to a local glut of fruit.

Ingredients

  • 4 cups chopped feijoas
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground allspice

Steps

  1. Combine all the ingredients in a wide pot.
  2. Simmer, stirring, until thick, about 40 minutes.
  3. The chutney should hold a trail when you pull a spoon through it.
  4. Cool and jar.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a month. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Apple can replace part of the fruit if the feijoa season is short.
  • A hotter chutney adds a sliced chile.
