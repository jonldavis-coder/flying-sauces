Mapo Sauce

Doubanjiang, fermented black bean, and Sichuan pepper: hot, numbing, and savory.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Mapo tofu is a Chengdu dish whose sauce is doubanjiang, fermented chile-bean paste, with ground meat, fermented black beans, and Sichuan pepper. The name is tied to a pockmarked woman’s tofu shop in Qing-era Chengdu in local food lore. The sauce is the spicy gravy the tofu braises in, eaten with rice.

Cultural background

Doubanjiang does the heavy lifting. Chile oil alone will not taste like mapo. The Sichuan pepper is the numbing layer on top of the heat.

Why it tastes this way

Doubanjiang, fermented chile-bean paste, tastes savory and hot. Sichuan pepper numbs. Fermented black beans add depth. Chile oil alone will not taste like mapo.

How it is made

Ground meat fries with doubanjiang and black beans, stock and tofu simmer, and a starch slurry thickens the sauce.

Authentic touch. Fry the doubanjiang until the oil turns red, then simmer the tofu gently so it does not break apart.

How it is used

Serve it over rice with the tofu.

Field notes

  • Pixian doubanjiang is the paste Sichuan cooks mean for this sauce.
  • Mala, hot and numbing, needs both chile and Sichuan pepper.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 4 ounces ground pork
  • 2 tablespoons doubanjiang
  • 1 teaspoon fermented black beans, rinsed
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 cup stock
  • 14 ounces soft tofu, cubed
  • 1 teaspoon cornstarch mixed with 1 tablespoon water
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground Sichuan pepper

Steps

  1. Fry the pork in the oil.
  2. Add the doubanjiang, black beans, and garlic and fry until the oil turns red.
  3. Add the stock and tofu and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. Stir in the cornstarch slurry.
  5. Finish with Sichuan pepper and serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A meat-free version uses more doubanjiang and mushroom stock and is less classic.
  • Chile oil on top is a finish, not the whole sauce.

Yuxiang Sauce

Doubanjiang, garlic, vinegar, and sugar: hot, sweet, and sour.

10 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Yuxiang, fish-fragrant sauce, is a Sichuan flavor built to suggest the seasonings of fish cookery: doubanjiang, garlic, ginger, scallion, vinegar, and sugar. It is used on eggplant and shredded pork and does not have to contain fish. The name describes the seasoning, not a fish stock.

Cultural background

Eggplant in this sauce is one of the most common Sichuan home dishes. The sweet-sour note is what separates it from a plain mapo fry.

Why it tastes this way

Garlic, doubanjiang, vinegar, and sugar taste hot, sweet, and sour. The name means fish-fragrant because of the seasoning pattern, not because the sauce contains fish. The sweet-sour is what separates it from mapo.

How it is made

Doubanjiang, garlic, and ginger fry, then vinegar, sugar, soy, and a slurry make a glossy sauce.

Authentic touch. Fry the bean paste, then add vinegar and sugar. Both should be obvious when you taste it.

How it is used

Toss it with fried eggplant or with stir-fried pork.

Field notes

  • Fish-fragrant does not mean the sauce contains fish.
  • Vinegar and sugar are what make the flavor, along with the chile bean paste.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 tablespoon doubanjiang
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon ginger
  • 1 tablespoon Chinkiang vinegar
  • 2 teaspoons sugar
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 teaspoon cornstarch mixed with 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Fry the doubanjiang, garlic, and ginger in the oil until the oil reddens.
  2. Add the vinegar, sugar, soy, and water.
  3. Simmer 2 minutes.
  4. Stir in the slurry until glossy.
  5. Toss with cooked eggplant.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pickled chile can join the doubanjiang.
  • A version with wood ear and pork is the classic shredded dish.

Dumpling Dipping Sauce

Soy, black vinegar, garlic, and chile oil: salty, sharp, and hot.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1/2 cup

History

A soy-and-vinegar dip is the usual sauce for Chinese dumplings, especially in the north, where black vinegar is the sharp note. Garlic and chile oil are common additions. There is no single imperial recipe. The balance of vinegar to soy is the cook’s choice, and vinegar often leads.

Cultural background

Dumplings are dipped, not sauced in the pot. The sauce should be thin. A thick glaze belongs on other dishes.

Why it tastes this way

Black vinegar is malty and sharp, soy is salty, and garlic and chile oil heat it. Northern dumpling dips often let vinegar lead. The dumplings are mild, so the dip is strong.

How it is made

Soy, black vinegar, garlic, and a spoon of chile oil are stirred together.

Authentic touch. Use black vinegar if you can. White vinegar tastes harsher and less malty.

How it is used

Serve it with boiled or pan-fried dumplings.

Field notes

  • Black vinegar, not white vinegar, is the usual acid.
  • The dumplings are mild so the dip can be sharp.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons Chinkiang black vinegar
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1 teaspoon chile oil
  • 1 teaspoon sesame oil

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients.
  2. Taste and add vinegar if you want it sharper.
  3. Serve with hot dumplings.
  4. Make it the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More vinegar is the northern lean.
  • Ginger instead of garlic is another family version.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Noodles
  • jiaozi
  • wontons
  • scallion pancakes
  • Vegan

Hoisin Sauce

Fermented soybean, sugar, vinegar, and five-spice: sweet, salty, and aromatic.

5 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Hoisin is a Cantonese sauce based on fermented soybean paste, sweetened and spiced. Commercial jars are the usual pantry form. This recipe seasons fermented bean paste into a home hoisin-style sauce. It does not recreate the factory ferment. It is the sauce for Peking duck pancakes in many kitchens, alongside sweet bean sauce.

Cultural background

It is a glaze, a dip, and a stir-fry seasoning. Five-spice and sesame are the perfume. The sweetness is intentional.

Why it tastes this way

Fermented beans taste savory. Sugar and vinegar make the sauce sweet and sharp. Five-spice, especially star anise, is the aroma people recognize. A plain soy reduction lacks the bean ferment.

How it is made

Bean paste, sugar, vinegar, garlic, and five-spice simmer into a thick sauce.

Authentic touch. Start with fermented bean paste or soybean paste, not only soy sauce. Simmer it with sugar, vinegar, and five-spice until thick.

How it is used

Use it as a dip for pancakes with roast duck, or as a glaze.

Field notes

  • Hoisin means seafood in the name’s words and is not a fish sauce.
  • The base is fermented bean paste.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup fermented soybean paste
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons honey or sugar
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon sesame oil
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until thick, about 8 minutes.
  2. Cool.
  3. Use as a dip or glaze.
  4. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sweet bean sauce, tianmianjiang, is the other classic pancake sauce.
  • This simmer is a kitchen version of a fermented commercial sauce.

Char Siu Sauce

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice: sweet, savory, and glossy.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Char siu is Cantonese roasted pork, lacquered with a sweet soy marinade of hoisin or fermented bean sauce, sugar or honey, and five-spice. The red color of shop char siu often comes from fermented red bean curd or food coloring. This marinade uses hoisin and honey and does not rely on dye.

Cultural background

The pork is roasted and the marinade becomes the glaze. It is eaten with rice or in noodle soups. The sugar burns if it meets high heat too early.

Why it tastes this way

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice taste sweet and savory. The sugar lacquers the pork. Shop-window red is often color, not the honey. High heat too early burns the sugar.

How it is made

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice coat pork, which is roasted and basted.

Authentic touch. Roast first and baste near the end. Boil any marinade that touched raw pork before you use it as a glaze.

How it is used

Slice the roasted pork and serve it with rice. Boil any marinade that touched raw meat before basting at the end.

Field notes

  • Char siu means fork roast, from the way the meat was hung.
  • The red of many shop windows is color, not the honey.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons hoisin sauce
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon honey
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 teaspoon rice wine
  • 1 garlic clove, minced

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients.
  2. Coat pork shoulder or loin and refrigerate at least 2 hours.
  3. Roast until cooked through, basting near the end.
  4. Rest the meat before slicing.
  5. Serve with rice.

Boil marinade that has touched raw pork before you brush it on cooked meat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red fermented bean curd is the traditional color and savor in many shop recipes.
  • Honey can be swapped for maltose, which is stickier.

Scallion Oil

Slow-fried scallion in oil, with soy at the table: sweet, savory, and fragrant.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Scallion oil, you cong, is the sauce of Shanghainese scallion oil noodles. Scallions are fried slowly in a large amount of oil until they are brown and sweet, and the oil is tossed with noodles and a salty soy seasoning. The method is patience. Hot oil dumped on raw scallions is a different, sharper sauce.

Cultural background

It is a noodle-shop and home dish of the lower Yangtze. The fried scallion solids are part of the sauce, not a garnish you throw away.

Why it tastes this way

Scallions fried slowly until chestnut brown taste sweet. The oil takes that sweetness. A fast pour of hot oil on raw scallions tastes sharper and is a different sauce. Soy at the end is the salt.

How it is made

Scallions fry gently in oil until chestnut brown. The oil and the solids are kept together.

Authentic touch. Start the scallions in cold oil and cook them slowly to brown, not black. Keep the fried solids.

How it is used

Toss the oil with hot noodles and a spoon of soy sauce.

Field notes

  • The scallions should cook slowly until sweet. Burnt scallions are bitter.
  • The fried solids stay in the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup neutral oil
  • 2 cups scallions cut in 2-inch lengths
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 12 ounces noodles

Steps

  1. Put the oil and scallions in a cold pan and bring them up slowly.
  2. Fry until the scallions are chestnut brown, about 15 minutes. Do not rush.
  3. Take the pan off the heat.
  4. Toss hot noodles with the oil, scallions, soy, and sugar.
  5. Serve at once.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Ginger fried in the same oil is a related Shanghai finish.
  • A fast raw-scallion oil is sharper and is not this sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Noodles
  • Dipping
  • wheat noodles
  • soy sauce
  • sugar, a pinch
  • Vegan

XO Sauce

Dried scallop, shrimp, ham, and chile in oil: savory, smoky, and rich.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

XO sauce is a Hong Kong condiment that emerged in hotel kitchens in the 1980s. The name borrows the prestige of XO cognac and the sauce contains no cognac. It is dried seafood, often scallop and shrimp, with chile, garlic, and sometimes cured ham, fried in oil. Specific restaurant credits vary, so this page keeps the place and the decade.

Cultural background

A spoonful finishes noodles, vegetables, and fried rice. It is a luxury pantry sauce because of the dried seafood, not because of alcohol.

Why it tastes this way

Dried scallop and shrimp fried in chile oil taste savory and rich. The name refers to a luxury grade of cognac and the sauce contains none. The dried seafood is the flavor.

How it is made

Soaked dried seafood and chile are fried slowly in oil with garlic until the bits are crisp and fragrant.

Authentic touch. Soak and chop the seafood, then fry it slowly until fragrant. Do not expect cognac in the taste.

How it is used

Spoon it onto noodles or stir-fried vegetables.

Field notes

  • XO sauce does not contain cognac. The name is a luxury reference.
  • It became a Hong Kong sauce in the 1980s.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup dried scallops, soaked and finely chopped
  • 1/4 cup dried shrimp, soaked and chopped
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 shallots, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chile flakes
  • 1/2 cup neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce

Steps

  1. Drain and chop the soaked seafood.
  2. Fry the shallot and garlic in the oil until soft.
  3. Add the seafood and chile and fry on low until fragrant and slightly crisp, about 10 minutes.
  4. Stir in the soy.
  5. Cool and refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Jinhua ham is traditional in many batches and is omitted here in favor of the dried seafood.
  • A smoother commercial jar is minced finer than this home one.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood

Teriyaki Sauce

Soy, mirin, and sugar: salty, sweet, and glossy.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Teriyaki is a Japanese technique: tare of soy sauce, mirin, sake, and sugar brushed on broiling food so the surface shines. The word joins teri, shine, and yaki, grill or broil. Bottled thick teriyaki sauce is a later, often sweeter product, especially outside Japan. This recipe is the classic thin glaze.

Cultural background

Fish such as yellowtail and chicken are the usual partners. The sauce reduces on the food. It is not a marinade that sits overnight in a sweet American-Chinese glaze, though those glazes borrow the name.

Why it tastes this way

Soy is the salt, mirin and sugar make the shine and the sweetness, and reduction thickens it. The gloss on the food is the point of teri. It is a basting glaze, so it tastes concentrated, not brothy.

How it is made

Soy, mirin, sake, and sugar simmer into a light syrup, then the food is basted as it cooks.

Authentic touch. Brush it on near the end of grilling. Early brushing burns the sugar.

How it is used

Brush it on grilling fish or chicken in the last minutes, and reduce a few extra spoons as a glaze.

Field notes

  • Teri means the shine the sauce leaves on the food.
  • Classic teriyaki is a basting glaze, not a cup of sauce poured over a finished plate.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup soy sauce
  • 1/4 cup mirin
  • 2 tablespoons sake
  • 1 tablespoon sugar

Steps

  1. Simmer the soy, mirin, sake, and sugar until slightly syrupy, about 8 minutes.
  2. Brush it on fish or chicken near the end of grilling.
  3. Return the pieces to the heat so the glaze shines.
  4. Do not leave the sugar unattended. It burns once it is thick.
  5. Keep leftover glaze refrigerated and boil it before using it on already-cooked food.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Unagi tare is reduced longer and used in layers on eel.
  • A cornstarch slurry makes the thick bottled style and is less traditional.

Pairings and uses

  • Grilling
  • Noodles
  • Rice
  • Seafood
  • grilled chicken
  • yellowtail
  • rice

Ponzu

Soy, citrus, and dashi: light, salty, and bright.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Ponzu is a Japanese sauce of soy sauce and citrus, classically yuzu, often with dashi and vinegar. Bottled ponzu is a pantry staple. The citrus is the point. Lemon can stand in and will not taste like yuzu. It is a dipping and dressing sauce, not a glaze.

Cultural background

It is served with hot pot, tataki, and cold noodles. The dashi makes it more savory than soy and lemon alone.

Why it tastes this way

Soy is salty and citrus is bright. Dashi, when you add it, tastes savory. Yuzu is the traditional aroma. Lemon is sharper and is an honest stand-in, not the same fruit.

How it is made

Soy, citrus, rice vinegar, and dashi are stirred and rested.

Authentic touch. Stir and rest it. Do not reduce it into a glaze. Ponzu should stay thin.

How it is used

Use it as a dip for hot pot or a dressing for cold dishes.

Field notes

  • Ponzu is soy plus citrus. Without the citrus it is just soy sauce.
  • Yuzu is the prized fruit, and lemon is an honest substitute, not the same aroma.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup soy sauce
  • 1/4 cup yuzu or lemon juice
  • 2 tablespoons rice vinegar
  • 1/4 cup dashi
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients until the sugar dissolves.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Taste for citrus.
  4. Serve as a dip.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a few days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Yuzu is traditional. Lemon is the stand-in.
  • A thicker tare is a different sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Seafood
  • Salads
  • Noodles
  • nabe
  • beef tataki
  • cold tofu

Tonkatsu Sauce

Worcestershire-style sauce, tomato, and fruit: thick, sweet, and savory.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Tonkatsu sauce is the thick brown sauce served with Japanese breaded pork cutlets. It grew in the twentieth century alongside tonkatsu, which itself adapted a European cutlet. The sauce leans on Worcestershire-style seasoning, tomato, and fruit. Bulldog is a well-known commercial brand. This is a home version of the style. Okonomiyaki sauce is a sweeter cousin and is treated as a variation.

Cultural background

The cutlet is sliced and the sauce is spooned on, or it is put on the side for katsu curry and sandwiches. It should be thick enough to sit on the crust.

Why it tastes this way

A Worcestershire-style base, tomato, and fruit taste thick, sweet, and savory. It was made for a breaded cutlet, so it sits on the crust. Okonomiyaki sauce is sweeter still.

How it is made

Ketchup, Worcestershire, soy, sugar, and a little grated apple simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Simmer until thick. Grated apple is a classic sweetness. Serve it with the cutlet, not as a soup.

How it is used

Spoon it over sliced tonkatsu.

Field notes

  • Tonkatsu sauce is a Japanese reading of Worcestershire-style sauce, thickened and sweetened.
  • The cutlet is a twentieth-century dish, and the sauce grew up with it.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup ketchup
  • 2 tablespoons Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 2 tablespoons grated apple
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients 10 minutes.
  2. The sauce should be thick and glossy.
  3. Cool slightly.
  4. Spoon over sliced tonkatsu.
  5. Refrigerate leftovers.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Okonomiyaki sauce is sweeter and often includes more sugar and a thicker body.
  • A spoon of mustard sharpens it.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • tonkatsu

  • katsu sandwich

Goma Dare

Sesame paste, soy, and vinegar: nutty, salty, and tangy.

8 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Goma dare is the Japanese sesame sauce used on shabu-shabu, cold spinach, and salads. Toasted sesame paste is loosened with soy, vinegar, and dashi or water. It is a different sauce from Chinese sesame noodles and from Indonesian peanut sauce.

Cultural background

At a shabu-shabu meal it is one of the dips, next to ponzu. On spinach it is a dressing. The sesame should taste toasted.

Why it tastes this way

Toasted sesame paste tastes nutty. Soy and vinegar make it salty and sharp. Water makes it a dip instead of a paste. It is not a peanut satay sauce.

How it is made

Sesame paste is whisked with soy, vinegar, sugar, and water until pourable.

Authentic touch. Whisk in water until it pours. Taste for toasted sesame. Pale raw tahini is a flatter substitute.

How it is used

Use it as a dip for hot pot or a dressing for cold vegetables.

Field notes

  • Goma means sesame. The paste should be toasted sesame, not a pale raw tahini if you can choose.
  • It is a dip and a dressing, thinned until it spoons easily.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons Japanese sesame paste or tahini
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 3 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Stir the sesame paste, soy, vinegar, and sugar.
  2. Add water until the sauce is pourable.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Serve with vegetables or hot pot.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of miso makes a richer dressing.
  • Peanut butter is not the traditional paste.

Pairings and uses

  • Salads

  • Vegetables

Japanese Curry Sauce

Mild curry, onion, and apple: savory, slightly sweet, and thick.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · about 5 cups

History

Japanese curry arrived in the Meiji era through British naval curry and was adapted into a mild, thick sauce served with rice. Commercial roux blocks later made it a weeknight staple. This is a from-scratch roux with curry powder, onion, and apple. It is not an Indian curry and it is not as hot.

Cultural background

It is home and school-lunch food, with potatoes, carrots, and meat over rice. The sweetness often comes from apple or a little honey. This version uses apple.

Why it tastes this way

A browned roux and mild curry powder, with onion and apple, taste savory and slightly sweet, not hot. The style came through British naval curry and changed in Japan. Thickness is correct.

How it is made

A brown roux is seasoned with curry powder, then onion, apple, and stock simmer into a thick gravy.

Authentic touch. Cook the roux and curry powder before the stock so the sauce does not taste of raw flour or raw powder.

How it is used

Serve it over rice with the vegetables and meat that cooked in it.

Field notes

  • Japanese curry came through British naval cooking in the Meiji period, then changed.
  • It is mild and thick on purpose.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 1 tablespoon curry powder
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 1 apple, grated
  • 1 potato, cubed
  • 3 cups chicken stock
  • 1 teaspoon soy sauce
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the butter until soft. Stir in the flour and curry powder for 1 minute.
  2. Add the apple, potato, and stock.
  3. Simmer until the potato is soft and the sauce is thick, about 25 minutes.
  4. Add the soy and salt.
  5. Serve over rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

Ssamjang

Doenjang and gochujang, with garlic and sesame: savory, hot, and salty.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Ssamjang is the Korean paste for ssam, grilled meat wrapped in lettuce. It mixes doenjang, fermented soybean paste, with gochujang, chile paste, plus garlic, sesame oil, and often onion. The two pastes are long ferments. This recipe seasons them. It does not claim to ferment either one from scratch.

Cultural background

A Korean barbecue table sets ssamjang next to the grill. A dab goes on the leaf with rice and meat. It is thicker than a dipping vinegar.

Why it tastes this way

Doenjang tastes salty and savory. Gochujang tastes hot and a little sweet. Garlic and sesame oil bind them into a wrap paste. This seasons the ferments. It does not recreate the months they took.

How it is made

Doenjang and gochujang are stirred with garlic, sesame oil, and a little sugar.

Authentic touch. Mix it thick. A thin sauce slips off the lettuce. Use a small smear with rice and meat.

How it is used

Spread a little on a lettuce leaf with rice and grilled meat.

Field notes

  • Ssamjang is a mix of two fermented pastes, doenjang and gochujang.
  • This page seasons the pastes. It does not reproduce the months-long ferment.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup doenjang
  • 2 tablespoons gochujang
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1 tablespoon sesame oil
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 tablespoon minced onion
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame seeds

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients into a thick paste.
  2. Taste for heat and salt.
  3. Use it the same day for the best onion flavor.
  4. Spread it thinly on lettuce.
  5. Refrigerate leftovers.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More gochujang makes it hotter.
  • A spoon of minced onion keeps it looser.

Bulgogi Marinade

Soy, pear, garlic, sesame, and sugar: savory, sweet, and aromatic.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Bulgogi is Korean grilled sliced beef. The marinade of soy sauce, sugar or pear, garlic, scallion, and sesame is the sauce. The dish’s modern popularity grew in the twentieth century, with roots in earlier seasoned grilled meats. Galbi uses a related, often sweeter marinade for short ribs.

Cultural background

The pear tenderizes and sweetens. The beef is sliced thin and grilled at the table or in a hot pan, then wrapped or served with rice and banchan.

Why it tastes this way

Soy, sugar, garlic, and sesame are the savory-sweet taste. Grated pear adds sweetness and tenderizes thin beef, which is why the meat tastes seasoned through after a short rest.

How it is made

Soy, grated pear, garlic, sugar, and sesame are mixed and left on the meat before a fast grill.

Authentic touch. Slice the beef thin and do not skip the pear or another ripe fruit if you want the familiar sweetness and texture.

How it is used

Grill the marinated beef and serve it with rice and lettuce. Boil unused marinade if it touched raw meat and you want to brush it on at the end.

Field notes

  • The pear is a tenderizer and a sweetener, not only a garnish.
  • Bulgogi is a soy marinade. It is not a chile paste.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup soy sauce
  • 1/2 Asian pear, grated
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 tablespoon toasted sesame oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 scallions, minced
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame seeds
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir the soy, pear, sugar, sesame oil, garlic, scallion, seeds, and pepper.
  2. Mix with thinly sliced beef.
  3. Refrigerate 30 minutes to overnight.
  4. Grill or pan-sear over high heat.
  5. Serve with rice.

Cook marinade that has touched raw beef before you eat it as a sauce.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Galbi marinade is close, often with more sugar and sometimes with kiwi or pear, for short ribs.
  • Gochujang is not part of classic bulgogi. It belongs to other sauces.

Yangnyeom Sauce

Gochujang, ketchup, garlic, and sugar: sweet, hot, and sticky.

5 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Yangnyeom chicken is Korean fried chicken tossed in a sweet-hot sauce of gochujang, ketchup, garlic, and sugar or corn syrup. The style became a national fried-chicken order in the late twentieth century, alongside plain fried chicken. The sauce is a glaze, added after frying so the crust stays briefly crisp.

Cultural background

It is delivery and late-night food. The sauce should cling and taste both sweet and hot. It is not a buffalo sauce, which is vinegar and butter.

Why it tastes this way

Gochujang, ketchup, garlic, and sugar taste sweet and hot. The sauce goes on after frying so the crust stays briefly crisp and the glaze tastes sticky rather than baked on.

How it is made

Gochujang, ketchup, garlic, and sugar simmer into a glaze and are tossed with just-fried chicken.

Authentic touch. Toss just-fried chicken in the hot glaze and serve immediately.

How it is used

Toss fried chicken in the sauce and serve at once.

Field notes

  • The sauce goes on after frying. It is not a batter flavor.
  • Korean fried chicken’s sweet-hot glaze is a late-twentieth-century style.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons gochujang
  • 2 tablespoons ketchup
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon grated ginger
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until glossy, about 5 minutes.
  2. Fry or reheat chicken separately.
  3. Toss the chicken in the sauce.
  4. Serve at once, before the crust softens.
  5. The glaze should taste sweet and hot.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Soy-garlic chicken is the milder sibling glaze.
  • More gochujang makes it hotter and less ketchup-sweet.
