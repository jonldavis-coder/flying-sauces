Adjika

Fresh chile, garlic, and herbs: hot, salty, and green or red.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Adjika is a chile and herb paste of Abkhazia and western Georgia. Fresh hot chiles, garlic, salt, and herbs such as cilantro and blue fenugreek are pounded together. Red and green versions depend on the chile. It is both a condiment and a seasoning for stews. Dry spice blends sold under the same name are a different product.

Cultural background

A little seasons a pot of beans or a grilled meat. It is salty enough to keep in the refrigerator as a paste. Megrelian cooking uses it heavily.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh chile, garlic, herbs, and salt taste hot and green or red because the paste is raw. Salt is both flavor and a way to keep it. A dry spice mix sold as adjika is a different product.

How it is made

Chiles, garlic, herbs, and salt are pounded or blended to a coarse paste. It is not cooked.

Authentic touch. Pound it coarse and salt it well. Use it by the spoonful. It is a seasoning paste, not a gravy.

How it is used

Serve a small spoonful with grilled meat, or stir a little into beans.

Field notes

  • The fresh paste and the dry spice mix sold as adjika are not the same thing.
  • Salt is part of how the paste is kept.

Ingredients

  • 6 fresh red chiles, stemmed
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 cup cilantro and parsley
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon white wine vinegar

Steps

  1. Blend or pound the chiles, garlic, herbs, and salt to a coarse paste.
  2. Stir in the vinegar.
  3. Taste. It should be very hot and salty.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Use by the spoonful.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green adjika uses unripe chiles and more herbs.
  • A cooked tomato adjika is a related sauce and a different texture.

Afghan Cilantro Chutney

Cilantro, chile, garlic, and vinegar: bright, hot, and sharp.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

A green chile and cilantro chutney is served with Afghan dumplings such as mantu and ashak, and with grilled kebabs. Walnut versions also exist. This is the herb-and-chile table sauce, not the Indian pudina chutney, though the family resemblance is real.

Cultural background

It is part of the dumpling spread, next to a yogurt sauce and a meat filling. The vinegar keeps it sharp.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro, chile, garlic, and vinegar stay raw, so the chutney tastes bright and hot next to rich dumplings and grilled meat. Vinegar is the sharp edge.

How it is made

Cilantro, chile, garlic, and vinegar are blended with a little water. It is not cooked.

Authentic touch. Blend it the day you eat it. Cilantro dulls if it sits overnight.

How it is used

Spoon it over mantu or next to kebabs.

Field notes

  • Afghan dumpling meals often put a meat sauce, a yogurt sauce, and this green chutney on the same plate.
  • It is uncooked, so the cilantro stays bright.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cilantro leaves
  • 1 green chile
  • 1 garlic clove
  • 2 tablespoons white vinegar
  • 1/4 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Blend everything until nearly smooth.
  2. Add a spoon of water if it will not turn.
  3. Taste for salt and vinegar.
  4. Rest 10 minutes.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A walnut version is thicker and richer.
  • Yogurt garlic sauce is served beside it, not instead of it.

Pairings and uses

  • Rice
  • mantu
  • ashak

Aïoli

Garlic pounded with olive oil: sharp, thick, and pungent.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3/4 cup

History

Provençal aïoli is garlic and olive oil pounded to an emulsion, traditionally without egg. The grand aïoli is a meal of salt cod, vegetables, and snails around a bowl of it. Many restaurant aïolis add egg and are garlic mayonnaise. This recipe is the egg-free mortar version.

Cultural background

In Provence it is Friday and feast food as much as a sauce. The garlic should be tasted. A timid aïoli is a different sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Raw garlic pounded with olive oil tastes sharp because there is no egg to soften it and no cooking to tame the garlic. Provençal oil is the fat. The sauce should taste frankly of garlic.

How it is made

Garlic and salt are pounded to a paste, then olive oil is worked in drop by drop until the sauce stands up.

Authentic touch. Pound the garlic and salt to a paste before any oil. Add the oil drop by drop. Rushing splits it.

How it is used

Serve it with boiled vegetables, salt cod, or grilled fish.

Field notes

  • Traditional Provençal aïoli has no egg. The garlic is the emulsifier.
  • The grand aïoli is a whole spread of vegetables and salt fish, not only the bowl of sauce.

Ingredients

  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Pound the garlic and salt to a glossy paste.
  2. Add the oil a few drops at a time, pounding until it thickens.
  3. Keep adding oil until the sauce is thick.
  4. Stir in the lemon juice.
  5. Serve the same day.

This is a fresh garlic-and-oil emulsion. Refrigerate it and use it within a few days. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Egg-yolk aïoli is garlic mayonnaise and is more stable.
  • Rouille adds chile and fish broth.

Ají (Ecuador)

Tree tomato, hot chile, onion, and cilantro: tart, hot, and fresh.

15 min prep · 2 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Ecuadorian table ají is a raw or barely blended sauce of hot chile, tree tomato (tamarillo), onion, cilantro, and lime. It is served with hornado, llapingachos, and other highland plates. It is not Peruvian ají amarillo sauce, which is built on the yellow ají pepper and is often cooked. Tree tomato is the tart fruit that makes the Ecuadorian table sauce taste like itself.

Cultural background

A bowl sits on the table and each person spoons it on. The heat depends on the chile and on how many seeds are left in. The fruit keeps it from being only fire.

Why it tastes this way

Tree tomato is sharply tart, more than a ripe red tomato, which is why this ají tastes sour even before the lime. The chile is the heat and a little fruit of its own. Onion and cilantro stay raw, so the sauce tastes fresh. Salt and a spoon of water loosen it enough to drip off a spoon onto potatoes or pork.

How it is made

Tree tomato, chile, onion, cilantro, lime, and salt are blended with a little water until spoonable, not smooth as a puree.

Authentic touch. Use tree tomatoes if you can. Peel them after a short blanch. An ordinary red tomato is a different sauce. Leave the chile seeds in if you want the heat the table expects.

How it is used

Spoon it over hornado, potato cakes, or grilled meat. Make it the day you eat it.

Field notes

  • Tree tomato, not roma tomato, is the fruit in the usual highland ají.
  • Peruvian ají amarillo is a different chile sauce.

Ingredients

  • 4 tree tomatoes (tamarillos)
  • 1 or 2 hot red or orange chiles
  • 1/2 white onion, chopped
  • 1/2 cup cilantro leaves
  • Juice of 1 lime
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup water

Steps

  1. Score the tree tomatoes, cover them with boiling water for 1 minute, then peel them.
  2. Chop the chiles. Keep the seeds for more heat.
  3. Blend the tomatoes, chiles, onion, cilantro, lime, salt, and water until spoonable but not glassy.
  4. Taste for salt.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hotter chile makes a hotter bowl. The fruit stays the same.
  • Some tables add a little oil. The fruit and chile are the sauce.

Ají Amarillo Sauce

Yellow ají, onion, garlic, and oil: fruity, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Ají amarillo is the yellow chile at the center of much Peruvian cooking. A simple cooked sauce of the chile, onion, garlic, and oil is a base for stews and a table sauce. The chile is fruity rather than only hot. Pastes sold in jars are the practical way to cook it outside the Andes.

Cultural background

Peruvian kitchens use ají amarillo in causa, papa a la huancaína’s cousin sauces, and everyday saltados. This is the plain cooked chile sauce, not the cheese sauce huancaína.

Why it tastes this way

Ají amarillo tastes fruity as well as hot. Frying the paste with onion and garlic cooks off the raw edge and leaves that fruit in a savory sauce.

How it is made

Onion and garlic soften in oil, then ají paste or soaked dried ají is fried and loosened with water.

Authentic touch. Fry the paste in oil before you add water. Skipping the fry leaves a raw, dusty taste.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled chicken, potatoes, or rice, or stir a spoonful into a stew.

Field notes

  • Ají amarillo tastes fruity. Heat is only part of the point.
  • Jarred paste is a legitimate ingredient when fresh pods are unavailable.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/4 cup ají amarillo paste
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the garlic and ají paste and fry 2 minutes.
  3. Stir in the water and salt and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. Add the vinegar.
  5. Cool slightly and use as a spooning sauce.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Crema de ají blends the chile with mayonnaise and is milder.
  • Huancaína adds cheese and crackers and is a different sauce.

Ají Colombiano

Chile, scallion, and cilantro in vinegar: fresh and hot.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Ají is the Colombian fresh chile sauce, often with scallion, cilantro, and vinegar or lime, served alongside hogao rather than instead of it. The chile varies by region. It is a table hot sauce, not the cooked tomato base.

Cultural background

A bowl of aji sits on the table so each person heats their own plate. Hogao is the savory base. Ají is the heat.

Why it tastes this way

Raw chile and vinegar taste sharp and hot. Scallion and cilantro keep it fresh. It is thin enough to spoon, not a paste.

How it is made

Chile, scallion, cilantro, vinegar, and salt are chopped together.

Authentic touch. Chop it and dress it with vinegar. Serve it the same day beside the meal, not cooked into the beans.

How it is used

Spoon it on empanadas, grilled meat, or beans.

Field notes

  • Ají and hogao do different jobs on the same plate.
  • The heat depends on the local chile.

Ingredients

  • 4 fresh hot chiles, minced
  • 4 scallions, minced
  • 1/2 cup cilantro
  • 2 tablespoons vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Stir everything together.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A blended ají is more liquid.
  • Lime instead of vinegar is brighter.

Ajvar

Roasted red pepper and eggplant: smoky, sweet, and silky.

30 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Ajvar is a Balkan roasted-pepper relish, especially associated with Serbia and North Macedonia. Red peppers and often eggplant are roasted, chopped, and cooked down with oil and vinegar in the autumn. Ljutenica and pinđur are related pepper relishes with more tomato or a different balance, and they are not the same jar.

Cultural background

Families roast large batches when peppers are cheap and keep the relish for winter. It is eaten with bread, grilled meat, and eggs.

Why it tastes this way

Roasted red pepper and eggplant taste smoky and sweet because the skins char and the flesh cooks down in oil. Vinegar keeps it from being only sweet. It is an autumn preserve, meant to taste of the roast.

How it is made

Peppers and eggplant are roasted, peeled, and simmered with oil and vinegar until thick.

Authentic touch. Char the peppers until the skins blacken, then peel them. Skipping the char is why some ajvar tastes like boiled pepper.

How it is used

Spread it on bread or serve it beside grilled meats and eggs.

Field notes

  • Autumn pepper roasting is part of making ajvar, not an optional garnish.
  • The name is used across several Balkan countries for closely related relishes.

Ingredients

  • 4 red bell peppers
  • 1 small eggplant
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 garlic clove, minced

Steps

  1. Roast the peppers and eggplant until the skins blacken. Steam them in a covered bowl, then peel and seed them.
  2. Chop the flesh fine.
  3. Simmer it with the oil, vinegar, salt, and garlic until thick, about 30 minutes.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Hot ajvar uses more chile.
  • Pinđur leans on eggplant and tomato and is a cousin, not a duplicate.

Amba

Pickled mango, turmeric, and fenugreek: sour, spicy, and savory.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Amba is the Iraqi pickled-mango condiment. The name comes from the word for mango, via Indian trade, and the sauce is tied to Iraqi cooking and to Iraqi Jewish communities. It is now also a standard sauce in Israel, especially with sabich. Turmeric, chile, and fenugreek scent the mango. This is a quicker cooked version, not a long traditional pickle.

Cultural background

It is eaten with falafel, sabich, and grilled meat. The color is yellow from turmeric. A commercial jar is fermented or brined longer than this pot.

Why it tastes this way

Unripe mango, vinegar, turmeric, and fenugreek taste sour, yellow, and slightly bitter. The mango traveled by trade. Fenugreek is the bitter note that marks amba.

How it is made

Green mango simmers with vinegar, turmeric, fenugreek, chile, and salt until soft and sharp.

Authentic touch. Keep the fenugreek modest. Too much tastes soapy. This simmer is a kitchen version, not a long pickle.

How it is used

Spoon it into a sandwich or next to grilled meat.

Field notes

  • The name means mango.
  • Iraqi amba and the sauce in an Israeli sabich are the same condiment’s path through migration.

Ingredients

  • 2 firm green mangoes, peeled and diced
  • 1/2 cup white vinegar
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground fenugreek
  • 1/2 teaspoon chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until the mango is soft, about 20 minutes.
  2. Mash part of it so the sauce is chunky, not smooth.
  3. Taste for salt and vinegar.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. This is not a long ferment. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A long brine is the traditional preserve. This simmer is a kitchen version.
  • Fenugreek is the bitter note. Leaving it out makes a simpler mango pickle.

American Burger Sauce

Mayonnaise, ketchup, pickle, and onion: creamy, sweet, and sharp.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

A creamy pink burger sauce of mayonnaise, ketchup, and chopped pickle is a standard American diner sauce, related to Thousand Island dressing. Chain restaurants sell their own versions. This is a home recipe for that style, not a copy of a trademarked sauce.

Cultural background

It lives on burgers and as a fry dip. The pickles and onion are what separate it from plain ketchup or plain mayonnaise.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise makes it creamy, ketchup makes it sweet and pink, and pickle and onion keep it sharp. That mix stands up to a salty grilled patty and a soft bun.

How it is made

Mayonnaise and ketchup are stirred with pickle, onion, and a little vinegar.

Authentic touch. Chop the pickle and onion very fine so the sauce spreads instead of falling off the bun in chunks.

How it is used

Spread it on burgers, or use it as a dip for fries and onion rings.

Field notes

  • The pink color is ketchup meeting mayonnaise, the same idea as salsa golf in Argentina, with a different history.
  • This page does not reproduce a chain’s secret formula.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup mayonnaise
  • 2 tablespoons ketchup
  • 2 tablespoons finely chopped dill pickle
  • 1 tablespoon minced onion
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon paprika
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients together.
  2. Rest 15 minutes so the onion softens.
  3. Taste for salt and vinegar.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Use within a few days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thousand Island adds more relish and sometimes egg.
  • A dash of hot sauce makes a spicier house sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Dipping
  • hamburgers
  • fries
  • onion rings

American Yellow Mustard

Vinegar and mustard seed, colored with turmeric: sharp and bright.

10 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Bright yellow prepared mustard became an American condiment in the early twentieth century. French’s yellow mustard, introduced at the 1904 St. Louis World’s Fair, is the commercial example most often cited. The color comes from turmeric. The heat is from white or yellow mustard seed, softened by vinegar.

Cultural background

It is the mustard of hot dogs and ballpark sausages, milder and brighter than Dijon or English powder mustard.

Why it tastes this way

How it is used

Mayonnaise

Oil emulsified with egg yolk and acid: thick, neutral, and rich.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Mayonnaise is a cold emulsion of oil, egg yolk, and vinegar or lemon. French cookbooks of the early nineteenth century already treat it as a standard sauce. A story that ties the name to Mahón in Menorca after a French victory in 1756 is often repeated and not settled, so this page keeps the documented part: it is a French sauce technique with a disputed name origin.

Cultural background

From French tables it became a global sandwich and salad sauce. Belgian frites are often eaten with mayonnaise. Japanese Kewpie mayonnaise is a later, seasoned style with a different recipe.

Why it tastes this way

The neutral richness is oil suspended in yolk and acid. Mustard helps the emulsion and adds a small bite. The oil you choose is the flavor: a strong olive oil tastes loud, a neutral oil tastes clean.

How it is made

Oil is whisked drop by drop into yolk, mustard, and acid until a thick emulsion forms, then the rest of the oil can go in faster.

Authentic touch. Start the oil drop by drop. Once the sauce thickens, you can stream it. Dumping the oil in at the start is how mayonnaise breaks.

How it is used

Spread it on sandwiches, serve it with cold seafood or fries, or use it as the base of rémoulade and aïoli-style garlic mayonnaise.

Field notes

  • Mustard helps the emulsion and is why many home recipes include a spoon of Dijon.
  • The Menorca naming story is popular and not proven, so it is not treated here as fact.

Ingredients

  • 1 egg yolk
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice or white wine vinegar
  • 1 cup neutral oil or light olive oil
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Whisk the yolk, mustard, lemon juice, and salt until smooth.
  2. Whisk in the oil a few drops at a time until the mixture thickens.
  3. Add the remaining oil in a thin stream, whisking steadily.
  4. Adjust the salt and acid. Whisk in a teaspoon of water if it is stiffer than you want.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a few days.

This uses raw yolk. Refrigerate it and use it within a few days, or use a pasteurized yolk.

Fresh chile, garlic, and ginger: very hot and sharp.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3/4 cup

History

Sakay is the Malagasy chile condiment, a paste or oil of hot chiles with garlic and often ginger. It is on the table for rice, grilled meat, and stews. Heat levels vary, and the condiment is supposed to be hot. It is not a mild tomato ketchup.

Cultural background

A dab seasons a plate of rice. Families keep a jar. Ginger is a common partner to the chile and is included here.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh chile, garlic, and ginger pounded together taste very hot and sharp. It is a dab for rice, so it is concentrated. A mild ketchup would not season the plate the same way.

How it is made

Chiles, garlic, and ginger are pounded with salt and a little oil and vinegar.

Authentic touch. Pound it coarse, salt it, and use a small amount. It is a condiment, not a pouring gravy.

How it is used

Serve a small amount beside rice and grilled zebu or chicken.

Field notes

  • Sakay is a chile paste, used by the dab, not a pouring gravy.
  • Ginger is a frequent partner and is part of this version.

Ingredients

  • 8 fresh red chiles
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon grated ginger
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Pound or blend the chiles, garlic, ginger, and salt.
  2. Stir in the vinegar and oil.
  3. Taste carefully.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Serve in small amounts with rice.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

Salsa Brava

Tomato, paprika, and chile: smoky, lightly hot, and thick.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Salsa brava is the sauce on patatas bravas, the bar potatoes of Spain. Madrid’s version is typically a thickened tomato and paprika sauce, sometimes with a separate aioli. Other cities make a spicier sauce or serve both white and red sauces. The potato dish spread through Spanish tapas bars in the twentieth century.

Cultural background

It is a tapa sauce first. The potatoes are fried, and the sauce is spooned on. Heat varies by bar. Smoked paprika is the usual flavor, not only cayenne.

Why it tastes this way

Smoked paprika and tomato, lightly thickened, taste warm and mildly hot on fried potatoes. The potato is mild and crisp. The sauce is the spice. Some bars skip tomato. This is the tomato version.

How it is made

Onion, paprika, and tomato simmer, then a little flour or a reduction thickens the sauce.

Authentic touch. Fry the paprika in oil for a few seconds before the tomato so it blooms. Burnt paprika turns bitter.

How it is used

Spoon it over fried potatoes. A garlic mayonnaise beside it is the two-sauce style.

Field notes

  • Brava refers to the spicy sauce, and the potatoes are the vehicle.
  • Madrid’s tomato version and hotter regional versions are both served as bravas.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 1 cup crushed tomato
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sherry vinegar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the oil until soft.
  2. Stir in the paprika, cayenne, and flour for 30 seconds.
  3. Add the tomato and salt and simmer 12 minutes.
  4. Stir in the vinegar.
  5. Spoon over fried potatoes.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some bars use only a spicy oil, with no tomato.
  • The two-sauce plate adds garlic mayonnaise.

Salsa Macha

Dried chile and garlic fried in oil, with nuts: crunchy, smoky, and hot.

10 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 10 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Salsa macha is a Mexican oil-based chile sauce, especially associated with Veracruz, of dried chiles, garlic, and often peanuts or sesame fried until crisp. It is closer to a chile crisp than to a fresh pico. The oil is the sauce as much as the solids.

Cultural background

A spoonful seasons eggs, tacos, and fruit with chile and salt. Peanuts are common and are why this version is not nut-free. The chiles should fry until fragrant, not burnt.

Why it tastes this way

Dried chiles fried in oil taste toasted and smoky. Garlic and peanuts add savor and crunch. The oil carries the heat, which is why it stays potent in small amounts.

How it is made

Dried chiles, garlic, and peanuts fry in oil, then salt and vinegar are added.

Authentic touch. Fry the chiles and garlic in oil until they are crisp and fragrant, then cool them in that oil. Burnt chile tastes bitter.

How it is used

Spoon the oil and the crunchy bits over eggs, tacos, or vegetables.

Field notes

  • The oil is part of the sauce, not a byproduct.
  • It is a fried chile condiment, not a fresh tomato salsa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup neutral oil
  • 6 dried árbol or guajillo chiles, stemmed
  • 6 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1/4 cup peanuts
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon vinegar

Steps

  1. Fry the peanuts and garlic in the oil until the garlic is golden. Remove them.
  2. Fry the chiles in the same oil until fragrant and slightly crisp.
  3. Cool, then stir the solids back in with salt and vinegar.
  4. Keep the oil.
  5. Spoon both oil and bits over food.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

How it is made

Tomato, chile, onion, and garlic are charred, blended, and fried briefly.

How it is used

Spoon it on tacos, eggs, or grilled meat.

History

Mexican salsa verde is built on tomatillos, not on Italian parsley. Tomatillos are husked, boiled or roasted, and blended with chile, onion, and often cilantro. The sauce for enchiladas verdes and for tacos is this family.

Cultural background

The tart taste is the tomatillo itself. Cilantro is common and not universal. Roasting makes it sweeter and smokier. Boiling makes it brighter.

Why it tastes this way

Tomatillos are naturally tart, which is why salsa verde tastes sour even before lime. Chile brings heat. A fry or a simmer softens the raw edge.

How it is made

Tomatillos and chile are cooked, then blended with onion, garlic, and cilantro.

Authentic touch. Husk and rinse the tomatillos. The sticky coat is bitter if you leave it on. Then boil or roast them until they soften.

How it is used

Use it on enchiladas, chicken, or tacos.

Field notes

  • The green is tomatillo, not parsley.
  • Rinse off the sticky husk residue before cooking.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 onion
  • 1 garlic clove

Steps

  3. Fry the salsa 3 minutes if you want it rounder.

Tomato Ketchup

Cooked tomato, vinegar, and warm spice: sweet, sour, and smooth.

15 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 16 · about 2 cups

History

English ketchups of the eighteenth century were often mushroom, walnut, or fish sauces. The name is linked to words for fish sauce in Southeast Asia and southern China, borrowed into English. Tomato ketchup became the American table sauce in the nineteenth century. Henry J. Heinz began selling his tomato ketchup in 1876, and the sweet cooked style became the default.

Cultural background

It is the sauce of American burgers, fries, and meatloaf. This recipe is a home cooked ketchup, not a copy of a factory formula.

Why it tastes this way

Long cooking with vinegar and sugar turns tomato from sharp and watery into sweet, sour, and smooth. Earlier English ketchups were mushroom or fish. The American taste is the tomato version of that idea.

How it is made

Tomatoes, vinegar, sugar, and spices simmer until thick, then the sauce is blended and simmered again.

Authentic touch. Simmer until thick. A short boil tastes like seasoned tomato juice, not ketchup.

How it is used

Use it on burgers and fries, or as the base of cocktail sauce and barbecue sauce.

Field notes

  • Early English ketchup was not necessarily made of tomato.
  • The sweet tomato style became the American standard in the nineteenth century.

Ingredients

  • 1 can (28 ounces) tomato puree
  • 1/3 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 3 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon onion powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon mustard powder
  • Pinch of clove
  • Pinch of cinnamon

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients in a saucepan.
  2. Simmer 40 minutes, stirring, until thick.
  3. Blend until smooth if you want a bottled texture.
  4. Simmer 5 minutes more.
  5. Cool and refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within three weeks. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mushroom ketchup is the older English cousin.
  • Cocktail sauce adds horseradish and is a separate sauce.

Worcestershire-style sauce, tomato, and fruit: thick, sweet, and savory.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Tonkatsu sauce is the thick brown sauce served with Japanese breaded pork cutlets. It grew in the twentieth century alongside tonkatsu, which itself adapted a European cutlet. The sauce leans on Worcestershire-style seasoning, tomato, and fruit. Bulldog is a well-known commercial brand. This is a home version of the style. Cultural background

The cutlet is sliced and the sauce is spooned on, or it is put on the side for katsu curry and sandwiches. It should be thick enough to sit on the crust.

Why it tastes this way

A Worcestershire-style base, tomato, and fruit taste thick, sweet, and savory. It was made for a breaded cutlet, so it sits on the crust. Okonomiyaki sauce is sweeter still.

How it is made

Ketchup, Worcestershire, soy, sugar, and a little grated apple simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Simmer until thick. Grated apple is a classic sweetness. Serve it with the cutlet, not as a soup.

How it is used

Spoon it over sliced tonkatsu.

Field notes

  • Tonkatsu sauce is a Japanese reading of Worcestershire-style sauce, thickened and sweetened.

Ingredients

Steps

Toum

Garlic and oil, whipped: fluffy, sharp, and intense.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Toum is the Lebanese garlic sauce served with grilled chicken, especially shawarma and rotisserie chicken. Garlic, oil, lemon, and salt are whipped into a white emulsion. Egg is not required. The garlic itself holds the oil if it is added slowly.

Cultural background

It is the sauce in a chicken shawarma wrap and on the plate with toum-painted grilled chicken. A little goes a long way.

Why it tastes this way

Raw garlic emulsified with oil tastes intensely of garlic because there is no cooking to soften it and often no egg to dilute it. Lemon keeps the emulsion bright and white.

How it is made

Garlic and salt are blended, then oil is added in a thin stream with lemon until the sauce turns fluffy and white.

Authentic touch. Add the oil in a thin stream. If it breaks, start with a fresh garlic clove and beat the broken sauce in slowly. Refrigerate it. Do not leave it at room temperature.

How it is used

Serve it with grilled chicken or as a sandwich spread.

Field notes

  • Toum can emulsify without egg. The garlic is the emulsifier.
  • It is meant to be very garlicky. A mild toum is under-seasoned.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup peeled garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 cups neutral oil
  • 1/4 cup lemon juice
  • 2 tablespoons ice water

Steps

  1. Blend the garlic and salt to a paste.
  2. With the motor running, add a few drops of oil until the paste looks sticky and white.
  3. Alternate small additions of oil and lemon, then the ice water.
  4. Stop when the sauce is fluffy. If it tastes harsh, rest it 10 minutes.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a few days. Do not store garlic-in-oil at room temperature.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • If it breaks, start again with a fresh garlic clove and beat the broken sauce in slowly.

How it is made

How it is used

Serve it as a dip for grilled pork or lumpia.

How it is made

Hot peppers, garlic, and vinegar simmer and are blended with salt.

Tzatziki

Yogurt, cucumber, garlic, and dill: cool, sharp, and thick.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Tzatziki is the Greek yogurt and cucumber sauce served with grilled meats, fried vegetables, and bread. Thick strained yogurt, grated cucumber, garlic, olive oil, and dill or mint are the usual pieces. Turkish cacık is a close relative and is often thinner, more like a cold soup. They are sibling sauces, not one recipe.

Cultural background

It is meze and a plate sauce for souvlaki and gyros. The cucumber has to be salted and drained or the sauce turns watery. Garlic is to taste and is not shy in many tavern versions.

Why it tastes this way

Strained yogurt, garlic, and cucumber taste cool and sharp. Cucumber water would thin it, so the cucumber is salted and squeezed. Dill or mint is the herb. It is thicker than Turkish cacık.

How it is made

Cucumber is salted and squeezed, then folded into thick yogurt with garlic, oil, and dill.

Authentic touch. Salt the grated cucumber and squeeze it dry before it meets the yogurt. Use thick yogurt.

How it is used

Serve it cold with grilled meat, pita, or fried eggplant.

Field notes

  • Draining the cucumber is the difference between a sauce and a puddle.
  • Cacık is related and is written as its own sauce in this guide.

Ingredients

  • 1 cucumber, grated
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 1/2 cups strained plain yogurt
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon chopped dill
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Salt the cucumber, rest 10 minutes, and squeeze it dry.
  2. Stir it into the yogurt with the garlic, oil, dill, and lemon.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Refrigerate 20 minutes.
  5. Serve cold.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cacık is the Turkish relative, often looser and with mint.
  • Mint instead of dill is used in some Greek houses.

Wisconsin Beer Cheese

Cheddar, beer, and mustard: sharp, malty, and smooth.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Beer cheese is a Wisconsin tavern and supper-club dip of cheddar, beer, and mustard. The state’s German-founded brewing and cheesemaking made both ingredients ordinary. The dip as served with pretzels and crackers is a twentieth-century bar food. This page does not name a single inventor.

Cultural background

It is a spread for pretzels and rye bread. The beer should be something you would drink. Cheddar is the body. Mustard keeps it from being only salty cheese.

Why it tastes this way

Aged cheddar tastes sharp. Beer adds malt and a little bitterness and loosens the cheese. Mustard and garlic keep it savory. German-American brewing and dairy are why those two ingredients meet in Wisconsin pots.

How it is made

Cheddar is melted with beer, mustard, and a little flour so it stays smooth.

Authentic touch. Grate the cheese fine and melt it over low heat. A boil makes it grainy. Use a simple lager, not a sweet soda-like malt drink.

How it is used

Serve it warm with pretzels or bread.

Field notes

  • Wisconsin beer cheese is tavern food, not a nineteenth-century farm recipe with a known author.
  • German immigration is why beer and cheddar were both everyday ingredients there.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 1 cup lager beer
  • 8 ounces sharp cheddar, grated
  • 1 teaspoon mustard
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Cook the butter and flour 1 minute.
  2. Whisk in the beer and simmer until thick.
  3. Add the cheese, mustard, garlic, and salt over low heat until smooth.
  4. Do not boil.
  5. Serve warm.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

Onion, tomato, habanero, and sour orange are chopped and salted. Nothing is cooked.
