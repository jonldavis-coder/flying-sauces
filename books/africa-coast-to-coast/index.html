Chermoula

Cilantro, cumin, paprika, garlic, and lemon: herbaceous and warm.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Chermoula is the Moroccan herb marinade for fish, built on cilantro, garlic, cumin, paprika, oil, and lemon. It is spread on fish before grilling or baking. Similar herb pastes are used across the Maghreb. This is the uncooked fish marinade, not a simmered tomato sauce.

Cultural background

Coastal Moroccan cooking puts it on sardines and whole fish. The paprika and cumin make it redder and warmer than a plain cilantro sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro, cumin, paprika, garlic, and lemon stay raw, so the marinade tastes fresh and warm. Paprika and cumin are why it tastes redder and spicier than a plain herb oil. It is built for fish.

How it is made

Herbs, spices, garlic, lemon, and oil are pounded or blended into a loose paste.

Authentic touch. Blend it coarse and salt it enough to season the fish. Keep a spoonful aside before it touches raw fish if you want sauce at the table.

How it is used

Rub it on fish and grill or bake it. Keep a spoonful that never touched raw fish for the table.

Field notes

  • Chermoula is a marinade and a sauce. Reserve some before it touches raw fish if you want it at the table.
  • Cumin and paprika are as defining as the herbs.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro leaves
  • 1/2 cup parsley leaves
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil

Steps

  1. Blend the herbs, garlic, spices, salt, and lemon.
  2. Stream in the oil.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Rub it on fish and refrigerate up to a few hours.
  5. Grill or bake the fish until cooked through.

Do not serve marinade that has touched raw fish unless you cook it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of tomato makes a saucier baked-fish version.
  • Parsley often joins the cilantro.

Harissa

Dried chile, garlic, coriander, and caraway: hot, aromatic, and oily.

25 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Harissa is the chile paste of Tunisia, built from dried chiles, garlic, salt, and the spices coriander and caraway, then covered with olive oil. It is a staple condiment and a cooking ingredient across Tunisian meals. Similar pastes exist in Algeria and Libya, but harissa as a named paste is Tunisian.

Cultural background

It is stirred into couscous broth, spread on bread, and used to season stews. Baklouti chile is traditional when you can get it. Other hot dried chiles change the flavor.

Why it tastes this way

Dried chiles, garlic, coriander, and caraway, covered in olive oil, are the taste. Caraway is why it does not taste like a plain chile paste. Oil keeps the paste and carries the flavor.

How it is made

Soaked dried chiles are pounded or blended with garlic, spices, and oil into a thick paste.

Authentic touch. Seed the chiles if you want heat without bitterness, and cover the jar with oil so the top does not dry out.

How it is used

Serve a spoonful with couscous, grilled meat, or eggs, or fry a little in oil at the start of a stew.

Field notes

  • Caraway is a usual harissa spice, which is one way it differs from many other chile pastes.
  • A layer of oil on top is how the paste is kept.

Ingredients

  • 4 ounces dried hot red chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon coriander seeds, toasted
  • 1 teaspoon caraway seeds, toasted
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup olive oil, plus more to cover

Steps

  1. Soak the chiles in hot water 20 minutes. Drain.
  2. Grind the coriander and caraway.
  3. Blend the chiles, garlic, spices, salt, and oil to a paste.
  4. Pack into a jar and cover with a thin layer of oil.
  5. Refrigerate.

Cover the surface with oil and refrigerate. Use within a few weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Rose harissa is a commercial style scented with rose. It is not the plain pantry paste.
  • Libyan filfel chuma is a garlic-heavier chile paste and a different recipe.

Filfel Chuma

Hot paprika or chile, garlic, and caraway: fiery and savory.

10 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Filfel chuma is the Libyan Jewish chile-and-garlic paste, fragrant with caraway and sometimes cumin. It seasons soups, fish, and Shabbat dishes. It is hotter and more garlicky than many harissas, and the spice balance is its own. A short cooked version in oil is how many home cooks keep it.

Cultural background

A spoonful goes into a pot rather than covering a plate. Libyan cooking in diaspora kept the paste as a defining flavor.

Why it tastes this way

A heavy amount of garlic, hot chile, and caraway tastes fiery and savory. Caraway is the spice that sets it apart from many other chile pastes. Oil carries it.

How it is made

Garlic, hot paprika or ground chile, caraway, salt, and oil are cooked briefly into a paste.

Authentic touch. Fry the paste gently in oil until it smells toasted, then keep it under a film of oil.

How it is used

Stir a little into stews, or serve a small dab with fish and bread.

Field notes

  • Caraway and a heavy hand with garlic distinguish it from a generic chile oil.
  • It is used by the spoonful as a seasoning paste.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup hot paprika or ground chile
  • 6 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon ground caraway
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/3 cup neutral oil
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Stir the paprika, garlic, caraway, salt, oil, and water.
  2. Cook over low heat 5 minutes, stirring, until fragrant and thick.
  3. Cool.
  4. Pack in a jar and cover with a film of oil.
  5. Refrigerate.

Cover with oil and refrigerate. Use within a few weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Harissa uses more coriander and is the Tunisian paste.
  • Fresh red chiles can replace paprika if they are very hot.

Koshari Sauce

Tomato, vinegar, garlic, and cumin: tangy, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

The tomato sauce on Egyptian koshari is a sharp, spiced sauce of tomato, garlic, vinegar, and cumin, ladled over the rice, lentils, and pasta. Koshari is Egypt’s famous street bowl. The sauce is cooked and often finished with a garlic-vinegar daqqa. It is not an Italian pasta sauce.

Cultural background

A bowl of koshari without the red sauce and the hot sauce on the side is incomplete. Cumin and vinegar are what make it taste like the street version.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato cooked with cumin, garlic, and vinegar tastes tangy and savory. The vinegar is why it cuts a heavy bowl of rice, lentils, and pasta. Cumin is the spice.

How it is made

Onion and garlic fry, tomato and cumin simmer, and vinegar is stirred in at the end.

Authentic touch. Add the vinegar near the end and taste. The sauce should be sharp, not only tomato-sweet.

How it is used

Ladle it over koshari: rice, lentils, and pasta.

Field notes

  • Koshari sauce is defined by vinegar and cumin as much as by tomato.
  • The dish stacks rice, lentils, and pasta under this sauce.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 1 can (14 ounces) crushed tomatoes
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon chili flakes

Steps

  1. Fry the onion in the oil until golden.
  2. Add the garlic, tomato paste, and cumin and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes, salt, and chile and simmer 15 minutes.
  4. Stir in the vinegar.
  5. Serve over rice, lentils, and pasta.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A separate garlic-vinegar splash is the hotter street finish.
  • More chile makes a sauce closer to the hot bottle beside the tomato one.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping

Shito

Dried fish, shrimp, chile, and oil cooked down: dark, hot, and savory.

20 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 12 · about 2 cups

History

Shito is a Ghanaian chile sauce of dried fish or dried shrimp, onion, tomato, chile, and a large amount of oil, cooked until dark. Black shito is cooked longer and darker than fresher red versions. It is a staple accompaniment to kenkey, rice, and grilled fish.

Cultural background

Ga and other Ghanaian kitchens treat shito as a cooked condiment that keeps under oil. The dried seafood is the savor, not a garnish.

Why it tastes this way

Dried fish and shrimp, fried long in chile and oil, taste dark and savory because the seafood and the oil concentrate. It is a cooked condiment, not a fresh pepper salsa.

How it is made

Onion, ginger, and dried seafood fry in oil with chile and tomato until the sauce is dark and jammy.

Authentic touch. Cook it slowly until the color deepens and the oil separates. Pale, quickly fried shito tastes raw.

How it is used

Spoon it on rice, kenkey, grilled fish, or boiled yam.

Field notes

  • Black shito gets its color from a long fry, not from a dye.
  • It is an oily chile condiment meant to be eaten by the spoonful, not a thin vinegar hot sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup neutral oil
  • 1 onion, minced
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1/4 cup dried shrimp, ground
  • 1/4 cup dried fish, flaked fine
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 tablespoons chile powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Fry the onion and ginger in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the dried shrimp and fish and fry until fragrant.
  3. Stir in the tomato paste and chile powder.
  4. Cook on low, stirring often, until the sauce is dark and jammy, about 30 minutes.
  5. Salt it, cool it, and refrigerate.

Keep it refrigerated. Do not store the oil and seafood at room temperature for days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green shito uses fresh chiles and herbs and is cooked less dark.
  • The dried shrimp can be reduced if you want less funk, and the sauce will be milder in savor.

Pairings and uses

  • Stews
  • Dipping
  • Breakfast
  • kenkey
  • jollof rice
  • grilled tilapia

Maafe

Peanut, tomato, and chile: rich, savory, and gently hot.

20 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 cups

History

Maafe, also spelled mafé, is a peanut stew sauce of the western Sahel, cooked in Mali, Senegal, The Gambia, and neighboring countries. Peanuts reached West Africa through Atlantic trade and became a staple crop and a sauce base. No single town owns the recipe.

Cultural background

It is Sunday food and everyday food, served with rice or fonio. Chicken, lamb, or vegetables simmer in the sauce. Senegalese domoda is a close relative.

Why it tastes this way

Peanuts became a Sahel staple and a sauce base. Tomato and chile season the nut, and a long simmer makes the peanut taste round instead of pasty. Unsweetened peanut butter is the right flavor.

How it is made

A tomato-onion base is enriched with peanut butter and stock, then simmered with meat or vegetables until the sauce is thick.

Authentic touch. Whisk the peanut butter into hot stock so it dissolves. A lump of cold peanut butter stays grainy.

How it is used

Serve over rice with the meat or vegetables that cooked in the sauce.

Field notes

  • The sauce is known across several Sahel countries under related names.
  • Natural peanut butter without sugar is the right ingredient.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1/2 cup unsweetened peanut butter
  • 3 cups chicken stock
  • 1 scotch bonnet, whole
  • Salt
  • 1 pound chicken pieces or firm vegetables

Steps

  1. Fry the onion, garlic, and ginger in the oil.
  2. Stir in the tomato paste and peanut butter.
  3. Add the stock and whisk until smooth. Add the whole chile and the chicken or vegetables.
  4. Simmer until the sauce is thick and the chicken is cooked through, about 40 minutes.
  5. Remove the chile, salt the sauce, and serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Vegetable maafe skips the meat and uses a vegetable stock.
  • Ghanaian groundnut soup is a related peanut sauce with its own seasoning.

Pairings and uses

  • Stews
  • Rice
  • Meat
  • steamed rice
  • chicken
  • sweet potato

Yassa

Onion, lemon or lime, and chile: sharp, sweet, and savory.

20 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups with the chicken

History

Yassa is the Senegalese onion and citrus marinade and sauce, especially chicken yassa, widely associated with the Casamance region. Meat or fish marinates in lemon, onion, mustard or chile, and oil, then the onions cook down into the sauce. It is served with rice.

Cultural background

The pile of onion is the sauce, not a garnish. Lime or lemon has to be plentiful. Mustard appears in many Dakar versions.

Why it tastes this way

A large amount of onion cooked in lemon, mustard, and chile tastes sweet and sharp. The onion is the body of the sauce. A small handful will not taste like yassa.

How it is made

Chicken marinates in lemon, onion, chile, and mustard, then the onions cook in oil until soft and the chicken simmers in that sauce.

Authentic touch. Marinate in lemon and onion, then cook those same onions down until they are soft. Do not discard them.

How it is used

Serve the chicken and the onion sauce over rice.

Field notes

  • The onions are the body of the sauce. A small amount will not taste like yassa.
  • Casamance is the region most often named with chicken yassa.

Ingredients

  • 2 onions, sliced
  • 1/4 cup lemon juice
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 tablespoon Dijon mustard
  • 1 scotch bonnet, seeded and minced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 pound chicken pieces
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Mix the onion, lemon, oil, mustard, chile, and salt. Coat the chicken and refrigerate 1 hour.
  2. Remove the chicken and brown it in a pan. Set it aside.
  3. Cook the onion marinade until the onion is soft and sweet.
  4. Return the chicken, add the water, and simmer until cooked through.
  5. Serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fish yassa uses the same onion sauce.
  • A spoon of mustard is common and not in every pot.

Ata Din Din

Fried tomato and pepper: hot, savory, and thick.

15 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

Ata din din is the Yoruba fried pepper sauce: blended tomato, pepper, and onion fried in oil until the raw taste is gone and the sauce turns deep red. It is a base for eggs, boiled yam, and rice, and a cousin of the longer Nigerian stews. Palm oil or vegetable oil is used depending on the cook.

Cultural background

It is everyday sauce, hotter or milder with the pepper chosen. The fry is what separates it from a fresh pepper blend.

Why it tastes this way

Blended tomato and pepper fried in oil taste sweet and deep once the raw edge is gone and the oil separates. The fry is the difference from a fresh pepper blend.

How it is made

Tomato, pepper, and onion are blended, then fried in oil until thick and sweet.

Authentic touch. Fry the puree, stirring, until the color darkens and the oil shows. Pale sauce still tastes raw.

How it is used

Spoon it over rice, boiled yam, or eggs.

Field notes

  • Din din refers to the fried sauce, not a raw salsa.
  • The sauce is done when the oil separates and the raw pepper smell is gone.

Ingredients

  • 4 tomatoes
  • 2 red bell peppers
  • 1 scotch bonnet
  • 1 onion
  • 1/3 cup oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup meat or vegetable stock

Steps

  1. Blend the tomatoes, peppers, chile, and onion.
  2. Heat the oil and fry the puree, stirring, until it darkens and the oil shows, about 25 minutes.
  3. Add the stock and salt and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. The sauce should be thick.
  5. Serve with rice or yam.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Palm oil makes a different, more traditional pot for some cooks.
  • Stock instead of water makes it a light stew.

Egusi Sauce

Melon seed, pepper, and bitter leaf or spinach: savory, thick, and hot.

20 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 cups

History

Egusi is the West African sauce-stew thickened with ground melon seeds, cooked across southern Nigeria and also in Benin and Ghana. Tomato and pepper are fried, the ground seed is stirred in, and greens and meat or fish simmer in it. Egusi is a seed, not a peanut, which is why this page does not tag it as a nut sauce allergen.

Cultural background

It is eaten with pounded yam, fufu, or rice. Bitter leaf, spinach, or ugu are the greens. The seed should cook until it no longer tastes raw.

Why it tastes this way

Ground melon seed, cooked in a pepper base, tastes nutty and thick. It is a seed, not a peanut. Greens and a long cook round the raw seed taste.

How it is made

A pepper base is fried, ground egusi is cooked in the oil, then stock, meat, and greens simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook the egusi in the sauce until it no longer tastes raw and pasty. Then add the greens.

How it is used

Serve it with pounded yam or another swallow.

Field notes

  • Egusi is a melon seed, not a peanut and not pumpkin in every market sense.
  • The same seed sauce is cooked in several countries around the Bight of Benin.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup ground egusi
  • 1/4 cup palm oil or neutral oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 tomatoes
  • 1 scotch bonnet
  • 2 cups meat stock
  • 4 cups chopped spinach
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Blend the onion, tomatoes, and chile.
  2. Fry that puree in the oil until it tightens.
  3. Stir in the egusi and cook 8 minutes, stirring.
  4. Add the stock and simmer 15 minutes.
  5. Stir in the spinach and salt and cook until wilted.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A smoother egusi is fried as a paste. A lumpier one is steamed in balls.
  • Spinach stands in when bitter leaf is unavailable and tastes milder.

Pairings and uses

  • Stews
  • Meat
  • pounded yam
  • fufu

Peri-Peri Sauce

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, lemon, and oil: hot, sharp, and bright.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Peri-peri, or piri-piri, is the African bird’s eye chile and the sauce made from it. The chile’s Swahili name, pilipili, passed into Portuguese. The sauce of chile, garlic, lemon, and oil or vinegar is associated with Mozambique and Angola and with Portuguese grilled chicken. This is a home sauce in that style, not a bottled brand formula.

Cultural background

Portuguese taverns made peri-peri chicken famous far beyond southern Africa. The chile itself is African. The grill style is a shared Mozambican and Portuguese table.

Why it tastes this way

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, and lemon are the heat and the brightness. Oil or vinegar carries them. The chile is fruity and hot, which is the taste of the sauce on grilled chicken.

How it is made

Chiles and garlic are blended with lemon and oil, then briefly simmered so the raw edge softens.

Authentic touch. Keep some sauce aside before it touches raw chicken if you want a table sauce. Marinade that has held raw meat has to be cooked.

How it is used

Use it as a marinade and a basting sauce for grilled chicken, and put more on the table.

Field notes

  • The chile name traveled from Swahili into Portuguese.
  • The sauce seasons the chicken. It is not a tomato barbecue glaze.

Ingredients

  • 8 fresh red bird’s eye chiles
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar

Steps

  1. Blend the chiles, garlic, lemon, oil, paprika, salt, and vinegar.
  2. Simmer the puree 5 minutes.
  3. Cool.
  4. Use some as a marinade and save some for the table.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A vinegar-heavy version keeps longer and tastes sharper.
  • Yogurt is not part of the classic sauce.

Awaze

Berbere, wine or water, and oil: hot, spiced, and thick.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1/2 cup

History

Awaze is an Ethiopian dipping and marinade paste made by stirring berbere with tej, wine, or water and sometimes oil. Berbere itself is the chile-and-spice blend. Awaze is what happens when that blend becomes a sauce. It is served with kitfo in some meals and used to marinate meat.

Cultural background

The heat and the spice list live in the berbere. This page does not pretend a teaspoon of chili powder is berbere. Use a berbere you trust.

Why it tastes this way

Berbere already holds chile, garlic, and warm spices. Loosened with wine or water, it tastes like that blend in sauce form. A plain chili powder will not.

How it is made

Berbere is stirred with water or dry red wine and a little oil into a thick dip.

Authentic touch. Start from a berbere you trust. Stir it to a thick paste. This page does not rebuild berbere from a guess.

How it is used

Use it as a dip for bread or a marinade for meat.

Field notes

  • Awaze is berbere loosened into a sauce. Berbere alone is the dry or paste spice.
  • Mitmita is a different, often hotter Ethiopian chile blend.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons berbere
  • 2 tablespoons water or dry red wine
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir the berbere with the water or wine.
  2. Add the oil and salt.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. The paste should be thick enough to cling to bread.
  5. Use it the same day as a dip, or as a marinade you then cook.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wine version is sharper.
  • More oil makes it a looser spooning sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Grilling
  • Meat
  • injera
  • grilled beef
  • kitfo, if you know that dish’s raw-beef risks

Chakalaka

Tomato, baked beans, pepper, and curry spice: tangy, sweet, and hot.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 4 cups

History

Chakalaka is a South African vegetable relish associated with township cooking and with braais. It typically includes onion, pepper, tomato, carrot, baked beans, and curry spices. It does not have a single inventor on record. Commercial jars made it a national pantry item.

Cultural background

It is served cold or warm beside pap, braaied meat, and bread. The spice level is a household choice.

Why it tastes this way

Baked beans, tomato, pepper, and curry spices taste like a braai relish because the vegetables are cooked down with the spices, not left raw. Curry powder is the spice trail in this South African pot.

How it is made

Aromatics and vegetables fry with curry powder, tomato, and beans until thick.

Authentic touch. Simmer until thick enough to sit on pap or bread. A watery pot tastes stewed, not like chakalaka.

How it is used

Spoon it next to grilled meat, pap, or onto bread.

Field notes

  • Chakalaka is a relish, usually eaten beside starch and meat rather than as a glaze.
  • Baked beans are a usual ingredient, which surprises people who expect only fresh vegetables.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 1 carrot, grated
  • 1 tablespoon curry powder
  • 1 can (14 ounces) diced tomatoes
  • 1 can (14 ounces) baked beans
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Fry the onion, pepper, and carrot in the oil until soft.
  2. Stir in the curry powder and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes and beans.
  4. Simmer 20 minutes until thick.
  5. Salt and cool.

Refrigerate and eat within five days. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add cabbage or apple.
  • A hotter chakalaka uses fresh bird’s eye chile.
