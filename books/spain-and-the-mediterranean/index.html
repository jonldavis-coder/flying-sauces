They share olive oil and garlic in places. They are not one Mediterranean sauce.

Romesco

Roasted tomato, dried pepper, almond, hazelnut, and vinegar: smoky and nutty.

20 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Romesco is the sauce of the fishermen of Tarragona in Catalonia, served with grilled calçots in winter and with seafood through the year. Roasted tomato, dried ñora or other mild dried pepper, fried bread in some versions, almonds, hazelnuts, garlic, vinegar, and oil are pounded together. This recipe uses nuts and skips the bread.

Cultural background

The calçotada, the spring onion feast, is the famous setting. The sauce is also everyday with grilled fish. Salvitxada is the related calçot sauce and is close enough that some tables use the names loosely. This page keeps romesco as the nut and dried-pepper sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Roasted tomato, dried pepper, almond, and hazelnut taste smoky and nutty. Vinegar sharpens the nuts. It is a Tarragona sauce for grilled onions and seafood, built by pounding, not by a long tomato simmer.

How it is made

Tomatoes and garlic are roasted, nuts are toasted, and everything is pounded with vinegar and oil.

Authentic touch. Toast the nuts and char the tomato. Raw nuts and raw tomato taste flat in romesco.

How it is used

Serve with grilled spring onions, seafood, or roasted vegetables.

Field notes

  • Romesco belongs to Tarragona’s fishermen as much as to the calçot festival.
  • Ñora pepper is traditional. A soaked ancho is a practical stand-in and not identical.

Ingredients

  • 2 tomatoes
  • 4 garlic cloves, unpeeled
  • 1 dried ñora or ancho chile, stemmed and seeded
  • 1/4 cup almonds
  • 1/4 cup hazelnuts
  • 2 tablespoons sherry vinegar
  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Roast the tomatoes and garlic until soft. Peel them.
  2. Soak the chile 15 minutes. Toast the nuts.
  3. Blend the tomatoes, garlic, chile, nuts, vinegar, and salt.
  4. Stream in the oil.
  5. The sauce should be thick and spoonable. Rest it 20 minutes.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Salvitxada is the calçot sauce some cooks treat as a close cousin.
  • A slice of fried bread is traditional in many family recipes and adds wheat.

  • grilled prawns

Salsa Brava

Tomato, paprika, and chile: smoky, lightly hot, and thick.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Salsa brava is the sauce on patatas bravas, the bar potatoes of Spain. Madrid’s version is typically a thickened tomato and paprika sauce, sometimes with a separate aioli. Other cities make a spicier sauce or serve both white and red sauces. The potato dish spread through Spanish tapas bars in the twentieth century.

Cultural background

It is a tapa sauce first. The potatoes are fried, and the sauce is spooned on. Heat varies by bar. Smoked paprika is the usual flavor, not only cayenne.

Why it tastes this way

Smoked paprika and tomato, lightly thickened, taste warm and mildly hot on fried potatoes. The potato is mild and crisp. The sauce is the spice. Some bars skip tomato. This is the tomato version.

How it is made

Onion, paprika, and tomato simmer, then a little flour or a reduction thickens the sauce.

Authentic touch. Fry the paprika in oil for a few seconds before the tomato so it blooms. Burnt paprika turns bitter.

How it is used

Spoon it over fried potatoes. A garlic mayonnaise beside it is the two-sauce style.

Field notes

  • Brava refers to the spicy sauce, and the potatoes are the vehicle.
  • Madrid’s tomato version and hotter regional versions are both served as bravas.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 tablespoon flour
  • 1 cup crushed tomato
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sherry vinegar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the oil until soft.
  2. Stir in the paprika, cayenne, and flour for 30 seconds.
  3. Add the tomato and salt and simmer 12 minutes.
  4. Stir in the vinegar.
  5. Spoon over fried potatoes.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some bars use only a spicy oil, with no tomato.
  • The two-sauce plate adds garlic mayonnaise.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Tacos and tortillas
  • patatas bravas
  • fried potatoes
  • aioli

Mojo Rojo

Dried red pepper, cumin, garlic, and oil: warm, spicy, and sharp.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Mojo rojo is the red sauce of the Canary Islands, served with papas arrugadas, the salty wrinkled potatoes, and with grilled meats. Dried peppers, cumin, garlic, vinegar, and oil are the core. Mojo verde, the cilantro version, is the other half of the pair. Canarian mojo is not Cuban mojo, which is a sour-orange garlic sauce.

Cultural background

A meal of wrinkled potatoes without mojo is unfinished in the Canaries. The red mojo is the spicier of the two. Recipes differ by island and house.

Why it tastes this way

Dried pepper, cumin, garlic, and vinegar taste warm and spicy. Canarian mojo is not Cuban mojo. Cumin is the spice that makes the red sauce smell like the islands’ potato sauce.

How it is made

Dried pepper, garlic, cumin, and vinegar are blended with oil into a loose sauce.

Authentic touch. Soak the dried pepper until soft, then blend. A gritty, unsoaked pepper tastes harsh.

How it is used

Spoon it over salty boiled potatoes and grilled meat.

Field notes

  • Canarian mojo and Cuban mojo share a name and not a recipe.
  • Red and green mojo are served as a pair with the same potatoes.

Ingredients

  • 2 dried guajillo or other mild dried chiles, soaked
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 2 tablespoons sherry vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon paprika

Steps

  1. Blend the soaked chiles, garlic, cumin, vinegar, paprika, and salt.
  2. Stream in the oil.
  3. Taste for salt and vinegar.
  4. Rest 15 minutes.
  5. Serve with wrinkled potatoes.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mojo verde uses cilantro and green pepper instead of dried red pepper.
  • A piece of bread in the blender is a common thickener and adds wheat.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Meat
  • Tacos and tortillas
  • papas arrugadas
  • grilled pork
  • bread
  • Vegetarian

Mojo Verde

Cilantro, green pepper, garlic, and cumin: fresh, herbal, and sharp.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Mojo verde is the green Canarian sauce, usually cilantro or parsley with green pepper, garlic, cumin, vinegar, and oil. It is the partner of mojo rojo and of the same salty potatoes and grilled fish. It is not Mexican salsa verde.

Cultural background

Fish and papas arrugadas are the setting. Some houses use parsley when cilantro is unpopular. Both are local.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro or parsley, green pepper, garlic, and cumin stay raw, so the sauce tastes fresh next to the cooked red mojo. The pair is the point with salty potatoes.

How it is made

Herbs, pepper, garlic, cumin, and vinegar are blended with oil. Nothing is cooked.

Authentic touch. Blend it the day you serve it. Herbs fade. Taste the salt against the salty potatoes so you do not oversalt.

How it is used

Serve it beside mojo rojo with potatoes and grilled fish.

Field notes

  • Green mojo is an herb sauce. Red mojo is a dried-pepper sauce. They are a pair.
  • It is Canarian, not a tomatillo salsa.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro leaves
  • 1/2 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 2 tablespoons white wine vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup olive oil

Steps

  1. Blend the cilantro, pepper, garlic, cumin, vinegar, and salt.
  2. Stream in the oil.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Rest 10 minutes.
  5. Serve with potatoes or fish.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Parsley replaces cilantro in many family recipes.
  • A green chile makes it hotter.

Pairings and uses

Salsa Vizcaína

Dried choricero pepper and onion: sweet, deep, and smooth.

30 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · about 2 cups

History

Salsa vizcaína is the Basque sauce of dried choricero peppers and onion, the sauce of bacalao a la vizcaína. The peppers are soaked and scraped. Onion is cooked slowly in olive oil. Tomato appears in some pots and is not the main flavor. The sauce is a Bizkaia classic, not a general Spanish tomato sauce.

Cultural background

Salt cod and this sauce are festive food, especially around holidays. The choricero pepper is the ingredient that makes it vizcaína. Paprika alone is only an approximation.

Why it tastes this way

Dried choricero pepper and slow onion, not a pile of fresh tomato, are the sweet, deep taste. The pepper pulp is scraped from soaked skins. Onion cooked until sweet is the other half.

How it is made

Onion cooks slowly in oil, choricero pulp is added, and the sauce is simmered and blended smooth.

Authentic touch. Scrape the pepper pulp and discard the skins. Cook the onion slowly until it is sweet before the pulp goes in.

How it is used

Serve it with desalted salt cod or other white fish.

Field notes

  • The pepper pulp, not a pile of fresh tomato, is the body of the sauce.
  • Bacalao a la vizcaína is the dish that carries it.

Ingredients

  • 6 dried choricero peppers, or 3 anchos as a stand-in
  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 2 onions, finely sliced
  • 1 garlic clove
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Soak the peppers until soft. Scrape out the pulp and discard the skins.
  2. Cook the onions in the oil very slowly until sweet, about 25 minutes.
  3. Add the pepper pulp and a splash of the soaking water.
  4. Simmer 10 minutes and blend until smooth.
  5. Salt and serve with fish.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of tomato is used by some cooks and argued over by others.
  • If choricero peppers are unavailable, the sauce will not taste the same. Ancho is only a distant stand-in.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Meat
  • salt cod
  • potatoes
  • olive oil
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Aïoli

Garlic pounded with olive oil: sharp, thick, and pungent.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3/4 cup

History

Provençal aïoli is garlic and olive oil pounded to an emulsion, traditionally without egg. The grand aïoli is a meal of salt cod, vegetables, and snails around a bowl of it. Many restaurant aïolis add egg and are garlic mayonnaise. This recipe is the egg-free mortar version.

Cultural background

In Provence it is Friday and feast food as much as a sauce. The garlic should be tasted. A timid aïoli is a different sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Raw garlic pounded with olive oil tastes sharp because there is no egg to soften it and no cooking to tame the garlic. Provençal oil is the fat. The sauce should taste frankly of garlic.

How it is made

Garlic and salt are pounded to a paste, then olive oil is worked in drop by drop until the sauce stands up.

Authentic touch. Pound the garlic and salt to a paste before any oil. Add the oil drop by drop. Rushing splits it.

How it is used

Serve it with boiled vegetables, salt cod, or grilled fish.

Field notes

  • Traditional Provençal aïoli has no egg. The garlic is the emulsifier.
  • The grand aïoli is a whole spread of vegetables and salt fish, not only the bowl of sauce.

Ingredients

  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3/4 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Pound the garlic and salt to a glossy paste.
  2. Add the oil a few drops at a time, pounding until it thickens.
  3. Keep adding oil until the sauce is thick.
  4. Stir in the lemon juice.
  5. Serve the same day.

This is a fresh garlic-and-oil emulsion. Refrigerate it and use it within a few days. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Egg-yolk aïoli is garlic mayonnaise and is more stable.
  • Rouille adds chile and fish broth.

Pairings and uses

  • salt cod
  • boiled potatoes
  • green beans
  • grilled fish

Pesto alla Genovese

Raw basil, garlic, pine nuts, cheese, and olive oil: green, sharp, and rich.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Pesto alla Genovese is the basil sauce of Liguria. The name comes from pestare, to pound. The modern form, with basil, garlic, pine nuts, salt, Ligurian olive oil, and a mix of Parmesan and a hard Sardinian or local sheep cheese, was fixed in Genoese cooking in the nineteenth century. A mortar gives a creamier sauce than a blender.

Cultural background

It is a Ligurian sauce for trofie, trenette, and green beans with potato, not a generic herb paste. Other Italian pestos, such as Trapanese, are different recipes.

Why it tastes this way

Raw basil, garlic, pine nuts, cheese, and Ligurian olive oil are the flavor. Heat is not part of it. Pounding bruises the basil less than a long blend, so the sauce stays green and sweet instead of dark and bitter.

How it is made

Basil is pounded with salt, garlic, and pine nuts, then cheese and olive oil are worked in. Heat is not part of the sauce.

Authentic touch. Pound or pulse briefly and stop while it is still bright. Toss it with pasta off the boil. Boiling pesto turns it dull.

How it is used

Toss with pasta and a little pasta water, or stir into minestrone. Do not cook the pesto on a hard boil or the basil turns dull.

Field notes

  • Classic Genoese pesto is pounded, which bruises the basil less harshly than a long blend.
  • Pine nuts are traditional. Walnuts make a different Ligurian sauce, salsa di noci.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups packed fresh basil leaves
  • 1 small garlic clove
  • 2 tablespoons pine nuts
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup extra-virgin olive oil
  • 1/4 cup finely grated Parmesan
  • 2 tablespoons finely grated pecorino

Steps

  1. Pound the garlic and salt to a paste, then the pine nuts.
  2. Add the basil in handfuls and pound to a coarse green paste.
  3. Stir in the cheeses.
  4. Stream in the oil until the sauce is thick and spoonable.
  5. Toss with hot pasta and a splash of pasta water. Do not boil the pesto.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pesto alla Trapanese uses tomato and almonds and is a separate Sicilian sauce.
  • A blender works if you keep it cold and do not overmix the basil into a dark paste.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Vegetables
  • trofie
  • trenette with potatoes and green beans
  • minestrone
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free

Salmoriglio

Olive oil, lemon, oregano, and garlic: bright, herbal, and warm.

10 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Salmoriglio is the Sicilian sauce of olive oil, lemon, oregano, and garlic, often warmed and spooned over grilled swordfish. Oregano, which grows wild on the island, is the herb that makes it Sicilian rather than a plain lemon vinaigrette. It is a finishing sauce for the grill.

Cultural background

Swordfish and other grilled fish are the classic partners. It is also used on grilled meat. The sauce can be whisked warm so the oregano blooms.

Why it tastes this way

Olive oil, lemon, and dried oregano taste bright and herbal. Oregano, common in Sicily, is the herb that marks it. Warming the sauce blooms the oregano onto grilled fish.

How it is made

Lemon, oregano, and garlic are whisked with olive oil, sometimes over a little steam, until fragrant.

Authentic touch. Whisk it and warm it briefly over steam, then spoon it on fish that is still hot. Dried oregano needs that warmth more than fresh basil does.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled swordfish or other grilled fish as it comes off the heat.

Field notes

  • Oregano, not basil, is the herb that marks salmoriglio.
  • It is a grill sauce, made to hit hot fish.

Ingredients

  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon hot water

Steps

  1. Whisk the lemon, oregano, garlic, salt, and hot water.
  2. Whisk in the oil.
  3. Warm the bowl over a pot of simmering water for 2 minutes, whisking.
  4. Taste for salt.
  5. Spoon over grilled fish.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cold whisked version is fine if the fish is very hot.
  • Adding tomato moves it toward a different southern sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Grilling
  • Meat
  • swordfish
  • grilled shrimp
  • grilled vegetables

Chermoula

Cilantro, cumin, paprika, garlic, and lemon: herbaceous and warm.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Chermoula is the Moroccan herb marinade for fish, built on cilantro, garlic, cumin, paprika, oil, and lemon. It is spread on fish before grilling or baking. Similar herb pastes are used across the Maghreb. This is the uncooked fish marinade, not a simmered tomato sauce.

Cultural background

Coastal Moroccan cooking puts it on sardines and whole fish. The paprika and cumin make it redder and warmer than a plain cilantro sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro, cumin, paprika, garlic, and lemon stay raw, so the marinade tastes fresh and warm. Paprika and cumin are why it tastes redder and spicier than a plain herb oil. It is built for fish.

How it is made

Herbs, spices, garlic, lemon, and oil are pounded or blended into a loose paste.

Authentic touch. Blend it coarse and salt it enough to season the fish. Keep a spoonful aside before it touches raw fish if you want sauce at the table.

How it is used

Rub it on fish and grill or bake it. Keep a spoonful that never touched raw fish for the table.

Field notes

  • Chermoula is a marinade and a sauce. Reserve some before it touches raw fish if you want it at the table.
  • Cumin and paprika are as defining as the herbs.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro leaves
  • 1/2 cup parsley leaves
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil

Steps

  1. Blend the herbs, garlic, spices, salt, and lemon.
  2. Stream in the oil.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Rub it on fish and refrigerate up to a few hours.
  5. Grill or bake the fish until cooked through.

Do not serve marinade that has touched raw fish unless you cook it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of tomato makes a saucier baked-fish version.
  • Parsley often joins the cilantro.
