Harissa

Dried chile, garlic, coriander, and caraway: hot, aromatic, and oily.

25 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Harissa is the chile paste of Tunisia, built from dried chiles, garlic, salt, and the spices coriander and caraway, then covered with olive oil. It is a staple condiment and a cooking ingredient across Tunisian meals. Similar pastes exist in Algeria and Libya, but harissa as a named paste is Tunisian.

Cultural background

It is stirred into couscous broth, spread on bread, and used to season stews. Baklouti chile is traditional when you can get it. Other hot dried chiles change the flavor.

Why it tastes this way

Dried chiles, garlic, coriander, and caraway, covered in olive oil, are the taste. Caraway is why it does not taste like a plain chile paste. Oil keeps the paste and carries the flavor.

How it is made

Soaked dried chiles are pounded or blended with garlic, spices, and oil into a thick paste.

Authentic touch. Seed the chiles if you want heat without bitterness, and cover the jar with oil so the top does not dry out.

How it is used

Serve a spoonful with couscous, grilled meat, or eggs, or fry a little in oil at the start of a stew.

Field notes

  • Caraway is a usual harissa spice, which is one way it differs from many other chile pastes.
  • A layer of oil on top is how the paste is kept.

Ingredients

  • 4 ounces dried hot red chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon coriander seeds, toasted
  • 1 teaspoon caraway seeds, toasted
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup olive oil, plus more to cover

Steps

  1. Soak the chiles in hot water 20 minutes. Drain.
  2. Grind the coriander and caraway.
  3. Blend the chiles, garlic, spices, salt, and oil to a paste.
  4. Pack into a jar and cover with a thin layer of oil.
  5. Refrigerate.

Cover the surface with oil and refrigerate. Use within a few weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Rose harissa is a commercial style scented with rose. It is not the plain pantry paste.
  • Libyan filfel chuma is a garlic-heavier chile paste and a different recipe.

Sriracha

Ripe chile, garlic, vinegar, and sugar: hot, tangy, and a little sweet.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Sriracha is named for the coastal town of Si Racha in Thailand, where a garlic-chile sauce for seafood has been made for decades. Thai sriracha is usually hotter and thinner, with visible seeds, than the smooth California sauce later made by Huy Fong Foods. This recipe is a home sauce in the Thai style. It is not a copy of a brand.

Cultural background

It is a seafood and noodle sauce. Sugar and vinegar balance the chile. Garlic is not optional in the Thai sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Ripe red chile, garlic, vinegar, and sugar taste hot and tangy. Thai styles are often looser and seedier than the smooth California bottle. Garlic is part of the flavor, not an extra.

How it is made

Ripe red chiles and garlic simmer with vinegar and sugar, then the sauce is blended and simmered again.

Authentic touch. Simmer, blend, and simmer again. Leave it a day. This is a home sauce in the Thai style, not a brand copy.

How it is used

Spoon it on noodles, seafood, or rice.

Field notes

  • The name is a town in Thailand, Si Racha.
  • Thai sriracha and the famous California bottle are related and not the same sauce.

Ingredients

  • 8 ounces ripe red chiles, stemmed
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 3 tablespoons vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Simmer the chiles, garlic, sugar, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend to a coarse sauce.
  3. Simmer 5 minutes more.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The smooth American bottled style is a related sauce with a different texture.
  • A seed-in texture is closer to many Thai bottles.

Sambal Oelek

Crushed raw chile and salt: bright, hot, and simple.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1/2 cup

History

Sambal oelek is an Indonesian chile paste of crushed fresh red chiles and salt, named for the ulek-ulek, the mortar. It is not cooked and it does not include shrimp paste. Sambal terasi, which does include terasi, is a different sambal.

Cultural background

It is a table sambal and a cooking ingredient across Indonesia. Commercial jars made the name familiar worldwide. The mortar texture, with visible seeds, is the point.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh red chile and salt, crushed, taste bright and hot because they are raw. There is no shrimp paste and no long fry. The seeds and the coarse texture are part of the flavor.

How it is made

Fresh red chiles are pounded with salt until coarse. A little vinegar is a jarred-style option, not required in the simplest paste.

Authentic touch. Pound it. Do not cook it if you want sambal oelek. Cooking it with shrimp paste makes sambal terasi.

How it is used

Serve it beside rice, or fry a spoonful in oil at the start of a stir-fry.

Field notes

  • Oelek refers to the mortar and pestle, not to a chile variety.
  • If it contains shrimp paste, it is no longer plain sambal oelek.

Ingredients

  • 8 fresh red bird’s eye or Fresno chiles, stemmed
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon rice vinegar

Steps

  1. Pound the chiles and salt to a coarse paste.
  2. Stir in the vinegar.
  3. Taste. It should be intensely hot and salty.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Fry a spoonful when you want it in a cooked dish.

Refrigerate and use within a week, or cover with a little vinegar to hold it a bit longer. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sambal terasi adds fermented shrimp paste and is cooked.
  • Sambal matah is a raw Balinese sambal with lemongrass and oil.

Louisiana-Style Hot Sauce

Cayenne and vinegar: thin, sharp, and hot.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 20 · about 1 cup

History

Louisiana hot sauce is aged or simmered cayenne in vinegar and salt. The McIlhenny family on Avery Island began selling Tabasco sauce, made from tabasco peppers, in 1868, and that bottle defined the style for many eaters. This recipe is a weeknight cayenne-vinegar sauce in the Louisiana style. It is not the aged barrel formula of any brand.

Cultural background

It is on the table for oysters, greens, eggs, and beans. Thinness is correct. A thick chile paste is a different family.

Why it tastes this way

Cayenne and vinegar, simmered and strained, taste thin, sharp, and hot. Thickness is not the goal. The vinegar is why it seasons oysters and greens without covering them.

How it is made

Cayenne, garlic, vinegar, and salt simmer, then the sauce is blended and strained.

Authentic touch. Strain it if you want it to pour. Leave it a day in the refrigerator. The flavor settles.

How it is used

Shake it on oysters, greens, eggs, or rice and beans.

Field notes

  • The style is thin and vinegar-forward. Thickness is not a flaw to correct.
  • A brand name on a bottle is not the name of the whole style.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup fresh red cayenne peppers, stemmed
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 3/4 cup distilled white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Simmer the peppers, garlic, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend until smooth.
  3. Strain if you want it pourable from a bottle.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate. The flavor settles after a day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fermented cayenne mash is the commercial path and takes months.
  • Crystal-style sauces lean on cayenne. Tabasco-style sauces use tabasco peppers. Both are Louisiana vinegar sauces.

Pairings and uses

  • raw or grilled oysters

  • scrambled eggs
  • red beans

Peri-Peri Sauce

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, lemon, and oil: hot, sharp, and bright.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Peri-peri, or piri-piri, is the African bird’s eye chile and the sauce made from it. The chile’s Swahili name, pilipili, passed into Portuguese. The sauce of chile, garlic, lemon, and oil or vinegar is associated with Mozambique and Angola and with Portuguese grilled chicken. This is a home sauce in that style, not a bottled brand formula.

Cultural background

Portuguese taverns made peri-peri chicken famous far beyond southern Africa. The chile itself is African. The grill style is a shared Mozambican and Portuguese table.

Why it tastes this way

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, and lemon are the heat and the brightness. Oil or vinegar carries them. The chile is fruity and hot, which is the taste of the sauce on grilled chicken.

How it is made

Chiles and garlic are blended with lemon and oil, then briefly simmered so the raw edge softens.

Authentic touch. Keep some sauce aside before it touches raw chicken if you want a table sauce. Marinade that has held raw meat has to be cooked.

How it is used

Use it as a marinade and a basting sauce for grilled chicken, and put more on the table.

Field notes

  • The chile name traveled from Swahili into Portuguese.
  • The sauce seasons the chicken. It is not a tomato barbecue glaze.

Ingredients

  • 8 fresh red bird’s eye chiles
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar

Steps

  1. Blend the chiles, garlic, lemon, oil, paprika, salt, and vinegar.
  2. Simmer the puree 5 minutes.
  3. Cool.
  4. Use some as a marinade and save some for the table.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A vinegar-heavy version keeps longer and tastes sharper.
  • Yogurt is not part of the classic sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue
  • Marinades
  • Dipping
  • grilled chicken
  • prawns
  • fried potatoes

Nam Pla Prik

Fish sauce, lime, garlic, and chile: salty, sour, and hot.

8 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1/2 cup

History

Nam pla prik is the Thai table sauce of fish sauce with sliced chile, often lime and garlic. It is the condiment for grilled fish, omelets, and rice. It is not a cooked curry and it is not nam jim, which is usually a more mixed dipping sauce.

Cultural background

A Thai meal often puts this bowl on the table so each person can salt and heat their own plate. The fish sauce is the salt.

Why it tastes this way

Fish sauce is the salt, lime is the acid, and raw chile is the heat. It tastes strong because the rice and omelet it seasons are mild. Water is not the point. Concentration is.

How it is made

Chile, garlic, lime, and fish sauce are stirred together. Nothing is cooked.

Authentic touch. Slice the chile and garlic fresh. Taste on the food. It should be a little too strong from a spoon.

How it is used

Serve it with grilled fish, omelets, or rice.

Field notes

  • It is a table sauce, mixed to taste at the meal.
  • Fish sauce here is the seasoning, the way salt is on another table.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 1 tablespoon lime juice
  • 2 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 3 bird’s eye chiles, sliced

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients.
  2. Taste. Add lime if it is too salty.
  3. Serve the same day.
  4. Spoon it over rice or fish.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sliced raw shallot is common.
  • Nam jim seafood adds more palm sugar and is a separate dipping sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood

Buffalo Sauce

Cayenne hot sauce and butter: sharp, hot, and rich.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Buffalo sauce is the coating for the chicken wings associated with the Anchor Bar in Buffalo, New York, which was serving them by 1964. The sauce is cayenne hot sauce and butter. It is not a sweet glaze. Blue cheese or a celery stick is the usual side, not the sauce itself.

Cultural background

The wings are fried naked and tossed in the sauce afterward so the crust stays briefly crisp and the vinegar stays bright. More butter makes it milder.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar-based cayenne sauce is thin and sharp. Butter mellows it and helps it cling. Sugar is not the point, which is why it does not taste like a barbecue glaze.

How it is made

Butter and cayenne hot sauce are melted together.

Authentic touch. Melt butter into hot sauce, do not boil it hard, and toss just-fried wings. Serve them at once.

How it is used

Toss fried wings in the sauce.

Field notes

  • The Anchor Bar’s 1964 wings are the usual starting point of the story.
  • The sauce is butter and hot sauce, tossed on after frying.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup cayenne hot sauce
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • Pinch of black pepper

Steps

  1. Melt the butter with the hot sauce over low heat.
  2. Stir in the garlic powder and pepper.
  3. Toss with hot fried wings.
  4. Serve immediately.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More butter makes a milder sauce.
  • A sweet buffalo sauce is a later twist.

Pairings and uses

  • Wings
  • Dipping
  • Meat
  • chicken wings
  • celery
  • blue cheese dressing

Trinidadian Pepper Sauce

Scotch bonnet or Congo pepper, vinegar, and garlic: very hot and sharp.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 20 · about 1 cup

History

Trinidadian pepper sauce is a vinegar chile sauce built on local hot peppers, often Congo pepper or scotch bonnet, with garlic and sometimes culantro or mustard. Bottled pepper sauces from the island are famous. This is a fresh cooked home version of that style, not a brand formula.

Cultural background

It is on the table for pelau, grilled meat, and doubles in small amounts. The pepper is the ingredient. A mild sauce misses the point.

Why it tastes this way

Caribbean hot peppers taste fruity and extremely hot. Vinegar keeps the sauce thin and sharp so a few drops season a plate. Garlic rounds the fruit.

How it is made

Hot peppers, garlic, and vinegar simmer and are blended with salt.

Authentic touch. Simmer the peppers and garlic in vinegar, then blend. Use it by the drop. Leave the seeds in if you want the full heat.

How it is used

Shake a little onto rice, meat, or doubles.

Field notes

  • Congo pepper and scotch bonnet are the heat.
  • The sauce is thin and used sparingly.

Ingredients

  • 8 scotch bonnet or habanero peppers
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 3/4 cup white vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Simmer the peppers, garlic, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend smooth.
  3. Cool.
  4. Bottle and refrigerate.
  5. Use a few drops at a time.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Culantro, if you can buy it, is a common herbal note.
  • A mustard version is another local style.

Pairings and uses

Zhug

Cilantro, hot chile, garlic, and cardamom: bright, hot, and aromatic.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 10 · about 1 cup

History

Zhug, also spelled skhug, is the Yemeni cilantro and chile paste. Green zhug is herbs and green chile. Red zhug uses hotter red chile. Cardamom, cumin, and garlic are typical. Yemeni Jews brought it into wider Israeli cooking in the twentieth century. It is a condiment, not a cooked salsa.

Cultural background

It is spooned onto falafel, grilled meat, and breakfast eggs. A little is enough. The cardamom is the spice that keeps it from being a generic green hot sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro and hot chile taste bright. Cardamom and cumin are why it does not taste like a generic green hot sauce. It is uncooked, so the herbs stay raw.

How it is made

Cilantro, chile, garlic, and spices are pounded or blended with olive oil and salt.

Authentic touch. Pound or blend it the day you eat it, and use cardamom, not only chile.

How it is used

Serve a spoonful with grilled meat, eggs, or falafel.

Field notes

  • Cardamom is a usual spice in zhug, which is a quick way to tell it from many other green hot sauces.
  • Green and red zhug are both Yemeni. The color follows the chile.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cilantro leaves
  • 3 green chiles
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Blend everything to a coarse paste.
  2. Taste for salt.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red zhug uses dried or fresh red chile and less herb.
  • Sahawiq is the tomato-chile relish and a different Yemeni sauce.

Pairings and uses

Shito

Dried fish, shrimp, chile, and oil cooked down: dark, hot, and savory.

20 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 12 · about 2 cups

History

Shito is a Ghanaian chile sauce of dried fish or dried shrimp, onion, tomato, chile, and a large amount of oil, cooked until dark. Black shito is cooked longer and darker than fresher red versions. It is a staple accompaniment to kenkey, rice, and grilled fish.

Cultural background

Ga and other Ghanaian kitchens treat shito as a cooked condiment that keeps under oil. The dried seafood is the savor, not a garnish.

Why it tastes this way

Dried fish and shrimp, fried long in chile and oil, taste dark and savory because the seafood and the oil concentrate. It is a cooked condiment, not a fresh pepper salsa.

How it is made

Onion, ginger, and dried seafood fry in oil with chile and tomato until the sauce is dark and jammy.

Authentic touch. Cook it slowly until the color deepens and the oil separates. Pale, quickly fried shito tastes raw.

How it is used

Spoon it on rice, kenkey, grilled fish, or boiled yam.

Field notes

  • Black shito gets its color from a long fry, not from a dye.
  • It is an oily chile condiment meant to be eaten by the spoonful, not a thin vinegar hot sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup neutral oil
  • 1 onion, minced
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1/4 cup dried shrimp, ground
  • 1/4 cup dried fish, flaked fine
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 2 tablespoons chile powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Fry the onion and ginger in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the dried shrimp and fish and fry until fragrant.
  3. Stir in the tomato paste and chile powder.
  4. Cook on low, stirring often, until the sauce is dark and jammy, about 30 minutes.
  5. Salt it, cool it, and refrigerate.

Keep it refrigerated. Do not store the oil and seafood at room temperature for days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green shito uses fresh chiles and herbs and is cooked less dark.
  • The dried shrimp can be reduced if you want less funk, and the sauce will be milder in savor.

Pairings and uses

  • Rice
  • Stews
  • Dipping
  • Breakfast
  • kenkey
  • jollof rice
  • grilled tilapia
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Adjika

Fresh chile, garlic, and herbs: hot, salty, and green or red.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Adjika is a chile and herb paste of Abkhazia and western Georgia. Fresh hot chiles, garlic, salt, and herbs such as cilantro and blue fenugreek are pounded together. Red and green versions depend on the chile. It is both a condiment and a seasoning for stews. Dry spice blends sold under the same name are a different product.

Cultural background

A little seasons a pot of beans or a grilled meat. It is salty enough to keep in the refrigerator as a paste. Megrelian cooking uses it heavily.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh chile, garlic, herbs, and salt taste hot and green or red because the paste is raw. Salt is both flavor and a way to keep it. A dry spice mix sold as adjika is a different product.

How it is made

Chiles, garlic, herbs, and salt are pounded or blended to a coarse paste. It is not cooked.

Authentic touch. Pound it coarse and salt it well. Use it by the spoonful. It is a seasoning paste, not a gravy.

How it is used

Serve a small spoonful with grilled meat, or stir a little into beans.

Field notes

  • The fresh paste and the dry spice mix sold as adjika are not the same thing.
  • Salt is part of how the paste is kept.

Ingredients

  • 6 fresh red chiles, stemmed
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 cup cilantro and parsley
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon white wine vinegar

Steps

  1. Blend or pound the chiles, garlic, herbs, and salt to a coarse paste.
  2. Stir in the vinegar.
  3. Taste. It should be very hot and salty.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Use by the spoonful.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green adjika uses unripe chiles and more herbs.
  • A cooked tomato adjika is a related sauce and a different texture.

Nashville Hot Oil

Cayenne and hot oil: very hot, spicy, and staining.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1/2 cup

History

Nashville hot chicken is fried chicken coated in a cayenne-and-oil paste. The style is associated with Prince’s Hot Chicken Shack. The heat sits on the crust. It is an oil sauce, not a vinegar Buffalo sauce.

Cultural background

The chicken is spicy enough that pickles and white bread are the relief. Lard or frying oil carries the cayenne. Sugar and paprika color and round some recipes.

Why it tastes this way

Cayenne bloomed in hot fat tastes louder than cayenne stirred into a cold sauce. The oil stains the crust and keeps the heat on the surface. Vinegar is not the body.

How it is made

Cayenne, paprika, and a little sugar are stirred into very hot oil.

Authentic touch. Stir cayenne into hot frying oil or melted lard, not into cold butter. Brush or toss it on fried chicken immediately.

How it is used

Coat fried chicken and serve with pickles and bread.

Field notes

  • The heat is an oil paste on fried chicken.
  • Prince’s in Nashville is the shop most tied to the style.

Ingredients

  • 1/3 cup hot frying oil or melted lard
  • 2 tablespoons cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon brown sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder

Steps

  1. Stir the spices and sugar into the hot oil.
  2. The paste should be deep red and loose.
  3. Toss or brush it on just-fried chicken.
  4. Serve with pickles.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A brown-sugar version is slightly sweeter.
  • Buffalo sauce is vinegar and butter, a different heat.

Xnipec

Habenero, onion, and sour orange: very hot, sharp, and fresh.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Xnipec is the Yucatecan raw salsa of habanero, onion, and sour orange or lime. The name is often translated as dog’s nose, a joke about the heat making a nose run. It is a table salsa for grilled meat and tacos, not a cooked habanero hot sauce.

Cultural background

Yucatecan meals put this beside cochinita and grilled fish. The onion is soaked so it is sharp but not harsh. The chile is used with respect. A little seasons a lot.

Why it tastes this way

Raw habanero is fruity and extremely hot. Sour orange is sharper than lemon. Soaking the onion pulls out some of its raw sulfur so the chile and citrus can lead.

How it is made

Onion, tomato, habanero, and sour orange are chopped and salted. Nothing is cooked.

Authentic touch. Rinse the diced onion in cold water. Mince the habanero and add it to taste. Use sour orange or lime. Serve it the same day.

How it is used

Spoon a little on tacos or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • The heat is raw habanero. Start with less than you think.
  • Sour orange is the Yucatecan acid.

Ingredients

  • 1 white onion, finely diced and rinsed
  • 2 tomatoes, diced
  • 1 habanero, minced
  • 1/4 cup sour orange or lime juice
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Rinse the onion and drain it.
  2. Toss with tomato, a little habanero, cilantro, juice, and salt.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. Add more habanero only if you want more heat.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More tomato makes it juicier.
  • A cooked habanero salsa is a different, rounder sauce.

Datil Pepper Sauce

Datil chile, vinegar, and garlic: fruity, hot, and sharp.

10 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Datil pepper sauce is the table hot sauce of St. Augustine, Florida, made from the local datil chile, vinegar, and garlic. Minorcans were brought to the New Smyrna colony in 1768 and later lived in St. Augustine. Local accounts credit them with the datil pepper. That origin is traditional rather than documented, so this page does not treat it as proven. The sauce itself is a St. Augustine condiment.

Cultural background

It is shaken on seafood and grilled meat. The datil is fruity and hot, closer to a habanero than to a cayenne. If you cannot buy datils, the sauce will not be the same.

Why it tastes this way

The datil’s fruit and heat are the flavor. Vinegar makes the sauce thin and sharp so it seasons fried fish. Garlic rounds the fruit. A tomato ketchup base would hide the chile.

How it is made

Datil peppers, garlic, and vinegar are simmered and blended.

Authentic touch. Use datil peppers if you can. Simmer them briefly with vinegar and garlic, then blend. A habanero is only a rough stand-in.

How it is used

Shake it on fried fish, shrimp, or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • The datil is a St. Augustine chile. The folk story that Minorcans carried the seed is not treated here as fact.
  • The sauce is a vinegar hot sauce, used by the dash.

Ingredients

  • 8 datil peppers, or habaneros only if datils are unavailable
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 3/4 cup white vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon sugar

Steps

  1. Simmer the peppers, garlic, vinegar, salt, and sugar 8 minutes.
  2. Blend until smooth.
  3. Cool.
  4. Refrigerate a day before judging the heat.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of mustard appears in some St. Augustine bottles.
  • This is not Cuban mojo, which is sour orange and garlic.
