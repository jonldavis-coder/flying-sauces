Marinades

The sauces that go on before the cooking: jerk, mojo, bulgogi, cafreal, adobo.

A marinade seasons the surface and, when it contains acid or salt, changes texture. It is still a sauce: you can boil the leftover marinade if it has touched raw meat, or reserve a portion before it does.

Each recipe says what it is for. Do not reuse a marinade that has held raw meat unless you cook it through.

Bulgogi Marinade

Soy, pear, garlic, sesame, and sugar: savory, sweet, and aromatic.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Bulgogi is Korean grilled sliced beef. The marinade of soy sauce, sugar or pear, garlic, scallion, and sesame is the sauce. The dish’s modern popularity grew in the twentieth century, with roots in earlier seasoned grilled meats. Galbi uses a related, often sweeter marinade for short ribs.

Cultural background

The pear tenderizes and sweetens. The beef is sliced thin and grilled at the table or in a hot pan, then wrapped or served with rice and banchan.

Why it tastes this way

Soy, sugar, garlic, and sesame are the savory-sweet taste. Grated pear adds sweetness and tenderizes thin beef, which is why the meat tastes seasoned through after a short rest.

How it is made

Soy, grated pear, garlic, sugar, and sesame are mixed and left on the meat before a fast grill.

Authentic touch. Slice the beef thin and do not skip the pear or another ripe fruit if you want the familiar sweetness and texture.

How it is used

Grill the marinated beef and serve it with rice and lettuce. Boil unused marinade if it touched raw meat and you want to brush it on at the end.

Field notes

  • The pear is a tenderizer and a sweetener, not only a garnish.
  • Bulgogi is a soy marinade. It is not a chile paste.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup soy sauce
  • 1/2 Asian pear, grated
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 tablespoon toasted sesame oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 scallions, minced
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame seeds
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir the soy, pear, sugar, sesame oil, garlic, scallion, seeds, and pepper.
  2. Mix with thinly sliced beef.
  3. Refrigerate 30 minutes to overnight.
  4. Grill or pan-sear over high heat.
  5. Serve with rice.

Cook marinade that has touched raw beef before you eat it as a sauce.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Galbi marinade is close, often with more sugar and sometimes with kiwi or pear, for short ribs.
  • Gochujang is not part of classic bulgogi. It belongs to other sauces.

Cafreal Marinade

Cilantro, green chile, ginger, and spices: fresh, hot, and herbal.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Cafreal is the Goan green marinade for chicken, associated with Portuguese-Goan cooking. A paste of cilantro, green chile, ginger, garlic, and spices coats the chicken before it is fried or grilled. It is not a tomato curry. Recheado is the red spice paste and a different Goan marinade.

Cultural background

Beach shacks and home kitchens both serve chicken cafreal. The color is green from the herb, not from spinach.

Why it tastes this way

A large amount of cilantro and green chile tastes fresh and hot. The paste cooks on the chicken, so the herb becomes a crust instead of a gravy. It is not a tomato curry.

How it is made

Cilantro and spices are blended into a paste that coats chicken before cooking.

Authentic touch. Coat the chicken thickly and let it sit. Grill or fry until the paste browns and the meat is cooked through.

How it is used

Marinate chicken, then grill or pan-fry it until cooked through.

Field notes

  • Cafreal is green because of cilantro, not food coloring.
  • It is a marinade that becomes the crust, not a gravy you pour on after.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro
  • 4 green chiles
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon ginger
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon lime juice
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend everything into a paste.
  2. Coat chicken pieces and refrigerate at least 1 hour.
  3. Grill or pan-fry until the chicken is cooked through.
  4. Scrape the browned paste back over the meat.
  5. Serve with lime.

Cook the chicken through. Do not eat the raw marinade.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Recheado is the red vinegar paste for fish.
  • A spoon of lime in the paste brightens it.

Char Siu Sauce

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice: sweet, savory, and glossy.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Char siu is Cantonese roasted pork, lacquered with a sweet soy marinade of hoisin or fermented bean sauce, sugar or honey, and five-spice. The red color of shop char siu often comes from fermented red bean curd or food coloring. This marinade uses hoisin and honey and does not rely on dye.

Cultural background

The pork is roasted and the marinade becomes the glaze. It is eaten with rice or in noodle soups. The sugar burns if it meets high heat too early.

Why it tastes this way

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice taste sweet and savory. The sugar lacquers the pork. Shop-window red is often color, not the honey. High heat too early burns the sugar.

How it is made

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice coat pork, which is roasted and basted.

Authentic touch. Roast first and baste near the end. Boil any marinade that touched raw pork before you use it as a glaze.

How it is used

Slice the roasted pork and serve it with rice. Boil any marinade that touched raw meat before basting at the end.

Field notes

  • Char siu means fork roast, from the way the meat was hung.
  • The red of many shop windows is color, not the honey.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons hoisin sauce
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon honey
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 teaspoon rice wine
  • 1 garlic clove, minced

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients.
  2. Coat pork shoulder or loin and refrigerate at least 2 hours.
  3. Roast until cooked through, basting near the end.
  4. Rest the meat before slicing.
  5. Serve with rice.

Boil marinade that has touched raw pork before you brush it on cooked meat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red fermented bean curd is the traditional color and savor in many shop recipes.
  • Honey can be swapped for maltose, which is stickier.

Chermoula

Cilantro, cumin, paprika, garlic, and lemon: herbaceous and warm.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Chermoula is the Moroccan herb marinade for fish, built on cilantro, garlic, cumin, paprika, oil, and lemon. It is spread on fish before grilling or baking. Similar herb pastes are used across the Maghreb. This is the uncooked fish marinade, not a simmered tomato sauce.

Cultural background

Coastal Moroccan cooking puts it on sardines and whole fish. The paprika and cumin make it redder and warmer than a plain cilantro sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro, cumin, paprika, garlic, and lemon stay raw, so the marinade tastes fresh and warm. Paprika and cumin are why it tastes redder and spicier than a plain herb oil. It is built for fish.

How it is made

Herbs, spices, garlic, lemon, and oil are pounded or blended into a loose paste.

Authentic touch. Blend it coarse and salt it enough to season the fish. Keep a spoonful aside before it touches raw fish if you want sauce at the table.

How it is used

Rub it on fish and grill or bake it. Keep a spoonful that never touched raw fish for the table.

Field notes

  • Chermoula is a marinade and a sauce. Reserve some before it touches raw fish if you want it at the table.
  • Cumin and paprika are as defining as the herbs.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro leaves
  • 1/2 cup parsley leaves
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil

Steps

  1. Blend the herbs, garlic, spices, salt, and lemon.
  2. Stream in the oil.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Rub it on fish and refrigerate up to a few hours.
  5. Grill or bake the fish until cooked through.

Do not serve marinade that has touched raw fish unless you cook it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of tomato makes a saucier baked-fish version.
  • Parsley often joins the cilantro.

Chipotle in Adobo

Smoked jalapeño, vinegar, and tomato: smoky, sour, and hot.

10 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Chipotle is a smoked ripe jalapeño. Packed in adobo, it sits in a sauce of vinegar, tomato, garlic, and spices. Mexican adobo here means that chile sauce, not Filipino vinegar-soy adobo. Canned chipotles are the pantry form of this preserve.

Cultural background

A spoon of the sauce seasons mayo, beans, and braises. The smoke is from the chile, so liquid smoke is unnecessary if the chipotles are real.

Why it tastes this way

Smoke-drying the jalapeño is why chipotle tastes smoky rather than green and fresh. Vinegar and tomato make a tart sauce around the fruit. The seeds and veins carry extra heat.

How it is made

Smoked chiles simmer in vinegar, tomato, garlic, and spices until soft.

Authentic touch. Simmer dried chipotles or use smoked jalapeños until they are soft in the vinegar-tomato sauce. Taste before adding salt.

How it is used

Use the chiles and the sauce in marinades, beans, or a spoon on tacos.

Field notes

  • Chipotle means a smoked chile, usually jalapeño.
  • Mexican adobo and Filipino adobo are not the same word in the kitchen.

Ingredients

  • 8 dried chipotle chiles or canned smoked jalapeños
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/4 cup vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil

Steps

  1. Simmer the chiles in water 15 minutes if they are dry.
  2. Blend half of them with vinegar, tomato, garlic, oregano, salt, and oil.
  3. Return the puree and whole chiles to the pot and simmer 10 minutes.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Filipino adobo is a different sauce.
  • Morita chiles are a fruitier smoked jalapeño.

Filipino Adobo Sauce

Vinegar, soy, garlic, and bay: sour, salty, and savory.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · about 2 cups with the chicken

History

Filipino adobo is a braise of vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, pepper, and bay. The Spanish word was applied to an older local vinegar cooking method. It is not Mexican adobo, which is a chile sauce. The sauce is the reduced braising liquid, served with the meat and rice.

Cultural background

Every region has a ratio. Some are more vinegary, some sweeter, some use coconut milk. This is the soy-and-vinegar chicken version.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and soy taste sour and salty. Garlic and bay perfume the reduced liquid. Filipino adobo is that vinegar braise, not Mexican chile adobo. The sauce should still taste sour after it reduces.

How it is made

Chicken simmers in vinegar, soy, and garlic until the liquid reduces to a sauce.

Authentic touch. Simmer until the liquid thickens and tastes sharp. If the sourness boils off, add a splash of vinegar back.

How it is used

Serve the chicken and the reduced sauce over rice.

Field notes

  • Filipino adobo and Mexican adobo share a name and not a recipe.
  • The vinegar should still be noticeable after the sauce reduces.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound chicken thighs
  • 1/2 cup vinegar
  • 1/4 cup soy sauce
  • 6 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Brown the chicken in the oil.
  2. Add the vinegar, soy, garlic, bay, pepper, and water.
  3. Simmer until the chicken is cooked and the sauce reduces, about 30 minutes.
  4. The sauce should taste sour and salty.
  5. Serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A coconut-milk adobo is a Bicol-leaning variation.
  • Mexican chile adobo is a different sauce with the same Spanish word.

Haitian Epis

Bell pepper, garlic, thyme, and scallion: savory and herbal.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Epis is the Haitian seasoning base of pepper, garlic, scallion, thyme, and often parsley or culantro, blended and used to start stews and marinades. It plays a role like a sofrito. Cooks keep it on hand. The blend is not one fixed recipe.

Cultural background

Rice, beans, chicken, and griot often start with epis fried in oil. That fry is when the raw blend becomes the flavor of the pot.

Why it tastes this way

A concentrated herb and allium paste tastes savory once it fries in oil. Bell pepper sweetens it. Thyme is the herbal note. Using it raw as a table sauce is not the usual path.

How it is made

Pepper, garlic, herbs, and scallion are blended, then fried as the base of a dish.

Authentic touch. Blend it, then fry a spoonful in oil at the start of a stew until it smells sweet. That fried spoonful seasons the whole pot.

How it is used

Use it to start rice and stews or as a marinade that you then cook.

Field notes

  • Epis is a cooking base, like a sofrito.
  • Frying it is what turns the raw blend into the stew’s flavor.

Ingredients

  • 1 green bell pepper
  • 1 onion
  • 6 scallions
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 cup parsley
  • 6 sprigs thyme
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend everything except a spare spoon of oil, adding a splash of water if needed.
  2. Store it cold.
  3. When you cook, fry a few tablespoons in oil until fragrant.
  4. Build the stew on that base.
  5. Use the jar within a few days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Trinidadian green seasoning is a close cousin.
  • A Scotch bonnet in the blend makes it hot.

Hoisin Sauce

Fermented soybean, sugar, vinegar, and five-spice: sweet, salty, and aromatic.

5 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Hoisin is a Cantonese sauce based on fermented soybean paste, sweetened and spiced. Commercial jars are the usual pantry form. This recipe seasons fermented bean paste into a home hoisin-style sauce. It does not recreate the factory ferment. It is the sauce for Peking duck pancakes in many kitchens, alongside sweet bean sauce.

Cultural background

It is a glaze, a dip, and a stir-fry seasoning. Five-spice and sesame are the perfume. The sweetness is intentional.

Why it tastes this way

Fermented beans taste savory. Sugar and vinegar make the sauce sweet and sharp. Five-spice, especially star anise, is the aroma people recognize. A plain soy reduction lacks the bean ferment.

How it is made

Bean paste, sugar, vinegar, garlic, and five-spice simmer into a thick sauce.

Authentic touch. Start with fermented bean paste or soybean paste, not only soy sauce. Simmer it with sugar, vinegar, and five-spice until thick.

How it is used

Use it as a dip for pancakes with roast duck, or as a glaze.

Field notes

  • Hoisin means seafood in the name’s words and is not a fish sauce.
  • The base is fermented bean paste.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup fermented soybean paste
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons honey or sugar
  • 1 tablespoon rice vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon sesame oil
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until thick, about 8 minutes.
  2. Cool.
  3. Use as a dip or glaze.
  4. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sweet bean sauce, tianmianjiang, is the other classic pancake sauce.
  • This simmer is a kitchen version of a fermented commercial sauce.

Jerk Marinade

Scotch bonnet, allspice, thyme, and scallion: hot, aromatic, and smoky-sweet.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Jerk is the Jamaican method of seasoning and smoke-grilling meat, historically associated with the Maroons. Allspice, called pimento in Jamaica, and scotch bonnet chile define it, along with thyme and scallion. The wet marinade is one way the seasoning is applied. A dry rub is the other.

Cultural background

Roadside jerk pits cook pork and chicken over pimento wood. Home cooks use the same seasoning under a grill. Soy sauce appears in many modern marinades and is not the oldest layer of the technique.

Why it tastes this way

Scotch bonnet, allspice, thyme, and scallion are the taste. Allspice, called pimento in Jamaica, is the spice that makes it jerk rather than a generic chile paste. The wet paste seasons the surface before smoke.

How it is made

Scotch bonnet, scallion, thyme, allspice, and acid are blended into a wet paste that coats the meat before grilling.

Authentic touch. Cut slits in the meat so the paste gets under the surface. Reserve some paste before it touches raw meat if you want a table sauce.

How it is used

Rub it on chicken or pork and grill it. Serve with more sauce only if that portion never touched raw meat.

Field notes

  • Allspice berry, not a mix of spices, is the pimento in jerk.
  • Scotch bonnet is traditional. A milder chile makes a different dish.

Ingredients

  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • 2 scotch bonnet chiles
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 tablespoon ground allspice
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar

Steps

  1. Blend all the ingredients to a coarse paste.
  2. Cut slits in chicken or pork and rub the paste in.
  3. Refrigerate at least 2 hours.
  4. Grill until the meat is cooked through.
  5. Boil any leftover marinade before you consider serving it.

Do not eat marinade that has touched raw meat unless you cook it through.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dry jerk rub skips the liquid and uses the same spices.
  • Reserve a cup of marinade before it touches raw meat if you want a table sauce.

  • Barbecue

Kecap Manis

Sweet soy, garlic, and star anise: thick, salty, and sweet.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Kecap manis is Indonesian sweet soy sauce, a pillar of Javanese cooking. Commercial kecap is fermented soy sweetened with palm sugar. This recipe is the home reduction of soy sauce with palm sugar and spices. It is a kitchen version of the flavor, not a from-scratch soy fermentation.

Cultural background

It glazes satay, nasi goreng, and mie goreng. The thickness is what lets it coat noodles. Salty soy alone will not do that job.

Why it tastes this way

Palm sugar cooked into soy tastes thick and sweet-salty. Reduction is why it coats noodles. This home pot starts from soy sauce. It is not a from-scratch ferment.

How it is made

Soy sauce, palm sugar, garlic, and a spice simmer until syrupy.

Authentic touch. Simmer until syrupy and cool it. It thickens as it cools. Use it as a glaze, not by the cup.

How it is used

Brush it on satay or toss it with fried rice and noodles.

Field notes

  • Manis means sweet. Kecap asin is the salty soy.
  • A home reduction starts from soy sauce. It does not replace the ferment.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup soy sauce
  • 1/2 cup palm or brown sugar
  • 2 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 1 star anise
  • 1/4 cup water

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until syrupy, about 10 minutes.
  2. Remove the garlic and star anise.
  3. Cool. It will thicken.
  4. Use it to glaze satay or season fried rice.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The fermented commercial sauce is the pantry original. This is a reduction.
  • Star anise is a common spice in home reductions and not in every bottle.

Mojo Criollo

Sour orange, garlic, cumin, and oil: sharp, garlicky, and savory.

15 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Mojo criollo is the Cuban garlic and sour-orange sauce, also called Cuban mojo. It goes on yuca and is the marinade for roast pork. Sour orange, naranja agria, is the traditional acid. Lime and orange together are the usual stand-in when sour oranges are scarce. It is not the Spanish word for a gravy in general, and it is not the mojito drink.

Cultural background

Lechón and Christmas pork in Cuban cooking are tied to mojo. The boiled yuca on the table gets the same sauce, often heated so the garlic softens.

Why it tastes this way

Sour orange and a lot of garlic are the taste. Sour orange is sharper than sweet orange. Cumin and oil round it. The sauce is both a pork marinade and a spooning sauce for yuca.

How it is made

Garlic and cumin are pounded, mixed with sour orange juice, and combined with oil. A short warm-up blooms the garlic for the table version.

Authentic touch. If you cannot buy sour oranges, use equal parts lime and orange juice. Warm the oil and pour it over the garlic so the garlic softens without burning.

How it is used

Marinate pork in it, and spoon more over boiled yuca.

Field notes

  • Mojo the sauce and mojito the drink share a word history around moisture and sauce, and they are not the same recipe.
  • Sour orange is the acid that makes the marinade taste right.

Ingredients

  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/3 cup sour orange juice, or half lime and half orange
  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon oregano

Steps

  1. Mash the garlic with the salt and cumin.
  2. Stir in the juice and oregano.
  3. Warm the oil, pour it over the garlic, and stir.
  4. Use it as a pork marinade for several hours.
  5. Spoon more over boiled yuca at the table.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Puerto Rican mojito isleño adds tomato, pepper, and olives for fish.
  • Equal parts lime and sweet orange approximate sour orange.

Peri-Peri Sauce

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, lemon, and oil: hot, sharp, and bright.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Peri-peri, or piri-piri, is the African bird’s eye chile and the sauce made from it. The chile’s Swahili name, pilipili, passed into Portuguese. The sauce of chile, garlic, lemon, and oil or vinegar is associated with Mozambique and Angola and with Portuguese grilled chicken. This is a home sauce in that style, not a bottled brand formula.

Cultural background

Portuguese taverns made peri-peri chicken famous far beyond southern Africa. The chile itself is African. The grill style is a shared Mozambican and Portuguese table.

Why it tastes this way

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, and lemon are the heat and the brightness. Oil or vinegar carries them. The chile is fruity and hot, which is the taste of the sauce on grilled chicken.

How it is made

Chiles and garlic are blended with lemon and oil, then briefly simmered so the raw edge softens.

Authentic touch. Keep some sauce aside before it touches raw chicken if you want a table sauce. Marinade that has held raw meat has to be cooked.

How it is used

Use it as a marinade and a basting sauce for grilled chicken, and put more on the table.

Field notes

  • The chile name traveled from Swahili into Portuguese.
  • The sauce seasons the chicken. It is not a tomato barbecue glaze.

Ingredients

  • 8 fresh red bird’s eye chiles
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar

Steps

  1. Blend the chiles, garlic, lemon, oil, paprika, salt, and vinegar.
  2. Simmer the puree 5 minutes.
  3. Cool.
  4. Use some as a marinade and save some for the table.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A vinegar-heavy version keeps longer and tastes sharper.
  • Yogurt is not part of the classic sauce.

Puerto Rican Sofrito

Ají dulce, pepper, cilantro, and culantro: sweet, herbal, and savory.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Puerto Rican sofrito is a blended base of onion, garlic, sweet pepper, ají dulce, cilantro, and culantro, often with tomato and oil. It starts beans, rice, and stews. Spanish sofrito is a cooked tomato-onion base and is not the same blend. Ají dulce is aromatic and not very hot.

Cultural background

A freezer jar of sofrito is how many kitchens start dinner. Culantro, recao, tastes stronger than cilantro. Both appear in many blends. Frying the raw blend is the step that makes the pot taste right.

Why it tastes this way

Sweet peppers and ají dulce taste fragrant rather than hot. Culantro and cilantro are herbal. Fried in oil with tomato, the blend tastes savory and sweet, which is the base under rice and beans.

How it is made

Peppers, onion, garlic, and herbs are blended, then fried as the start of a dish.

Authentic touch. Blend it raw, then fry a few spoonfuls in oil until the raw smell is gone before you add rice or beans. If you cannot find ají dulce, a bit of bell pepper is milder and not the same.

How it is used

Use it as the base of rice, beans, and stews.

Field notes

  • Ají dulce is fragrant, not a habanero’s heat.
  • Puerto Rican sofrito and Spanish sofrito share a name and not a recipe.

Ingredients

  • 1 green bell pepper
  • 1 onion
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 cup cilantro
  • 1/2 cup culantro if available, or more cilantro
  • 1 tomato
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend the vegetables, herbs, and salt.
  2. Store cold.
  3. Fry a portion in oil until fragrant before building a rice or bean pot.
  4. Use the jar within a few days.
  5. Taste the finished dish for salt.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cuban sofrito leans on more tomato and different peppers.
  • A spoon of tomato sauce in the fry is common.

Recado Rojo

Annatto, garlic, oregano, and sour orange: earthy, sharp, and savory.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Recado rojo is the Yucatecan achiote paste used for cochinita pibil and other pit or oven meats. Annatto seeds dye it red and taste earthy. Sour orange, garlic, and oregano are the rest. It is not a tomato marinade.

Cultural background

Banana leaves wrap pibil meats and add a green aroma when they are used. The paste is the seasoning. Sour orange is the Yucatán acid, as in Cuban mojo, and lime plus orange is the stand-in.

Why it tastes this way

Annatto tastes earthy and colors the meat. Sour orange keeps it sharp. Oregano and garlic perfume the fat. Without the citrus, achiote paste tastes flat and dusty.

How it is made

Annatto, garlic, spices, and sour orange are blended into a paste that coats pork or chicken.

Authentic touch. Grind or blend the annatto with garlic and sour orange into a wet paste. Rub it on the meat and marinate. If you wrap in banana leaf, warm the leaf so it does not crack.

How it is used

Slow-cook the marinated meat until tender and serve it with tortillas and pickled onion.

Field notes

  • The red is annatto, not paprika alone.
  • Sour orange is the traditional acid of the Yucatán paste.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons annatto seeds, ground, or 1/4 cup achiote paste
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/2 cup sour orange juice or half lime and half orange
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend all the ingredients into a wet paste.
  2. Rub it over pork shoulder.
  3. Refrigerate at least 4 hours.
  4. Cook covered until tender.
  5. Serve with tortillas.

Cook the meat through. Do not eat the raw paste.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Recado blanco and recado negro are different Yucatecan pastes.
  • Equal parts lime and orange approximate sour orange.

Trinidadian Green Seasoning

Culantro or cilantro, thyme, garlic, and pepper: herbal and savory.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Green seasoning is the Trinidadian herb marinade of culantro or bandhania, thyme, garlic, scallion, and pepper, blended and used on meat before it is stewed or grilled. Culantro, when you can get it, tastes stronger than cilantro. The blend is a seasoning base, not a table salsa.

Cultural background

Home cooks keep a jar and season meats with it the night before. Thyme and garlic are constant. The herb changes with the market.

Why it tastes this way

A large amount of allium and herb, salted, tastes savory and green. It is blended fine so it clings to meat. Culantro is punchier than cilantro if you use it.

How it is made

Herbs, garlic, scallion, thyme, and pepper are blended with a little water and salt.

Authentic touch. Blend it fine, salt it, and rub it on meat for several hours before cooking. If you only have cilantro, the seasoning will be milder.

How it is used

Use it as a marinade, then cook the meat.

Field notes

  • Culantro and cilantro are different herbs. Culantro is the one many Trinidadian recipes mean.
  • It is a marinade base, used before the pot, not only at the table.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro or culantro
  • 4 scallions
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 6 sprigs thyme, leaves only
  • 1 bell pepper
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Blend everything to a coarse paste.
  2. Rub it on meat.
  3. Refrigerate the meat several hours.
  4. Cook the meat through.
  5. Keep unused seasoning cold and use it within a few days.

Cook any meat it has touched. Do not eat the used marinade raw.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Haitian epis is a related herb base.
  • A chile in the blend makes it a hotter marinade.

Worcestershire Sauce

Anchovy, vinegar, molasses, and tamarind: salty, sour, and deep.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 24 · about 1 cup

History

Worcestershire sauce was first sold by the chemists Lea and Perrins in Worcester in the 1830s. It is a fermented anchovy and vegetable sauce with vinegar, molasses, tamarind, and spices. The exact commercial formula is a trade secret. Tales about a forgotten barrel are company lore and are not repeated here as fact. This recipe is a simmered kitchen version of the flavor, not the aged original.

Cultural background

British cooking uses it in Welsh rarebit and with grilled meats. It also became a standard seasoning in cocktails and in kitchens far from Worcester. It is not a vegan sauce, because of the anchovy.

Why it tastes this way

Anchovy, vinegar, molasses, and tamarind taste salty, sour, and deep. The commercial sauce is fermented. This simmer captures the same family of flavors without the long age, so it will not taste identical to the bottle.

How it is made

Anchovy, onion, vinegar, molasses, tamarind, and spices simmer, then the sauce is strained. The result is a pantry approximation.

Authentic touch. Use it by the teaspoon. Reduce the simmered sauce until it is sharp. A weak mix tastes like sweet vinegar.

How it is used

Splash it into meat sauces, rarebit, or a marinade. A teaspoon is usually enough.

Field notes

  • Lea and Perrins of Worcester began selling the sauce in the 1830s.
  • The simmered home version is not the fermented factory sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup white vinegar
  • 1/4 cup molasses
  • 2 tablespoons tamarind paste
  • 4 anchovy fillets
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 garlic clove
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground clove
  • 1/4 teaspoon chili powder
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients 25 minutes.
  2. Blend and strain.
  3. Cool.
  4. Taste. It should be sharply savory, not sweet.
  5. Refrigerate. This is a kitchen version, not aged Worcestershire.

Refrigerate the strained sauce and use it within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The commercial sauce is fermented and aged. This simmered version is only a kitchen echo.
  • Soy sauce and anchovy together can stand in for a splash, and that is a shortcut, not this recipe.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Marinades
  • grilled steak
  • Welsh rarebit
  • Bloody Mary
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Yassa

Onion, lemon or lime, and chile: sharp, sweet, and savory.

20 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups with the chicken

History

Yassa is the Senegalese onion and citrus marinade and sauce, especially chicken yassa, widely associated with the Casamance region. Meat or fish marinates in lemon, onion, mustard or chile, and oil, then the onions cook down into the sauce. It is served with rice.

Cultural background

The pile of onion is the sauce, not a garnish. Lime or lemon has to be plentiful. Mustard appears in many Dakar versions.

Why it tastes this way

A large amount of onion cooked in lemon, mustard, and chile tastes sweet and sharp. The onion is the body of the sauce. A small handful will not taste like yassa.

How it is made

Chicken marinates in lemon, onion, chile, and mustard, then the onions cook in oil until soft and the chicken simmers in that sauce.

Authentic touch. Marinate in lemon and onion, then cook those same onions down until they are soft. Do not discard them.

How it is used

Serve the chicken and the onion sauce over rice.

Field notes

  • The onions are the body of the sauce. A small amount will not taste like yassa.
  • Casamance is the region most often named with chicken yassa.

Ingredients

  • 2 onions, sliced
  • 1/4 cup lemon juice
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 tablespoon Dijon mustard
  • 1 scotch bonnet, seeded and minced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 pound chicken pieces
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Mix the onion, lemon, oil, mustard, chile, and salt. Coat the chicken and refrigerate 1 hour.
  2. Remove the chicken and brown it in a pan. Set it aside.
  3. Cook the onion marinade until the onion is soft and sweet.
  4. Return the chicken, add the water, and simmer until cooked through.
  5. Serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fish yassa uses the same onion sauce.
  • A spoon of mustard is common and not in every pot.
