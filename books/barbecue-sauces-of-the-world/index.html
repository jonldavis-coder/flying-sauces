Barbecue Sauces of the World

Vinegar, mustard, molasses, and white sauce. A booklet of the sauces people brush, dip, and serve with fire-cooked food.

Barbecue sauce is not one recipe. In eastern North Carolina it can be vinegar and pepper. In Kansas City it is thick, sweet, and red. In Alabama it is mayonnaise and vinegar. Elsewhere, char siu glaze, jerk marinade, and peri-peri do the same job for grilled and roasted meat.

This book gathers field-guide sauces whose main use is barbecue or the grill. Print it as one booklet. Each recipe starts on its own page.

Alabama White Sauce

Mayonnaise, vinegar, and black pepper: tangy, peppery, and pale.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Alabama white sauce is credited to Bob Gibson of Decatur, who was serving barbecue in the 1920s. It is a mayonnaise and vinegar sauce, heavily peppered, used on smoked chicken. The attribution to Gibson’s barbecue in Decatur is the standard account in barbecue histories.

Cultural background

It is a north Alabama sauce, especially for chicken, and it now shows up on pork and on the table as a dip. It is not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise carries pepper and vinegar, so the sauce is pale, tangy, and peppery rather than red and sweet. It was built for smoked chicken, whose mild meat can take a sharp white sauce.

How it is made

Mayonnaise is loosened with vinegar and seasoned with pepper, salt, and a little sugar.

Authentic touch. Use plenty of black pepper and keep it cold. The pepper is the heat. Chile is optional.

How it is used

Toss it with smoked chicken, or serve it as a dip for smoked meats.

Field notes

  • The sauce is white because the base is mayonnaise, not because it is mild in flavor.
  • It was built for chicken in a state better known, from the outside, for pork and tomato sauces.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 1/3 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne

Steps

  1. Whisk the mayonnaise and vinegar until smooth.
  2. Stir in the pepper, salt, sugar, and cayenne.
  3. Rest it 15 minutes so the pepper blooms.
  4. Taste and add vinegar if you want it sharper.
  5. Refrigerate.

This is a mayonnaise sauce. Keep it cold.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Horseradish is a common addition and not required.
  • Use a thin homemade mayonnaise if you want more acid.

American Yellow Mustard

Vinegar and mustard seed, colored with turmeric: sharp and bright.

10 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Bright yellow prepared mustard became an American condiment in the early twentieth century. French’s yellow mustard, introduced at the 1904 St. Louis World’s Fair, is the commercial example most often cited. The color comes from turmeric. The heat is from white or yellow mustard seed, softened by vinegar.

Cultural background

It is the mustard of hot dogs and ballpark sausages, milder and brighter than Dijon or English powder mustard.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar tames mustard seed and turmeric dyes it yellow. The heat is the seed, softened by acid, which is why it is milder than a fresh English mustard mixed with water.

How it is made

Mustard powder, vinegar, water, and turmeric are stirred and left to mellow. A short rest is part of the recipe.

Authentic touch. Let it rest after cooking. Mustard tastes harsh the minute it is mixed and milder the next day.

How it is used

Spread it on hot dogs and ham sandwiches, or whisk it into a barbecue mop.

Field notes

  • The yellow color is turmeric, not a hotter chile.
  • Prepared yellow mustard’s American popularity is tied to the early 1900s, not to ancient Rome.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup yellow mustard powder
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/4 cup white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Pinch of sugar

Steps

  1. Whisk the mustard powder and water until smooth.
  2. Add the vinegar, turmeric, salt, and sugar.
  3. Warm it 3 minutes over low heat, whisking. Do not boil hard.
  4. Cool. It will thicken and mellow.
  5. Refrigerate at least a few hours before serving.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Dijon is a wine-mustard from France and is sharper.
  • Honey mustard sweetens this base.

Cape Malay Fruit Chutney

Dried fruit, vinegar, ginger, and chile: sweet, sour, and warm.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3 cups

History

Cape Malay chutneys and blatjangs grew from the cooking of Muslim communities at the Cape, with roots in Indonesian and South Asian preserves and local fruit. Dried fruit, vinegar, onion, ginger, and chile are typical. Mrs H.S. Ball’s chutney, first sold in the early twentieth century, became the famous commercial jar. This is a home fruit chutney in that Cape style, not a copy of a brand.

Cultural background

It is eaten with curry, bobotie, and cold meat. The texture should be soft and sticky. This is a refrigerator chutney, not a canning recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Dried fruit, vinegar, ginger, and chile taste sweet and sharp because Cape cooks adapted preserve methods to local fruit and spice. The long simmer concentrates both the fruit and the vinegar.

How it is made

Dried apricots, onion, vinegar, sugar, ginger, and chile simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook it until sticky. A thin syrup tastes like compote, not chutney. Refrigerate it. This is not a canning recipe.

How it is used

Serve it with curries, bobotie, or a cheese sandwich.

Field notes

  • Cape Malay chutney is part of a cooking tradition shaped at the Cape, not a direct copy of one Indian recipe.
  • The famous commercial fruit chutney is a brand. This is a home pot.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups dried apricots, chopped
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup water

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until the fruit is soft and the pot is thick, about 35 minutes.
  2. Stir often near the end so it does not catch.
  3. Cool.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Serve with curry or cold meat.

Refrigerate and use within a month. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Raisins or dates can join the apricots.
  • A hotter chile is a household choice.

Chakalaka

Tomato, baked beans, pepper, and curry spice: tangy, sweet, and hot.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 4 cups

History

Chakalaka is a South African vegetable relish associated with township cooking and with braais. It typically includes onion, pepper, tomato, carrot, baked beans, and curry spices. It does not have a single inventor on record. Commercial jars made it a national pantry item.

Cultural background

It is served cold or warm beside pap, braaied meat, and bread. The spice level is a household choice.

Why it tastes this way

Baked beans, tomato, pepper, and curry spices taste like a braai relish because the vegetables are cooked down with the spices, not left raw. Curry powder is the spice trail in this South African pot.

How it is made

Aromatics and vegetables fry with curry powder, tomato, and beans until thick.

Authentic touch. Simmer until thick enough to sit on pap or bread. A watery pot tastes stewed, not like chakalaka.

How it is used

Spoon it next to grilled meat, pap, or onto bread.

Field notes

  • Chakalaka is a relish, usually eaten beside starch and meat rather than as a glaze.
  • Baked beans are a usual ingredient, which surprises people who expect only fresh vegetables.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 1 carrot, grated
  • 1 tablespoon curry powder
  • 1 can (14 ounces) diced tomatoes
  • 1 can (14 ounces) baked beans
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Fry the onion, pepper, and carrot in the oil until soft.
  2. Stir in the curry powder and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes and beans.
  4. Simmer 20 minutes until thick.
  5. Salt and cool.

Refrigerate and eat within five days. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add cabbage or apple.
  • A hotter chakalaka uses fresh bird’s eye chile.

Char Siu Sauce

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice: sweet, savory, and glossy.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Char siu is Cantonese roasted pork, lacquered with a sweet soy marinade of hoisin or fermented bean sauce, sugar or honey, and five-spice. The red color of shop char siu often comes from fermented red bean curd or food coloring. This marinade uses hoisin and honey and does not rely on dye.

Cultural background

The pork is roasted and the marinade becomes the glaze. It is eaten with rice or in noodle soups. The sugar burns if it meets high heat too early.

Why it tastes this way

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice taste sweet and savory. The sugar lacquers the pork. Shop-window red is often color, not the honey. High heat too early burns the sugar.

How it is made

Hoisin, soy, honey, and five-spice coat pork, which is roasted and basted.

Authentic touch. Roast first and baste near the end. Boil any marinade that touched raw pork before you use it as a glaze.

How it is used

Slice the roasted pork and serve it with rice. Boil any marinade that touched raw meat before basting at the end.

Field notes

  • Char siu means fork roast, from the way the meat was hung.
  • The red of many shop windows is color, not the honey.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons hoisin sauce
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon honey
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 teaspoon rice wine
  • 1 garlic clove, minced

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients.
  2. Coat pork shoulder or loin and refrigerate at least 2 hours.
  3. Roast until cooked through, basting near the end.
  4. Rest the meat before slicing.
  5. Serve with rice.

Boil marinade that has touched raw pork before you brush it on cooked meat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red fermented bean curd is the traditional color and savor in many shop recipes.
  • Honey can be swapped for maltose, which is stickier.

Chimichurri

Parsley, garlic, oregano, vinegar, and olive oil: green, sharp, and herbal.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Chimichurri is the uncooked herb sauce of Argentina and Uruguay, served with grilled beef. Folk etymologies for the name, including stories about English or Irish speakers, are not reliable and are left out. The sauce itself is a documented everyday condiment of asado.

Cultural background

It is spooned over steak at the table and sometimes used as a marinade. Red chimichurri, with tomato and more chile, is a variation. Chilean pebre is a different salsa.

Why it tastes this way

Parsley, garlic, oregano, vinegar, and oil stay raw, so the sauce tastes fresh and sharp against charred beef. It is a finishing sauce for asado, not a cooked gravy.

How it is made

Parsley, garlic, oregano, vinegar, and oil are chopped and stirred, then rested so the garlic softens.

Authentic touch. Chop by hand if you can. A long blend turns the parsley dark and bitter.

How it is used

Spoon it on grilled steak, chorizo, or vegetables. It is a finishing sauce.

Field notes

  • Argentina and Uruguay both claim chimichurri as everyday asado sauce.
  • Name-origin stories are too shaky to repeat as fact.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup finely chopped parsley
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/2 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 3 tablespoons red wine vinegar
  • 1/2 cup olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir the parsley, garlic, oregano, and chile.
  2. Add the vinegar and salt.
  3. Stir in the oil.
  4. Rest 20 minutes.
  5. Spoon over grilled meat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chimichurri rojo adds tomato, paprika, and more chile.
  • A blender makes it smoother and less traditional.

Eastern North Carolina Barbecue Sauce

Vinegar, salt, and chile: thin, sharp, and hot.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Eastern North Carolina whole-hog barbecue is dressed with a thin sauce of vinegar and pepper, not a sweet tomato glaze. The style is tied to the eastern part of the state and to pit-cooked pork. It is one of the oldest documented American barbecue sauces still cooked in public.

Cultural background

The sauce is a mop and a table sauce. It cuts the fat of chopped pork and soaks into the meat. Lexington, farther west, adds a little tomato. That is a different sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Whole-hog pork is fatty. Thin vinegar and pepper cut that fat and soak into chopped meat. There is no tomato or sugar body, so the sauce tastes sharp and hot instead of sweet and thick.

How it is made

Cayenne or chile flakes and salt dissolve in cider vinegar. There is no thickener.

Authentic touch. Do not thicken it. If it coats a spoon like ketchup, it has become a different region’s sauce.

How it is used

Dress chopped or pulled pork, and put more on the table.

Field notes

  • The sauce is supposed to be thin. Thickness is a different barbecue region.
  • It is a defining split in North Carolina barbecue, east versus Lexington.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir everything until the salt and sugar dissolve.
  2. Let it sit 10 minutes.
  3. Taste and add more cayenne if you want a hotter mop.
  4. Use it on chopped pork.
  5. Keep the rest refrigerated.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lexington dip adds ketchup or tomato to a vinegar base.
  • Some pits use both red pepper and black pepper.

Pairings and uses

  • whole-hog pork

Feijoa Chutney

Feijoa, onion, vinegar, and ginger: fragrant, sweet, and sharp.

20 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3 cups

History

Feijoa, a South American fruit grown widely in New Zealand gardens, became a home-kitchen chutney fruit there in the twentieth century. The chutney follows the British cooked-chutney pattern of fruit, onion, vinegar, sugar, and spice, with feijoa’s perfume in place of mango or apple. It is a New Zealand household preserve, not an older Māori sauce.

Cultural background

Jars of it are made when the fruit drops in autumn and are eaten with cold meat and cheese. This version is a refrigerator chutney, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Feijoa’s perfume is the fruit. Vinegar, ginger, and sugar are the British-style chutney method that New Zealand home cooks used on a local glut. The result is fragrant, sweet, and sharp.

How it is made

Chopped feijoa, onion, vinegar, sugar, and ginger simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook it until a spoon leaves a trail. A thin chutney tastes like stewed fruit, not a preserve.

How it is used

Serve it with cold lamb, cheese, or a meat sandwich.

Field notes

  • Feijoa is also called pineapple guava, and it is not native to New Zealand.
  • The chutney style is the British cooked chutney adapted to a local glut of fruit.

Ingredients

  • 4 cups chopped feijoas
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground allspice

Steps

  1. Combine all the ingredients in a wide pot.
  2. Simmer, stirring, until thick, about 40 minutes.
  3. The chutney should hold a trail when you pull a spoon through it.
  4. Cool and jar.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a month. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Apple can replace part of the fruit if the feijoa season is short.
  • A hotter chutney adds a sliced chile.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat

  • cold lamb

Jerk Marinade

Scotch bonnet, allspice, thyme, and scallion: hot, aromatic, and smoky-sweet.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Jerk is the Jamaican method of seasoning and smoke-grilling meat, historically associated with the Maroons. Allspice, called pimento in Jamaica, and scotch bonnet chile define it, along with thyme and scallion. The wet marinade is one way the seasoning is applied. A dry rub is the other.

Cultural background

Roadside jerk pits cook pork and chicken over pimento wood. Home cooks use the same seasoning under a grill. Soy sauce appears in many modern marinades and is not the oldest layer of the technique.

Why it tastes this way

Scotch bonnet, allspice, thyme, and scallion are the taste. Allspice, called pimento in Jamaica, is the spice that makes it jerk rather than a generic chile paste. The wet paste seasons the surface before smoke.

How it is made

Scotch bonnet, scallion, thyme, allspice, and acid are blended into a wet paste that coats the meat before grilling.

Authentic touch. Cut slits in the meat so the paste gets under the surface. Reserve some paste before it touches raw meat if you want a table sauce.

How it is used

Rub it on chicken or pork and grill it. Serve with more sauce only if that portion never touched raw meat.

Field notes

  • Allspice berry, not a mix of spices, is the pimento in jerk.
  • Scotch bonnet is traditional. A milder chile makes a different dish.

Ingredients

  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • 2 scotch bonnet chiles
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 tablespoon ground allspice
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar

Steps

  1. Blend all the ingredients to a coarse paste.
  2. Cut slits in chicken or pork and rub the paste in.
  3. Refrigerate at least 2 hours.
  4. Grill until the meat is cooked through.
  5. Boil any leftover marinade before you consider serving it.

Do not eat marinade that has touched raw meat unless you cook it through.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dry jerk rub skips the liquid and uses the same spices.
  • Reserve a cup of marinade before it touches raw meat if you want a table sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue
  • Meat
  • jerk chicken
  • jerk pork
  • festival dumplings

Kansas City Barbecue Sauce

Tomato, molasses, vinegar, and smoke: thick, sweet, and tangy.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Kansas City barbecue became known for a thick, sweet, tomato-and-molasses sauce in the twentieth century, alongside a broad menu of smoked meats. Henry Perry is widely treated as an early figure in the city’s barbecue scene in the early 1900s. The thick commercial style associated with later restaurants is the one most people mean by Kansas City sauce.

Cultural background

It is a finishing and table sauce more than a thin mop. Burnt ends and ribs in Kansas City are closely tied to this flavor.

Why it tastes this way

Molasses and tomato make it thick and sweet, and vinegar keeps it from being only sweet. That balance suits ribs and burnt ends, which are already smoky. The sauce is a glaze and a table sauce, not a thin mop.

How it is made

Ketchup, molasses, vinegar, and spices simmer until the sauce is thick and glossy.

Authentic touch. Simmer until glossy, then brush it on late. Sugar burns if it spends the whole cook on the fire.

How it is used

Brush it on ribs and brisket near the end of cooking, and serve more alongside.

Field notes

  • Kansas City sauce is thick and sweet on purpose, unlike eastern Carolina vinegar sauce.
  • Molasses is the usual deep sweetener, not only white sugar.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup ketchup
  • 1/4 cup molasses
  • 1/4 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 teaspoon smoked paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients in a saucepan.
  2. Simmer 20 minutes, stirring, until glossy.
  3. Thin with a splash of water if it is tighter than you want.
  4. Cool before storing.
  5. Refrigerate and use within two weeks.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Memphis sauce is usually thinner and more vinegar-forward.
  • Liquid smoke is optional. Real smoke on the meat matters more.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue
  • Meat
  • Grilling
  • pork ribs
  • burnt ends
  • brisket

Lexington Barbecue Dip

Vinegar with a little tomato and cayenne: thin, sharp, and lightly red.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Lexington dip, or Piedmont barbecue sauce, is vinegar, a smaller amount of tomato, and pepper, used on pork shoulder in the Lexington area of North Carolina. It is redder than eastern Carolina sauce and thinner and less sweet than Kansas City sauce.

Cultural background

The local word is often dip, not sauce. It is spooned on chopped pork. The vinegar still leads. Tomato is the accent.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar is the main taste, so it stays thin and sharp. A little ketchup or tomato colors it and softens the edge without making a sweet glaze. Cayenne is the heat.

How it is made

Vinegar, a little ketchup, sugar, and cayenne are stirred together.

Authentic touch. Keep it thin. If it is as thick as ketchup, you have left Lexington. Toss it with chopped pork so it soaks in.

How it is used

Dress chopped pork shoulder with it.

Field notes

  • Dip is the local word.
  • Tomato is a minority ingredient. Vinegar still leads.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup ketchup
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir until the sugar and salt dissolve.
  2. Rest 20 minutes.
  3. Taste. It should be sharp.
  4. Toss with chopped pork.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Eastern sauce has no tomato.
  • Western North Carolina and Lexington are the names cooks argue over for this dip.

Pairings and uses

  • pork shoulder

Mango Chutney

Ripe or green mango, vinegar, ginger, and sugar: sweet, sour, and warm.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3 cups

History

Cooked mango chutney is an Indian preserve that British cooks adopted in the colonial period and sweetened into the jar now common with cheese and cold meat. Major Grey’s is a brand style, not a single historic recipe. This is a home cooked mango chutney with ginger and chile, for the refrigerator, not a canning formula.

Cultural background

In India, chutneys range from fresh to cooked. This cooked one is the style that traveled. It should stay chunky.

Why it tastes this way

Mango cooked with vinegar, ginger, and sugar tastes sweet and sour because the fruit concentrates and the vinegar preserves the sharpness. Green mango is sharper. Ripe mango is rounder.

How it is made

Mango, onion, vinegar, sugar, ginger, and chile simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook until thick and chunky. This is a refrigerator chutney, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

How it is used

Serve it with dal, rice, cheese, or cold meat.

Field notes

  • The very sweet British jar and a sharper Indian home chutney are related and not identical.
  • This is a refrigerator chutney, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

Ingredients

  • 3 mangoes, peeled and diced
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 3/4 cup vinegar
  • 1/2 cup sugar
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until the mango is soft and the pot is thick, about 35 minutes.
  2. Stir near the end.
  3. Cool.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Serve with rice or cold meat.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green mango makes a sharper chutney.
  • A fresh mint-mango relish is a different, uncooked sauce.

Memphis Barbecue Sauce

Tomato and vinegar, thinner than Kansas City: tangy and mildly sweet.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Memphis barbecue is known for dry-rubbed ribs and also for a thinner tomato-vinegar sauce than Kansas City’s thick molasses style. The sauce is a table and finishing sauce. Paprika and a sharp vinegar line are typical.

Cultural background

Ribs may be served dry, with sauce on the side. When the sauce is used, it should not be a candy glaze. Vinegar keeps it tangy.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato provides body, vinegar provides tang, and the simmer is short so the sauce stays thinner than a Kansas City glaze. Molasses, if any, is modest.

How it is made

Ketchup, vinegar, and spices simmer into a medium-thin sauce.

Authentic touch. Simmer it until it is pourable, not pasty. Serve it on the side of dry-rubbed ribs if you want the Memphis choice.

How it is used

Offer it with pork ribs.

Field notes

  • Memphis sauce is usually thinner and more vinegary than Kansas City sauce.
  • Dry rub is the other local tradition.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup ketchup
  • 1/2 cup cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup water

Steps

  1. Simmer everything 15 minutes.
  2. It should pour easily.
  3. Cool slightly.
  4. Serve with ribs.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dry rub with no sauce is the other Memphis path.
  • More molasses moves it toward Kansas City.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue
  • Meat
  • pork ribs
  • coleslaw
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Peri-Peri Sauce

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, lemon, and oil: hot, sharp, and bright.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Peri-peri, or piri-piri, is the African bird’s eye chile and the sauce made from it. The chile’s Swahili name, pilipili, passed into Portuguese. The sauce of chile, garlic, lemon, and oil or vinegar is associated with Mozambique and Angola and with Portuguese grilled chicken. This is a home sauce in that style, not a bottled brand formula.

Cultural background

Portuguese taverns made peri-peri chicken famous far beyond southern Africa. The chile itself is African. The grill style is a shared Mozambican and Portuguese table.

Why it tastes this way

African bird’s eye chile, garlic, and lemon are the heat and the brightness. Oil or vinegar carries them. The chile is fruity and hot, which is the taste of the sauce on grilled chicken.

How it is made

Chiles and garlic are blended with lemon and oil, then briefly simmered so the raw edge softens.

Authentic touch. Keep some sauce aside before it touches raw chicken if you want a table sauce. Marinade that has held raw meat has to be cooked.

How it is used

Use it as a marinade and a basting sauce for grilled chicken, and put more on the table.

Field notes

  • The chile name traveled from Swahili into Portuguese.
  • The sauce seasons the chicken. It is not a tomato barbecue glaze.

Ingredients

  • 8 fresh red bird’s eye chiles
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1/4 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar

Steps

  1. Blend the chiles, garlic, lemon, oil, paprika, salt, and vinegar.
  2. Simmer the puree 5 minutes.
  3. Cool.
  4. Use some as a marinade and save some for the table.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A vinegar-heavy version keeps longer and tastes sharper.
  • Yogurt is not part of the classic sauce.

South Carolina Mustard Barbecue Sauce

Yellow mustard, vinegar, and a little sugar: tangy, spicy, and sharp.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Mustard barbecue sauce is the style of the South Carolina Midlands, often linked to German settlement in the region. Yellow mustard and vinegar, not tomato, are the base. It is a distinct sauce from eastern North Carolina vinegar sauce and from Kansas City’s sweet tomato sauce.

Cultural background

It coats pulled pork. The mustard heat is sharp and then mellows. Sugar keeps it from being only harsh.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard and vinegar are both sharp, so the sauce tastes loud against fatty pork. A little sugar and spice round it. Tomato would make it a different region’s sauce.

How it is made

Yellow mustard, vinegar, sugar, and spices are simmered and rested.

Authentic touch. Simmer mustard and vinegar briefly, then let the sauce sit. Mustard tastes harsh until it rests.

How it is used

Mix it into pulled pork or serve it on the side.

Field notes

  • The Midlands mustard style is its own barbecue region.
  • German-American foodways are the usual historical explanation.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup yellow mustard
  • 1/2 cup cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer everything 10 minutes.
  2. Cool.
  3. Rest it a few hours if you can.
  4. Serve with pork.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of ketchup drifts it toward a different style.
  • More cayenne makes it hotter.

Texas Mop Sauce

Vinegar, black pepper, and beef drippings: thin, peppery, and savory.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Central Texas barbecue often serves brisket with salt, pepper, and smoke, and uses a thin mop of vinegar, pepper, and drippings rather than a sweet tomato glaze. The mop bastes the meat. It is not a Kansas City sauce.

Cultural background

The pepper crust on the meat is the seasoning. The mop adds moisture and a sharp note. A thick sweet sauce would cover the beef.

Why it tastes this way

Black pepper and vinegar taste sharp. Beef drippings, if you have them from the cook, add savor. There is little sugar, so it does not taste like a glaze.

How it is made

Vinegar, water, pepper, and a spoon of drippings or oil are combined.

Authentic touch. Mop the meat during a long cook. Keep the sauce thin. Use drippings from the beef if the cook produced them.

How it is used

Baste brisket or serve a little on the side.

Field notes

  • Central Texas flavor is smoke, salt, and pepper. The mop is thin on purpose.
  • A sweet red sauce is a different region.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cider vinegar
  • 1 cup water
  • 2 tablespoons black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons beef drippings or oil
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne

Steps

  1. Warm everything until the salt dissolves.
  2. Do not thicken it.
  3. Mop meat during cooking.
  4. Keep the rest warm.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tomato mop is more East Texas than Central Texas.
  • Salt and pepper on the meat matter more than the mop.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue
  • Meat
  • brisket
  • sausage
  • white bread
