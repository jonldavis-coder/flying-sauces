Appalachian Sauces

Biscuit gravies, sorghum, and chow-chow from the upland South.

Appalachian sauces in this book are the ones that can be tied to a real pot: red-eye gravy, sausage gravy (sometimes called sawmill gravy), chocolate gravy, tomato gravy, sorghum glaze, and chow-chow.

Scots-Irish and English settlers brought biscuit and vinegar habits into the mountains in the eighteenth century. German-speaking settlers, often by way of Pennsylvania, brought sweet-sour pickling. Enslaved people and later Black Appalachian cooks worked the same farms and kitchens. Their labor is part of this food, not a footnote.

Cherokee and other Native nations of these mountains grew corn and foraged long before those settlements. This book does not assign chocolate gravy, chow-chow, or sorghum glaze to them. Those recipes are later mountain home cooking. No Cherokee sauce is invented here to fill a gap.

Red-Eye Gravy

Ham drippings and black coffee: salty, bitter, and thin.

5 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Red-eye gravy is ham drippings loosened with black coffee, a Southern breakfast sauce for country ham and biscuits. It is thin. The name’s stories are folk explanations and are not repeated as fact. The coffee is the bitter note against salty ham.

Cultural background

Country ham is very salty. The gravy uses that fat and salt. Coffee keeps it from being only grease. It is not thickened with flour.

Why it tastes this way

Salty pork fat is the body. Black coffee adds bitterness and thins the drippings into a spoonable sauce. Flour would make it a different gravy.

How it is made

Ham is fried, then coffee is simmered in the drippings.

Authentic touch. Fry the ham, pour off only the excess, and deglaze with strong black coffee. Do not add flour.

How it is used

Spoon it over ham and biscuits.

Field notes

  • Coffee is the ingredient that makes it red-eye gravy.
  • It is supposed to be thin.

Ingredients

  • 4 slices country ham or smoked ham
  • 3/4 cup black coffee
  • Pinch of black pepper

Steps

  1. Fry the ham and set it aside. Leave the drippings.
  2. Pour in the coffee and simmer 3 minutes, scraping the pan.
  3. Add pepper.
  4. Serve the thin gravy over the ham.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A splash of water instead of coffee is a milder gravy.
  • A little flour makes it creamy and no longer red-eye.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Meat
  • country ham
  • biscuits
  • grits
  • Dairy-free
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Sausage Gravy

Pork sausage, black pepper, and milk: savory, peppery, and thick.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Sawmill gravy, or sausage gravy, is a Southern American breakfast sauce of pork drippings, flour, milk, and black pepper, served on biscuits. The sausage fat is the roux’s fat. Pepper is the spice. It is not British onion gravy.

Cultural background

Biscuits are the plate. The gravy should be peppery and loose enough to soak in. A blond roux keeps it from tasting burnt.

Why it tastes this way

Rendered sausage fat tastes of pork. Flour thickens milk into a white gravy. A heavy hand with black pepper is the traditional heat, not chile.

How it is made

Sausage is browned, flour is cooked in the fat, and milk is whisked in.

Authentic touch. Cook the sausage until the fat renders, sprinkle flour into that fat, and only then add milk. Use more pepper than you think.

How it is used

Spoon it over split biscuits.

Field notes

  • The pepper is part of the name people expect.
  • The fat from the sausage is the roux.

Ingredients

  • 8 ounces breakfast sausage
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 2 cups whole milk
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Brown the sausage and leave the fat in the pan.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook 1 minute.
  3. Whisk in the milk and simmer until thick.
  4. Add pepper and salt.
  5. Serve on biscuits.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of hot sauce is a personal addition.
  • Onion gravy is the British brown cousin.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Meat
  • biscuits
  • eggs
  • Nut-free

Chocolate Gravy

Cocoa, milk, and sugar: bittersweet, thick, and breakfast-soft.

5 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 2 cups

History

Chocolate gravy is a breakfast sauce of the Appalachian mountains and parts of the upland South, especially Kentucky, Tennessee, West Virginia, and Arkansas. Cocoa, flour, milk, and sugar are cooked into a pourable gravy for biscuits. It is home cooking, not a restaurant dessert sauce, and it is not a Cherokee recipe.

Cultural background

Mountain tables served it when cocoa was on hand and oven sweets were not the point. Families still argue about how sweet it should be. The biscuit is the plate.

Why it tastes this way

Cocoa is bitter. Sugar and milk make it sweet enough for breakfast, and a flour roux makes it a gravy rather than hot chocolate. Butter carries the cocoa fat. That is why it tastes like a sauce you pour, not a cup you drink.

How it is made

Butter, flour, and cocoa make a roux, then milk and sugar simmer into a gravy.

Authentic touch. Cook the cocoa in the butter and flour for a minute so it does not taste dusty. Keep it pourable. It should soak a split biscuit.

How it is used

Pour it over hot biscuits at breakfast.

Field notes

  • It is a breakfast gravy, served where other tables would pour sausage gravy.
  • It is Appalachian home cooking, not a Native American ceremonial dish.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 2 tablespoons unsweetened cocoa powder
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 2 cups whole milk
  • Pinch of salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon vanilla

Steps

  1. Melt the butter and stir in the flour and cocoa. Cook 1 minute.
  2. Whisk in the milk, sugar, and salt.
  3. Simmer until it coats a spoon, about 6 minutes.
  4. Stir in the vanilla.
  5. Serve over biscuits.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of vanilla is common.
  • Less sugar leaves it more bitter, which some tables prefer.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Desserts
  • biscuits
  • breakfast
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Tomato Gravy

Tomato, milk, and a soft roux: tangy, savory, and pale red.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 2 cups

History

Tomato gravy is an Appalachian and Southern breakfast and supper sauce: fat, flour, tomato, and milk, poured over biscuits. It is what cooks made when there was tomato and no meat for sausage gravy, or alongside it. Bacon fat is common. The tomato is the flavor, not a long marinara.

Cultural background

It belongs to the same biscuit table as sausage gravy and, in some houses, chocolate gravy. The tomato keeps it bright. The milk keeps it a gravy.

Why it tastes this way

A soft roux thickens milk, and tomato adds acid so the gravy is not only creamy. Bacon fat, when you use it, tastes salty and smoky. A short cook is why it still tastes of fresh or canned tomato rather than a reduced pasta sauce.

How it is made

Fat and flour make a roux, tomato is stirred in, then milk loosens it into a gravy.

Authentic touch. Cook the flour in the fat until it smells nutty but stays pale. Add tomato before the milk so the acid does not scorch the roux.

How it is used

Pour it over biscuits.

Field notes

  • It is a biscuit gravy, not a pasta sauce.
  • Tomato stands in when there is no sausage, and some tables want both.

Ingredients

  • 2 slices bacon, or 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 1 cup chopped tomato
  • 1 cup milk
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Cook the bacon and leave the fat, or melt the butter.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomato and stir until it softens.
  4. Whisk in the milk and simmer until thick.
  5. Season with salt and pepper and serve on biscuits.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Oil instead of bacon fat makes a different, meatless gravy.
  • A pinch of sugar is used when the tomato is very sharp.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Meat
  • biscuits
  • breakfast
  • fried potatoes
  • Nut-free

Sorghum Glaze

Sorghum syrup and butter: malty, tangy, and sticky.

2 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3/4 cup

History

Sweet sorghum syrup, often just called sorghum or molasses in the mountains, was a staple sweetener in Appalachia where cane sugar was bought and sorghum could be grown and milled nearby. Families poured it on biscuits and used it to glaze ham. This glaze is that syrup loosened with butter and sharpened with a little vinegar. The syrup itself is the traditional product.

Cultural background

Sorghum tastes malty and a little mineral, not like maple and not like blackstrap molasses. Mountain cooks used it because the cane grew there. It is a table sauce and a ham glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum syrup is less sweet and more grassy than maple. Butter makes it saucy. Vinegar keeps the glaze from being only sugar, which is why it can sit on salty ham as well as on biscuits.

How it is made

Sorghum, butter, and a spoon of vinegar are warmed together.

Authentic touch. Use sorghum syrup, not molasses and not maple, if you want this taste. Warm it only until it loosens. A hard boil turns it bitter.

How it is used

Pour it on biscuits or brush it on ham near the end of cooking.

Field notes

  • In much of Appalachia the word molasses means sorghum syrup.
  • The syrup was milled nearby, which is why it, not maple, became the mountain sweetener.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup sorghum syrup
  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 1 tablespoon cider vinegar
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Warm the sorghum, butter, vinegar, and salt over low heat.
  2. Stir until the butter melts and the glaze is smooth.
  3. Do not boil it hard.
  4. Use it warm on biscuits or ham.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Straight sorghum on a biscuit needs no butter.
  • A pinch of mustard makes a sharper ham glaze.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Meat
  • biscuits
  • ham
  • cornbread
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Chow-chow

Cabbage, green tomato, vinegar, and mustard: sharp, sweet, and crunchy.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 12 · about 4 cups

History

Chow-chow is a chopped Southern relish of cabbage, green tomato, onion, and pepper in vinegar, mustard, and turmeric. It is common in Appalachian and wider Southern pantries. The name’s origin is disputed, so this page does not repeat a folk etymology. This is a refrigerator relish, not a canning recipe.

Cultural background

It was a way to keep the last green tomatoes and cabbage. It goes with beans, field peas, and cold meat. Turmeric makes it yellow. Mustard makes it sharp.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and mustard preserve and sharpen the vegetables. Sugar keeps the pickle from being only sour. Turmeric colors it and adds a bitter edge. The vegetables stay crisp because they are salted and cooked briefly, not stewed to mush.

How it is made

Salted cabbage, green tomato, and pepper are folded into a simmered vinegar, mustard, and turmeric dressing.

Authentic touch. Salt the vegetables and drain them before they meet the vinegar. Cook the dressing until the raw mustard taste is gone, then add the vegetables only long enough to heat through.

How it is used

Eat it cold with beans, greens, or a ham biscuit.

Field notes

  • Green tomato and cabbage are the usual body.
  • This version is refrigerated. It is not a shelf-stable canning formula.

Ingredients

  • 3 cups chopped cabbage
  • 2 green tomatoes, chopped
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 bell pepper, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 cup cider vinegar
  • 1/3 cup sugar
  • 1 tablespoon mustard powder
  • 1 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1 teaspoon mustard seeds

Steps

  1. Toss the vegetables with the salt, rest 30 minutes, rinse, and drain.
  2. Simmer the vinegar, sugar, mustard powder, turmeric, and mustard seeds 5 minutes.
  3. Stir in the vegetables and cook 3 minutes.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and eat within two weeks. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some pots use more green tomato than cabbage.
  • A hotter chow-chow adds cayenne.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Meat
  • Vegetables
  • beans
  • ham biscuits
  • cooked greens
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free
