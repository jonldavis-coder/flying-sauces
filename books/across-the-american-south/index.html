Across the American South

A train route of vinegar, mustard, pepper, and Gulf sauces.

Southern barbecue is a map. Eastern North Carolina is vinegar and pepper. Lexington adds a little tomato. The South Carolina Midlands use mustard. Alabama’s white sauce is mayonnaise. Memphis is a thinner tomato sauce.

Enslaved West African cooks and their descendants shaped Southern pits and pepper sauces. English and Scottish settlers brought vinegar habits. German settlement in the Midlands is the usual account of the mustard sauce. This book follows that route and does not pretend one gravy belongs to one people only.

Eastern North Carolina Barbecue Sauce

Vinegar, salt, and chile: thin, sharp, and hot.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Eastern North Carolina whole-hog barbecue is dressed with a thin sauce of vinegar and pepper, not a sweet tomato glaze. The style is tied to the eastern part of the state and to pit-cooked pork. It is one of the oldest documented American barbecue sauces still cooked in public.

Cultural background

The sauce is a mop and a table sauce. It cuts the fat of chopped pork and soaks into the meat. Lexington, farther west, adds a little tomato. That is a different sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Whole-hog pork is fatty. Thin vinegar and pepper cut that fat and soak into chopped meat. There is no tomato or sugar body, so the sauce tastes sharp and hot instead of sweet and thick.

How it is made

Cayenne or chile flakes and salt dissolve in cider vinegar. There is no thickener.

Authentic touch. Do not thicken it. If it coats a spoon like ketchup, it has become a different region’s sauce.

How it is used

Dress chopped or pulled pork, and put more on the table.

Field notes

  • The sauce is supposed to be thin. Thickness is a different barbecue region.
  • It is a defining split in North Carolina barbecue, east versus Lexington.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon fine salt
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir everything until the salt and sugar dissolve.
  2. Let it sit 10 minutes.
  3. Taste and add more cayenne if you want a hotter mop.
  4. Use it on chopped pork.
  5. Keep the rest refrigerated.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lexington dip adds ketchup or tomato to a vinegar base.
  • Some pits use both red pepper and black pepper.

Lexington Barbecue Dip

Vinegar with a little tomato and cayenne: thin, sharp, and lightly red.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Lexington dip, or Piedmont barbecue sauce, is vinegar, a smaller amount of tomato, and pepper, used on pork shoulder in the Lexington area of North Carolina. It is redder than eastern Carolina sauce and thinner and less sweet than Kansas City sauce.

Cultural background

The local word is often dip, not sauce. It is spooned on chopped pork. The vinegar still leads. Tomato is the accent.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar is the main taste, so it stays thin and sharp. A little ketchup or tomato colors it and softens the edge without making a sweet glaze. Cayenne is the heat.

How it is made

Vinegar, a little ketchup, sugar, and cayenne are stirred together.

Authentic touch. Keep it thin. If it is as thick as ketchup, you have left Lexington. Toss it with chopped pork so it soaks in.

How it is used

Dress chopped pork shoulder with it.

Field notes

  • Dip is the local word.
  • Tomato is a minority ingredient. Vinegar still leads.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup ketchup
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir until the sugar and salt dissolve.
  2. Rest 20 minutes.
  3. Taste. It should be sharp.
  4. Toss with chopped pork.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Eastern sauce has no tomato.
  • Western North Carolina and Lexington are the names cooks argue over for this dip.

Pairings and uses

  • pork shoulder

South Carolina Mustard Barbecue Sauce

Yellow mustard, vinegar, and a little sugar: tangy, spicy, and sharp.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Mustard barbecue sauce is the style of the South Carolina Midlands, often linked to German settlement in the region. Yellow mustard and vinegar, not tomato, are the base. It is a distinct sauce from eastern North Carolina vinegar sauce and from Kansas City’s sweet tomato sauce.

Cultural background

It coats pulled pork. The mustard heat is sharp and then mellows. Sugar keeps it from being only harsh.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard and vinegar are both sharp, so the sauce tastes loud against fatty pork. A little sugar and spice round it. Tomato would make it a different region’s sauce.

How it is made

Yellow mustard, vinegar, sugar, and spices are simmered and rested.

Authentic touch. Simmer mustard and vinegar briefly, then let the sauce sit. Mustard tastes harsh until it rests.

How it is used

Mix it into pulled pork or serve it on the side.

Field notes

  • The Midlands mustard style is its own barbecue region.
  • German-American foodways are the usual historical explanation.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup yellow mustard
  • 1/2 cup cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup sugar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer everything 10 minutes.
  2. Cool.
  3. Rest it a few hours if you can.
  4. Serve with pork.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of ketchup drifts it toward a different style.
  • More cayenne makes it hotter.

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • pulled pork

Alabama White Sauce

Mayonnaise, vinegar, and black pepper: tangy, peppery, and pale.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Alabama white sauce is credited to Bob Gibson of Decatur, who was serving barbecue in the 1920s. It is a mayonnaise and vinegar sauce, heavily peppered, used on smoked chicken. The attribution to Gibson’s barbecue in Decatur is the standard account in barbecue histories.

Cultural background

It is a north Alabama sauce, especially for chicken, and it now shows up on pork and on the table as a dip. It is not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise carries pepper and vinegar, so the sauce is pale, tangy, and peppery rather than red and sweet. It was built for smoked chicken, whose mild meat can take a sharp white sauce.

How it is made

Mayonnaise is loosened with vinegar and seasoned with pepper, salt, and a little sugar.

Authentic touch. Use plenty of black pepper and keep it cold. The pepper is the heat. Chile is optional.

How it is used

Toss it with smoked chicken, or serve it as a dip for smoked meats.

Field notes

  • The sauce is white because the base is mayonnaise, not because it is mild in flavor.
  • It was built for chicken in a state better known, from the outside, for pork and tomato sauces.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 1/3 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne

Steps

  1. Whisk the mayonnaise and vinegar until smooth.
  2. Stir in the pepper, salt, sugar, and cayenne.
  3. Rest it 15 minutes so the pepper blooms.
  4. Taste and add vinegar if you want it sharper.
  5. Refrigerate.

This is a mayonnaise sauce. Keep it cold.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Horseradish is a common addition and not required.
  • Use a thin homemade mayonnaise if you want more acid.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers

Memphis Barbecue Sauce

Tomato and vinegar, thinner than Kansas City: tangy and mildly sweet.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Memphis barbecue is known for dry-rubbed ribs and also for a thinner tomato-vinegar sauce than Kansas City’s thick molasses style. The sauce is a table and finishing sauce. Paprika and a sharp vinegar line are typical.

Cultural background

Ribs may be served dry, with sauce on the side. When the sauce is used, it should not be a candy glaze. Vinegar keeps it tangy.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato provides body, vinegar provides tang, and the simmer is short so the sauce stays thinner than a Kansas City glaze. Molasses, if any, is modest.

How it is made

Ketchup, vinegar, and spices simmer into a medium-thin sauce.

Authentic touch. Simmer it until it is pourable, not pasty. Serve it on the side of dry-rubbed ribs if you want the Memphis choice.

How it is used

Offer it with pork ribs.

Field notes

  • Memphis sauce is usually thinner and more vinegary than Kansas City sauce.
  • Dry rub is the other local tradition.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup ketchup
  • 1/2 cup cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup water

Steps

  1. Simmer everything 15 minutes.
  2. It should pour easily.
  3. Cool slightly.
  4. Serve with ribs.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dry rub with no sauce is the other Memphis path.
  • More molasses moves it toward Kansas City.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue
  • Meat
  • pork ribs
  • coleslaw
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Nashville Hot Oil

Cayenne and hot oil: very hot, spicy, and staining.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1/2 cup

History

Nashville hot chicken is fried chicken coated in a cayenne-and-oil paste. The style is associated with Prince’s Hot Chicken Shack. The heat sits on the crust. It is an oil sauce, not a vinegar Buffalo sauce.

Cultural background

The chicken is spicy enough that pickles and white bread are the relief. Lard or frying oil carries the cayenne. Sugar and paprika color and round some recipes.

Why it tastes this way

Cayenne bloomed in hot fat tastes louder than cayenne stirred into a cold sauce. The oil stains the crust and keeps the heat on the surface. Vinegar is not the body.

How it is made

Cayenne, paprika, and a little sugar are stirred into very hot oil.

Authentic touch. Stir cayenne into hot frying oil or melted lard, not into cold butter. Brush or toss it on fried chicken immediately.

How it is used

Coat fried chicken and serve with pickles and bread.

Field notes

  • The heat is an oil paste on fried chicken.
  • Prince’s in Nashville is the shop most tied to the style.

Ingredients

  • 1/3 cup hot frying oil or melted lard
  • 2 tablespoons cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon brown sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder

Steps

  1. Stir the spices and sugar into the hot oil.
  2. The paste should be deep red and loose.
  3. Toss or brush it on just-fried chicken.
  4. Serve with pickles.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A brown-sugar version is slightly sweeter.
  • Buffalo sauce is vinegar and butter, a different heat.

Pairings and uses

  • Wings
  • Meat
  • fried chicken
  • pickles
  • white bread
  • Dairy-free
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Comeback Sauce

Mayonnaise, ketchup, and spice: creamy, sweet, and peppery.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Comeback sauce is the Mississippi dip of mayonnaise, chili sauce or ketchup, onion, and spices, associated with Greek-owned restaurants in Jackson in the mid-twentieth century. The name is the joke that you come back for it. It is a house sauce for fries, onion rings, and seafood.

Cultural background

It is thicker and spicier than plain burger sauce. Paprika, garlic, and black pepper are usual. Each restaurant had a ratio.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise and ketchup make it pink and rich. Worcestershire, if used, adds anchovy savor. Paprika and pepper keep it from tasting only sweet. This version skips Worcestershire so it stays vegetarian.

How it is made

Mayonnaise, ketchup, onion, and spices are mixed and chilled.

Authentic touch. Stir it and chill it. The onion should be very fine. Taste for pepper. It is a dip, so it should be thick.

How it is used

Serve it with fries, onion rings, or fried seafood.

Field notes

  • Jackson restaurants made it a local standard in the twentieth century.
  • The name is about coming back for the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 1/4 cup ketchup
  • 1 tablespoon minced onion
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Stir everything together.
  2. Chill 30 minutes.
  3. Taste for pepper.
  4. Serve as a dip.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dash of hot sauce is common.
  • Worcestershire is used in many Jackson versions and adds fish.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Seafood
  • fries
  • onion rings
  • fried shrimp
  • Vegetarian

Creole Sauce

Tomato, onion, celery, and bell pepper: savory, sweet, and lightly herbal.

15 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Creole sauce is the tomato sauce of Louisiana built on onion, celery, and bell pepper, cooked with garlic and thyme and served with shrimp. The three vegetables are the local aromatic base. It is a tomato sauce, distinct from a brown roux étouffée.

Cultural background

Shrimp Creole is the dish, eaten with rice. The vegetables should cook until soft before the tomato goes in. Cayenne heats it. The sauce should taste of pepper and celery, not only tomato.

Why it tastes this way

Bell pepper, celery, and onion cooked in oil taste sweet and savory. Tomato and thyme make a round sauce. A short cook keeps it brighter than a long Sunday gravy.

How it is made

The aromatic vegetables soften, then tomato, garlic, and thyme simmer.

Authentic touch. Cook the onion, celery, and pepper until soft before you add tomato. That base is the flavor.

How it is used

Serve it with shrimp and rice.

Field notes

  • Onion, celery, and green pepper are the Louisiana aromatic trio in this sauce.
  • It is tomato-based, unlike a brown étouffée.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 1 celery stalk, chopped
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 can (28 ounces) tomatoes, crushed
  • 1 teaspoon thyme
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Cook the onion, pepper, and celery in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the garlic, tomatoes, thyme, cayenne, and salt.
  3. Simmer 25 minutes.
  4. Add cooked shrimp at the end if you are making the dish.
  5. Serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A roux makes it closer to étouffée.
  • More cayenne is a household choice.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Rice
  • Stews
  • shrimp
  • rice

Louisiana Rémoulade

Mayonnaise, Creole mustard, and paprika: spicy, tangy, and pink.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Louisiana rémoulade is a spicy mayonnaise sauce for cold shrimp and fried seafood, seasoned with mustard, paprika, and often horseradish and hot sauce. It is sharper and redder than French rémoulade, which is herbs and capers. Creole mustard is the local grainy mustard.

Cultural background

Shrimp rémoulade is a New Orleans starter. The sauce should taste of mustard and paprika, not only of mayonnaise. A chill lets it thicken.

Why it tastes this way

Creole mustard is sharp. Paprika and cayenne add color and heat. Lemon and a little horseradish keep it bright. The French herb rémoulade is a cousin with a quieter spice list.

How it is made

Mayonnaise, mustard, paprika, and lemon are stirred together.

Authentic touch. Use a grainy Creole or whole-grain mustard and paprika. Chill the sauce before you dress cold shrimp.

How it is used

Spoon it over cold shrimp or use it as a fried-seafood dip.

Field notes

  • New Orleans rémoulade is a spiced mayonnaise, not the French herb sauce.
  • Paprika is the color.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 2 tablespoons whole-grain mustard
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon prepared horseradish
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon hot sauce
  • 1 scallion, minced
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir everything together.
  2. Chill 30 minutes.
  3. Taste for mustard and heat.
  4. Serve with shrimp.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • French rémoulade is herbier and less red.
  • Horseradish makes it sharper.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Dipping
  • cold shrimp
  • fried fish
  • lettuce
  • Vegetarian

Louisiana-Style Hot Sauce

Cayenne and vinegar: thin, sharp, and hot.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 20 · about 1 cup

History

Louisiana hot sauce is aged or simmered cayenne in vinegar and salt. The McIlhenny family on Avery Island began selling Tabasco sauce, made from tabasco peppers, in 1868, and that bottle defined the style for many eaters. This recipe is a weeknight cayenne-vinegar sauce in the Louisiana style. It is not the aged barrel formula of any brand.

Cultural background

It is on the table for oysters, greens, eggs, and beans. Thinness is correct. A thick chile paste is a different family.

Why it tastes this way

Cayenne and vinegar, simmered and strained, taste thin, sharp, and hot. Thickness is not the goal. The vinegar is why it seasons oysters and greens without covering them.

How it is made

Cayenne, garlic, vinegar, and salt simmer, then the sauce is blended and strained.

Authentic touch. Strain it if you want it to pour. Leave it a day in the refrigerator. The flavor settles.

How it is used

Shake it on oysters, greens, eggs, or rice and beans.

Field notes

  • The style is thin and vinegar-forward. Thickness is not a flaw to correct.
  • A brand name on a bottle is not the name of the whole style.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup fresh red cayenne peppers, stemmed
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 3/4 cup distilled white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Simmer the peppers, garlic, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend until smooth.
  3. Strain if you want it pourable from a bottle.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate. The flavor settles after a day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fermented cayenne mash is the commercial path and takes months.
  • Crystal-style sauces lean on cayenne. Tabasco-style sauces use tabasco peppers. Both are Louisiana vinegar sauces.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Seafood
  • Eggs
  • Rice
  • raw or grilled oysters
  • greens
  • scrambled eggs
  • red beans
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free

Sausage Gravy

Pork sausage, black pepper, and milk: savory, peppery, and thick.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Sawmill gravy, or sausage gravy, is a Southern American breakfast sauce of pork drippings, flour, milk, and black pepper, served on biscuits. The sausage fat is the roux’s fat. Pepper is the spice. It is not British onion gravy.

Cultural background

Biscuits are the plate. The gravy should be peppery and loose enough to soak in. A blond roux keeps it from tasting burnt.

Why it tastes this way

Rendered sausage fat tastes of pork. Flour thickens milk into a white gravy. A heavy hand with black pepper is the traditional heat, not chile.

How it is made

Sausage is browned, flour is cooked in the fat, and milk is whisked in.

Authentic touch. Cook the sausage until the fat renders, sprinkle flour into that fat, and only then add milk. Use more pepper than you think.

How it is used

Spoon it over split biscuits.

Field notes

  • The pepper is part of the name people expect.
  • The fat from the sausage is the roux.

Ingredients

  • 8 ounces breakfast sausage
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 2 cups whole milk
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Brown the sausage and leave the fat in the pan.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook 1 minute.
  3. Whisk in the milk and simmer until thick.
  4. Add pepper and salt.
  5. Serve on biscuits.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of hot sauce is a personal addition.
  • Onion gravy is the British brown cousin.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Meat
  • biscuits
  • eggs
  • Nut-free

Red-Eye Gravy

Ham drippings and black coffee: salty, bitter, and thin.

5 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Red-eye gravy is ham drippings loosened with black coffee, a Southern breakfast sauce for country ham and biscuits. It is thin. The name’s stories are folk explanations and are not repeated as fact. The coffee is the bitter note against salty ham.

Cultural background

Country ham is very salty. The gravy uses that fat and salt. Coffee keeps it from being only grease. It is not thickened with flour.

Why it tastes this way

Salty pork fat is the body. Black coffee adds bitterness and thins the drippings into a spoonable sauce. Flour would make it a different gravy.

How it is made

Ham is fried, then coffee is simmered in the drippings.

Authentic touch. Fry the ham, pour off only the excess, and deglaze with strong black coffee. Do not add flour.

How it is used

Spoon it over ham and biscuits.

Field notes

  • Coffee is the ingredient that makes it red-eye gravy.
  • It is supposed to be thin.

Ingredients

  • 4 slices country ham or smoked ham
  • 3/4 cup black coffee
  • Pinch of black pepper

Steps

  1. Fry the ham and set it aside. Leave the drippings.
  2. Pour in the coffee and simmer 3 minutes, scraping the pan.
  3. Add pepper.
  4. Serve the thin gravy over the ham.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A splash of water instead of coffee is a milder gravy.
  • A little flour makes it creamy and no longer red-eye.

Pairings and uses

  • Breakfast
  • Meat
  • country ham
  • biscuits
  • grits

  • Gluten-free
