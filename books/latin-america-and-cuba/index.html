Salsa Roja

Roasted tomato, chile, and garlic: smoky, savory, and hot.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Salsa roja is a cooked Mexican red sauce of tomato or tomatillo’s red cousins, dried or fresh chile, and garlic. Roasting or boiling the vegetables, then frying the puree, is a common path. It is a table salsa and a taco sauce, not a long Italian tomato sauce.

Cultural background

Taquerías and home kitchens both keep a red salsa. The chile chosen, árbol, guajillo, or jalapeño, decides the heat. The fry after blending is what makes many red salsas taste cooked rather than raw.

Why it tastes this way

Charring tomato and chile adds smoke. Frying the blended salsa in a little fat cooks off the raw taste and concentrates it. Watery boiled tomato without that fry tastes thin.

How it is made

Tomato, chile, onion, and garlic are charred, blended, and fried briefly.

Authentic touch. Roast or char the vegetables, blend, then fry the puree for a few minutes until it darkens.

How it is used

Spoon it on tacos, eggs, or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • The fry after blending is a usual step, not a mistake.
  • Red salsa is a category. The chile changes the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 4 ripe tomatoes
  • 2 jalapeños
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1/4 onion
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil

Steps

  1. Char the tomatoes, jalapeños, garlic, and onion in a dry pan or under a broiler.
  2. Blend with salt until coarse.
  3. Fry the puree in the oil 5 minutes.
  4. Taste for salt.
  5. Serve warm or cool.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A boiled salsa is milder and less smoky.
  • Árbol chile makes it hotter.

Salsa Verde

Tomatillo, chile, and cilantro: tart, bright, and hot.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Mexican salsa verde is built on tomatillos, not on Italian parsley. Tomatillos are husked, boiled or roasted, and blended with chile, onion, and often cilantro. The sauce for enchiladas verdes and for tacos is this family.

Cultural background

The tart taste is the tomatillo itself. Cilantro is common and not universal. Roasting makes it sweeter and smokier. Boiling makes it brighter.

Why it tastes this way

Tomatillos are naturally tart, which is why salsa verde tastes sour even before lime. Chile brings heat. A fry or a simmer softens the raw edge.

How it is made

Tomatillos and chile are cooked, then blended with onion, garlic, and cilantro.

Authentic touch. Husk and rinse the tomatillos. The sticky coat is bitter if you leave it on. Then boil or roast them until they soften.

How it is used

Use it on enchiladas, chicken, or tacos.

Field notes

  • The green is tomatillo, not parsley.
  • Rinse off the sticky husk residue before cooking.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound tomatillos, husked and rinsed
  • 2 jalapeños
  • 1/4 onion
  • 1 garlic clove
  • 1/2 cup cilantro
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the tomatillos and jalapeños in water until soft, about 10 minutes.
  2. Blend with onion, garlic, cilantro, and salt.
  3. Fry the salsa 3 minutes if you want it rounder.
  4. Taste.
  5. Serve.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Roasted tomatillo salsa is smokier.
  • Italian salsa verde is parsley and is a different sauce.

Pico de Gallo

Raw tomato, onion, chile, and lime: fresh, sharp, and juicy.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

Pico de gallo is a chopped raw salsa of tomato, white onion, chile, cilantro, and lime. It is not cooked. The name’s folk stories are not solid enough to repeat. The method is a fresh relish, eaten the day it is made.

Cultural background

It sits beside grilled meat, beans, and tacos. Because nothing is cooked, ripe tomato and a sharp onion matter. Lime and salt draw out the juice that becomes the sauce.

Why it tastes this way

No cook means the tomato stays bright and the onion stays sharp. Lime juice salts the mixture and pulls out liquid, so it tastes juicy rather than like dry dice.

How it is made

Tomato, onion, chile, cilantro, lime, and salt are chopped and rested.

Authentic touch. Salt it and let it sit 15 minutes. Chop the onion fine. Serve it the same day. It does not keep like a cooked salsa.

How it is used

Eat it with tacos, grilled meat, or beans.

Field notes

  • It is raw. Cooking it makes a different salsa.
  • The juice after a short rest is part of the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 3 ripe tomatoes, diced
  • 1/2 white onion, finely diced
  • 1 jalapeño, minced
  • 1/2 cup cilantro, chopped
  • 1 lime, juiced
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Combine everything.
  2. Rest 15 minutes.
  3. Taste for salt and lime.
  4. Drain nothing. The juice stays.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Serrano is hotter than jalapeño.
  • A mango pico is a different, sweeter relish.

Guacamole

Ripe avocado, lime, onion, and chile: rich, fresh, and bright.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Guacamole is mashed avocado seasoned with lime or salt, onion, chile, and often cilantro and tomato. Avocado sauces are old in Mesoamerican cooking. The word comes from Nahuatl ahuacamolli, avocado sauce. It is a dip and a sauce for grilled meat and tacos.

Cultural background

Ripe fruit is the body. Lime slows darkening and adds brightness. A mortar gives a coarser, more traditional texture than a blender.

Why it tastes this way

Avocado fat tastes rich and mild, so lime, salt, onion, and chile are what make it savory. Over-blending whips in air and makes it fluffy and pale instead of dense.

How it is made

Avocado is mashed with salt, lime, onion, chile, and cilantro.

Authentic touch. Mash ripe avocados with a fork, leaving some texture. Fold in onion, chile, cilantro, and lime. Do not use a long blend.

How it is used

Eat it with tortillas, tacos, or grilled meat the day it is made.

Field notes

  • The name is from Nahuatl for avocado sauce.
  • Lime and an airtight press slow browning. They do not stop it forever.

Ingredients

  • 3 ripe avocados
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 lime, juiced
  • 2 tablespoons minced onion
  • 1 jalapeño, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro

Steps

  1. Mash the avocados with salt and lime, leaving lumps.
  2. Fold in the onion, jalapeño, and cilantro.
  3. Taste.
  4. Press plastic against the surface if it has to wait.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Tomatillo in the mash is a greener, tarter version.
  • A completely smooth blender guacamole is a different texture.

Pairings and uses

  • Grilling

Mole Poblano

Dried chiles, nuts, seeds, spices, and a little chocolate: deep, mildly hot, and complex.

45 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 6 cups

History

Mole poblano is the thick chile sauce of Puebla, served with turkey or chicken. It uses several dried chiles, a fried puree, nuts and seeds, spices, and a small amount of chocolate as a seasoning, not as a dessert. Picturesque stories about a convent inventing it for a visiting bishop are legends, not reliable history, and they are left off this page.

Cultural background

In Puebla and in Mexican celebrations more broadly, mole poblano is feast food. It is one mole among many. Oaxacan mole negro, mole verde, and pipián are different sauces.

Why it tastes this way

The layered taste is fried chiles, nuts, seeds, and spices, with a little chocolate as one seasoning among many. Puebla’s dried chiles and the fry in fat are why it tastes toasted and deep rather than like a chocolate drink.

How it is made

Dried chiles are fried and soaked, then blended with toasted nuts, seeds, spices, tomato or tomatillo, and chocolate, and the puree is fried in fat and thinned with stock.

Authentic touch. Fry the blended puree in fat until it darkens. That fry, not extra chocolate, is what makes mole taste cooked.

How it is used

Serve with poached or roasted turkey or chicken, sesame seeds on top, and rice or tortillas.

Field notes

  • Chocolate in mole poblano is one ingredient among many, used the way a spice is used.
  • Convent-invention tales are widely told and not treated here as fact.

Ingredients

  • 4 dried ancho chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 3 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 dried pasilla chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 tablespoons lard or neutral oil
  • 1/4 cup almonds
  • 2 tablespoons peanuts
  • 2 tablespoons sesame seeds
  • 1/4 cup raisins
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 2 tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 cups chicken stock, plus more to thin
  • 1 ounce Mexican chocolate, chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Toast the chiles in a dry pan until fragrant, soak them in hot water 20 minutes, and drain.
  2. Fry the nuts, sesame, and raisins in the fat and remove them. Fry the onion and tomato.
  3. Blend the chiles, fried ingredients, cinnamon, and 1 cup stock until smooth.
  4. Fry the puree in the remaining fat, stirring, for about 10 minutes.
  5. Add the rest of the stock and the chocolate. Simmer 40 minutes, stirring, until thick. Season with salt and thin with stock if needed.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mole negro of Oaxaca uses chilhuacle chiles and a darker, smokier method.
  • The chocolate is a seasoning. Adding a large amount turns it into a different sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Rice
  • Stews
  • turkey
  • chicken
  • rice
  • warm tortillas
  • Dairy-free

Recado Rojo

Annatto, garlic, oregano, and sour orange: earthy, sharp, and savory.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Recado rojo is the Yucatecan achiote paste used for cochinita pibil and other pit or oven meats. Annatto seeds dye it red and taste earthy. Sour orange, garlic, and oregano are the rest. It is not a tomato marinade.

Cultural background

Banana leaves wrap pibil meats and add a green aroma when they are used. The paste is the seasoning. Sour orange is the Yucatán acid, as in Cuban mojo, and lime plus orange is the stand-in.

Why it tastes this way

Annatto tastes earthy and colors the meat. Sour orange keeps it sharp. Oregano and garlic perfume the fat. Without the citrus, achiote paste tastes flat and dusty.

How it is made

Annatto, garlic, spices, and sour orange are blended into a paste that coats pork or chicken.

Authentic touch. Grind or blend the annatto with garlic and sour orange into a wet paste. Rub it on the meat and marinate. If you wrap in banana leaf, warm the leaf so it does not crack.

How it is used

Slow-cook the marinated meat until tender and serve it with tortillas and pickled onion.

Field notes

  • The red is annatto, not paprika alone.
  • Sour orange is the traditional acid of the Yucatán paste.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons annatto seeds, ground, or 1/4 cup achiote paste
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/2 cup sour orange juice or half lime and half orange
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend all the ingredients into a wet paste.
  2. Rub it over pork shoulder.
  3. Refrigerate at least 4 hours.
  4. Cook covered until tender.
  5. Serve with tortillas.

Cook the meat through. Do not eat the raw paste.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Recado blanco and recado negro are different Yucatecan pastes.
  • Equal parts lime and orange approximate sour orange.

  • Meat
  • Grilling
  • Tacos and tortillas

  • warm tortillas

Mojo Criollo

Sour orange, garlic, cumin, and oil: sharp, garlicky, and savory.

15 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Mojo criollo is the Cuban garlic and sour-orange sauce, also called Cuban mojo. It goes on yuca and is the marinade for roast pork. Sour orange, naranja agria, is the traditional acid. Lime and orange together are the usual stand-in when sour oranges are scarce. It is not the Spanish word for a gravy in general, and it is not the mojito drink.

Cultural background

Lechón and Christmas pork in Cuban cooking are tied to mojo. The boiled yuca on the table gets the same sauce, often heated so the garlic softens.

Why it tastes this way

Sour orange and a lot of garlic are the taste. Sour orange is sharper than sweet orange. Cumin and oil round it. The sauce is both a pork marinade and a spooning sauce for yuca.

How it is made

Garlic and cumin are pounded, mixed with sour orange juice, and combined with oil. A short warm-up blooms the garlic for the table version.

Authentic touch. If you cannot buy sour oranges, use equal parts lime and orange juice. Warm the oil and pour it over the garlic so the garlic softens without burning.

How it is used

Marinate pork in it, and spoon more over boiled yuca.

Field notes

  • Mojo the sauce and mojito the drink share a word history around moisture and sauce, and they are not the same recipe.
  • Sour orange is the acid that makes the marinade taste right.

Ingredients

  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/3 cup sour orange juice, or half lime and half orange
  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon oregano

Steps

  1. Mash the garlic with the salt and cumin.
  2. Stir in the juice and oregano.
  3. Warm the oil, pour it over the garlic, and stir.
  4. Use it as a pork marinade for several hours.
  5. Spoon more over boiled yuca at the table.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Puerto Rican mojito isleño adds tomato, pepper, and olives for fish.
  • Equal parts lime and sweet orange approximate sour orange.

Chimichurri

Parsley, garlic, oregano, vinegar, and olive oil: green, sharp, and herbal.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Chimichurri is the uncooked herb sauce of Argentina and Uruguay, served with grilled beef. Folk etymologies for the name, including stories about English or Irish speakers, are not reliable and are left out. The sauce itself is a documented everyday condiment of asado.

Cultural background

It is spooned over steak at the table and sometimes used as a marinade. Red chimichurri, with tomato and more chile, is a variation. Chilean pebre is a different salsa.

Why it tastes this way

Parsley, garlic, oregano, vinegar, and oil stay raw, so the sauce tastes fresh and sharp against charred beef. It is a finishing sauce for asado, not a cooked gravy.

How it is made

Parsley, garlic, oregano, vinegar, and oil are chopped and stirred, then rested so the garlic softens.

Authentic touch. Chop by hand if you can. A long blend turns the parsley dark and bitter.

How it is used

Spoon it on grilled steak, chorizo, or vegetables. It is a finishing sauce.

Field notes

  • Argentina and Uruguay both claim chimichurri as everyday asado sauce.
  • Name-origin stories are too shaky to repeat as fact.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup finely chopped parsley
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/2 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 3 tablespoons red wine vinegar
  • 1/2 cup olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir the parsley, garlic, oregano, and chile.
  2. Add the vinegar and salt.
  3. Stir in the oil.
  4. Rest 20 minutes.
  5. Spoon over grilled meat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chimichurri rojo adds tomato, paprika, and more chile.
  • A blender makes it smoother and less traditional.

Pairings and uses

Ají Amarillo Sauce

Yellow ají, onion, garlic, and oil: fruity, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Ají amarillo is the yellow chile at the center of much Peruvian cooking. A simple cooked sauce of the chile, onion, garlic, and oil is a base for stews and a table sauce. The chile is fruity rather than only hot. Pastes sold in jars are the practical way to cook it outside the Andes.

Cultural background

Peruvian kitchens use ají amarillo in causa, papa a la huancaína’s cousin sauces, and everyday saltados. This is the plain cooked chile sauce, not the cheese sauce huancaína.

Why it tastes this way

Ají amarillo tastes fruity as well as hot. Frying the paste with onion and garlic cooks off the raw edge and leaves that fruit in a savory sauce.

How it is made

Onion and garlic soften in oil, then ají paste or soaked dried ají is fried and loosened with water.

Authentic touch. Fry the paste in oil before you add water. Skipping the fry leaves a raw, dusty taste.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled chicken, potatoes, or rice, or stir a spoonful into a stew.

Field notes

  • Ají amarillo tastes fruity. Heat is only part of the point.
  • Jarred paste is a legitimate ingredient when fresh pods are unavailable.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/4 cup ají amarillo paste
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the garlic and ají paste and fry 2 minutes.
  3. Stir in the water and salt and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. Add the vinegar.
  5. Cool slightly and use as a spooning sauce.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Crema de ají blends the chile with mayonnaise and is milder.
  • Huancaína adds cheese and crackers and is a different sauce.

  • Rice

Llajwa

Locoto chile and tomato, pounded: very hot and fresh.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Llajwa is the Bolivian table salsa of locoto chile and tomato, often with quirquiña, a local herb. It is pounded, not cooked, and served with grilled meat and salteñas. Locoto is very hot. Cilantro is the stand-in when quirquiña is unavailable, and it is not the same herb.

Cultural background

A meal without llajwa is unfinished in many Bolivian homes. The mortar texture is coarse. The heat is the chile, used in quantity.

Why it tastes this way

Raw locoto is fruity and extremely hot. Tomato and salt make a juicy base. Quirquiña, when used, tastes herbal and a little sharp. Cooking would dull that raw heat.

How it is made

Tomato, locoto or habanero, and salt are pounded with an herb.

Authentic touch. Pound tomato and chile with salt in a mortar. If you cannot find quirquiña, say so and use cilantro, knowing the aroma changes.

How it is used

Serve it with grilled meat or salteñas.

Field notes

  • Locoto is the chile. Habanero is only a stand-in.
  • It is pounded and raw.

Ingredients

  • 2 tomatoes
  • 1 or 2 locoto or habanero chiles
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro

Steps

  1. Pound the tomato, chile, and salt to a coarse salsa.
  2. Stir in the herb.
  3. Taste carefully.
  4. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A green llajwa uses tomatillo-like or hot green chiles.
  • Blending it smooth is less typical.

Pebre

Onion, tomato, cilantro, aji, and vinegar: fresh, sharp, and hot.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Pebre is the Chilean table salsa of onion, tomato or sometimes just onion, cilantro, aji chile, oil, and vinegar or lemon. It is served with bread, grilled meat, and empanadas. It is not Argentine chimichurri, which is parsley-heavy and usually tomato-free.

Cultural background

A bowl of pebre and bread starts many Chilean meals. The heat depends on the aji. Recipes vary by household more than by a single official formula.

Why it tastes this way

Chile, onion, tomato, cilantro, and vinegar are chopped, not cooked, so pebre tastes fresh and hot. Rinsing the onion keeps its raw bite from covering the chile and tomato.

How it is made

Onion is rinsed or salted, then mixed with tomato, cilantro, chile, vinegar, and oil. It is not cooked.

Authentic touch. Rinse the diced onion and drain it well. Then salt the bowl and let it sit so the juices come out.

How it is used

Eat it with bread, choripán, or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • Pebre is a Chilean salsa, not a name for chimichurri.
  • Rinsing the onion keeps the raw bite from taking over the bowl.

Ingredients

  • 1 small onion, finely diced and rinsed
  • 2 tomatoes, seeded and diced
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1 fresh aji or serrano chile, minced
  • 2 tablespoons red wine vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Rinse the diced onion under cold water and drain it well.
  2. Toss it with the tomato, cilantro, and chile.
  3. Add vinegar, oil, and salt.
  4. Rest 15 minutes.
  5. Serve with bread or grilled meat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some pebre skips tomato and is mostly onion and chile.
  • Chancho en piedra is a mashed Chilean tomato-chile sauce and a different recipe.

Hogao

Tomato and scallion cooked in oil: savory, sweet, and soft.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Hogao, or guiso, is the Colombian cooked base of long green onion or scallion and tomato. It starts beans, rice, and stews. The scallion is cooked until sweet. It is a sofrito-like sauce with its own allium.

Cultural background

A pot of beans without hogao tastes plain. The cook makes the sauce first and builds the dish on it. Cumin appears in some regions and is not in every pot.

Why it tastes this way

Scallion cooked slowly in oil tastes sweet and savory, and tomato cooked with it turns round. That sweetness is why hogao seasons mild beans and rice.

How it is made

Scallion fries in oil, then tomato simmers until thick.

Authentic touch. Use a lot of scallion, cook it in oil until soft before the tomato, and do not rush it.

How it is used

Stir it into beans or rice, or serve a spoonful on top.

Field notes

  • Long onion or scallion is the allium, not only yellow onion.
  • It is a base sauce, cooked before the beans.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • 4 tomatoes, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 garlic cloves

Steps

  1. Cook the scallion and garlic in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the tomatoes and salt.
  3. Simmer until thick, about 12 minutes.
  4. Use it as the base of beans or rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of cumin is regional.
  • Bell pepper can join the scallion.

Salsa Golf

Mayonnaise and ketchup: creamy, sweet, and pink.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Salsa golf is an Argentine sauce of mayonnaise and ketchup, associated with seafood restaurants in Mar del Plata in the 1920s. A story credits a young Luis Federico Leloir. That story is widely told and not treated here as proven. The sauce itself is the pink mix served with prawns and palm hearts.

Cultural background

It is a starter sauce. The ratio leans on mayonnaise. Ketchup colors and sweetens it. It is kin to fry sauce and to some burger sauces, with its own history.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise makes it rich. Ketchup makes it sweet and tangy. Together they taste mild enough for cold seafood. More ketchup makes it sweeter and less like the usual golf.

How it is made

Mayonnaise and ketchup are stirred together.

Authentic touch. Stir mayonnaise with a smaller amount of ketchup until pink. Chill it. Taste before you add anything else.

How it is used

Spoon it over cold prawns or hearts of palm.

Field notes

  • It is mayonnaise and ketchup, and the Mar del Plata seafood table made it famous.
  • The inventor story is often repeated and not relied on here.

Ingredients

  • 3/4 cup mayonnaise
  • 1/4 cup ketchup
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir everything until smooth and pink.
  2. Taste.
  3. Chill.
  4. Serve with cold seafood.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few drops of brandy or mustard appear in some restaurants.
  • Mustard makes it sharper.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Dipping
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • prawns
  • hearts of palm
  • fries
