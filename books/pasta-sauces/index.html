Aglio e Olio

Garlic, olive oil, and chile: fragrant, hot, and slick.

5 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · enough for 4 portions of pasta

History

Spaghetti aglio e olio is the pantry pasta of southern Italy: olive oil, garlic, and often dried chile, emulsified with pasta water. It is a sauce in the sense that the oil and water form a light coating. There is no tomato and no cheese in the classic bowl.

Cultural background

It is late-night and everyday food. The garlic should be golden, not burnt. Burnt garlic makes the whole pan bitter.

Why it tastes this way

Olive oil, garlic, and chile taste clean because there is no tomato or cheese. Starchy pasta water emulsifies the oil so it coats the noodles instead of sitting in a puddle.

How it is made

Garlic and chile warm in olive oil, then starchy pasta water is beaten in so the oil turns creamy.

Authentic touch. Keep the garlic pale gold. The moment it browns, start over. Toss hard with pasta water until the oil looks creamy.

How it is used

Toss it with spaghetti immediately. Cheese is optional and not the classic finish.

Field notes

  • The sauce is an emulsion of oil and pasta water, which is why a dry toss tastes oily and flat.
  • Golden garlic is the line. Dark garlic means start over.

Ingredients

  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 6 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1/2 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 12 ounces spaghetti
  • Salt
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley

Steps

  1. Boil the spaghetti in salted water.
  2. Warm the oil, garlic, and chile until the garlic is pale gold.
  3. Add a ladle of pasta water and the drained pasta.
  4. Toss until the oil looks creamy.
  5. Add parsley and serve.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Anchovy melted into the oil is a common and older-tasting variation.
  • Parsley at the end adds color and is usual.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • spaghetti
  • broccoli rabe
  • anchovy, as a variation
  • Vegetarian

Amatriciana

Guanciale, tomato, chile, and pecorino: savory, sweet, and sharp.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pasta portions

History

Sugo all’amatriciana comes from Amatrice, now in Lazio, and is also a Roman pasta sauce. Guanciale, tomato, chile, and pecorino are the core. White amatriciana, without tomato, is closer to gricia. The tomato version is the one most people mean. Onion in the sauce is debated between Amatrice and Rome. This recipe leaves the onion out.

Cultural background

It is served with bucatini or spaghetti. The pork fat, not olive oil, is the cooking fat. Pecorino goes on at the end.

Why it tastes this way

Rendered guanciale fat, tomato, and chile taste savory and slightly sweet. Pecorino at the end is sharp. The pork fat is the cooking fat, which is why it does not taste like an oil-and-tomato sauce.

How it is made

Guanciale renders, tomato and chile simmer in the fat, and the sauce is tossed with pasta and pecorino.

Authentic touch. Render the guanciale until the pieces are golden and the fat is liquid. Simmer the tomato in that fat, then add the cheese off the heat.

How it is used

Toss with bucatini and cover with pecorino.

Field notes

  • Amatrice and Rome both claim details of the sauce, including whether onion belongs.
  • Guanciale is the meat. Bacon changes the taste.

Ingredients

  • 4 ounces guanciale, sliced
  • 1/2 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 1 can (14 ounces) tomatoes, crushed
  • 12 ounces bucatini
  • 3/4 cup grated pecorino Romano

Steps

  1. Render the guanciale until golden. Leave the fat in the pan.
  2. Add the chile and tomato and simmer 15 minutes.
  3. Boil the bucatini.
  4. Toss the pasta with the sauce and a splash of pasta water.
  5. Add the pecorino off the heat and serve.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Gricia is the tomato-free ancestor.
  • A little onion is the Roman variation some cooks defend.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • bucatini
  • pecorino Romano
  • Nut-free

Arrabbiata

Tomato, garlic, and dried chile: hot, sharp, and bright.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Sugo all’arrabbiata is a Roman tomato sauce whose name means angry, from the chile. Garlic, dried chile, tomato, and olive oil are the core. Pecorino is often added at the table and is not cooked into the sauce. It is a twentieth-century Roman trattoria sauce with older hot-tomato roots.

Cultural background

It is pasta sauce, usually for penne. The anger is chile heat, not a long list of spices.

Why it tastes this way

Dried chile bloomed in oil with garlic is the heat. Tomato stays bright because the simmer is short. The anger in the name is that chile, not a long spice list.

How it is made

Chile and garlic bloom in oil, tomato simmers briefly, and the sauce stays loose enough to coat pasta.

Authentic touch. Bloom the chile flakes in the oil before the tomato so the heat perfumes the fat. Add cheese at the table if you want it, not in the pot.

How it is used

Toss with penne. Add pecorino at the table if you want it.

Field notes

  • Arrabbiata means angry, and the anger is dried chile.
  • Cheese is a topping, not a required ingredient in the pot.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 1 can (28 ounces) tomatoes, crushed
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 12 ounces penne

Steps

  1. Warm the oil, garlic, and chile until the garlic softens.
  2. Add the tomatoes and salt.
  3. Simmer 15 minutes.
  4. Toss with boiled penne and pasta water.
  5. Serve with pecorino if you like.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Marinara without chile is the mild relative.
  • A spoon of chile oil at the end makes it hotter without a longer cook.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • penne
  • pecorino
  • broccoli
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

  • Dairy-free

Béchamel

Milk, butter, and a white roux: smooth, lightly nutty, and savory.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Béchamel is one of the five French mother sauces in the repertoire associated with Auguste Escoffier. The name is tied to Louis de Béchamel, a financier at the court of Louis XIV, though the sauce of flour-thickened milk is older than that dedication. By the nineteenth century it was a standard base for gratins and for daughter sauces such as Mornay.

Cultural background

In French cooking it is a pantry technique as much as a sauce: the white sauce that carries cheese, nutmeg, or onion. Italian besciamella and Greek béchamel in pastitsio are close relatives with their own seasoning.

Why it tastes this way

The mild, milky taste is the liquid, not a spice. A white roux only cooks long enough to lose raw flour, so the sauce stays pale and sweet from milk instead of tasting toasted or meaty like a brown sauce.

How it is made

A white roux of butter and flour is whisked with warm milk and simmered until it coats a spoon.

Authentic touch. Add the milk warm and whisk constantly. Cold milk and a hot roux lump, and those lumps taste of raw starch.

How it is used

Use it under a gratin, in lasagna, or as the base of a cheese sauce for cauliflower, croque monsieur, or creamed spinach.

Field notes

  • Escoffier’s codification made béchamel a mother sauce, not a single restaurant recipe.
  • The Italian besciamella in lasagna alla bolognese is the same technique with a lighter hand on the nutmeg.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 2 cups whole milk, warmed
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • Pinch of white pepper
  • Pinch of nutmeg

Steps

  1. Melt the butter in a saucepan over medium heat.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook 1 to 2 minutes without browning.
  3. Whisk in the warm milk a little at a time until smooth.
  4. Simmer, whisking, until the sauce coats a spoon, about 8 minutes.
  5. Season with salt, white pepper, and nutmeg.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mornay stirs in Gruyère and Parmesan.
  • Soubise blends in a sweated onion puree.
  • A pinch of nutmeg is traditional; a bay leaf in the milk is optional.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Vegetables
  • Eggs
  • lasagna
  • cauliflower gratin
  • croque monsieur
  • creamed spinach
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Burro e Salvia

Browned butter and fried sage: nutty, herbal, and rich.

5 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pasta portions

History

Burro e salvia is the Italian brown-butter and sage sauce, a standard dressing for ravioli and pumpkin-filled pasta in the north. The butter is cooked until the milk solids brown, and sage leaves fry in that fat. It is a simple sauce with a narrow window between brown and burnt.

Cultural background

It is the sauce for filled pasta when a tomato or meat ragù would hide the filling. Parmesan is added at the table in many kitchens and is not required in the pan.

Why it tastes this way

Butter cooked until the milk solids brown tastes nutty, and sage fried in that butter tastes crisp and herbal. It is a sauce for filled pasta, light enough not to hide the filling.

How it is made

Butter browns, sage fries until crisp, and the pasta is tossed through the butter with a splash of pasta water.

Authentic touch. Brown the butter to a nutty smell and stop. A sharp smell means it has burned. Leave the sage leaves whole.

How it is used

Toss with ravioli or gnocchi and serve at once.

Field notes

  • The nutty smell is browned milk solids. If the pan smells sharp, the butter has gone too far.
  • Sage leaves left in the butter become the garnish.

Ingredients

  • 6 tablespoons butter
  • 12 sage leaves
  • Salt
  • 12 ounces ravioli or gnocchi

Steps

  1. Boil the pasta.
  2. Melt the butter and add the sage.
  3. Cook until the butter smells nutty and the sage is crisp.
  4. Take it off the heat.
  5. Toss with the drained pasta and a spoon of pasta water.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A squeeze of lemon makes it closer to French meunière.
  • Whole sage leaves, not a powder, are the texture people expect.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Vegetables
  • pumpkin ravioli
  • potato gnocchi
  • parmesan
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Cacio e Pepe

Pecorino and black pepper in starchy water: sharp, peppery, and creamy.

10 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pasta portions

History

Cacio e pepe is the Roman pasta sauce of pecorino Romano and black pepper, emulsified with pasta water. It has no egg, no cream, and no guanciale. It is the simplest of the Roman quartet and the base that gricia and carbonara build on. Shepherds’ stories about its origin are common and not needed to cook it.

Cultural background

The cheese must be finely grated or it clumps. The pepper is toasted in a dry pan in many Roman kitchens so it smells fragrant before the pasta arrives.

Why it tastes this way

Pecorino and black pepper, emulsified with starchy water, taste sharp and creamy without cream. The cheese is salty, so the pasta water should be only lightly salted. Coarse pepper, toasted, is the spice.

How it is made

Toasted pepper and very finely grated pecorino form a paste with hot pasta water, then the pasta is tossed in until the sauce turns creamy.

Authentic touch. Grate the cheese very fine and make a paste with hot water before the pasta goes in. Coarse shreds clump into strings.

How it is used

Serve it at once in warm bowls. It sets as it cools.

Field notes

  • There is no cream in the sauce. Starch from the pasta water is what makes it look creamy.
  • Pecorino that is grated too coarsely will turn into strings instead of a sauce.

Ingredients

  • 12 ounces tonnarelli or spaghetti
  • 1 1/2 cups finely grated pecorino Romano
  • 2 teaspoons coarse black pepper
  • Salt for the pasta water

Steps

  1. Toast the pepper in a dry pan until fragrant.
  2. Boil the pasta in water that is only lightly salted, because the cheese is salty.
  3. Put the cheese in a bowl and stir in a ladle of hot pasta water to make a paste.
  4. Toss the drained pasta with the pepper and the cheese paste, adding water until creamy.
  5. Serve at once.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Gricia adds rendered guanciale.
  • A handful of Parmesan makes the emulsion easier and less purely Roman.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • tonnarelli
  • spaghetti
  • more pecorino
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Carbonara

Guanciale, egg, pecorino, and black pepper: rich, salty, and sharp.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pasta portions

History

Carbonara is a Roman pasta sauce of guanciale, egg, pecorino Romano, and black pepper. Cream is not part of the Roman version. Stories that tie the dish to American rations in the 1940s are disputed, so this page does not repeat them as fact. The method is an emulsion of egg and cheese with hot pasta and rendered pork fat.

Cultural background

It is one of the four famous Roman pasta sauces, with cacio e pepe, gricia, and amatriciana. Guanciale, cured pork jowl, is the meat. Pancetta is the usual substitute.

Why it tastes this way

Cured pork fat, egg, and pecorino emulsify with hot pasta. There is no cream. The gloss is egg and cheese set by residual heat. Too much heat scrambles the egg and the sauce tastes dry.

How it is made

Guanciale renders, pasta is tossed with it off the heat, and beaten egg and pecorino cook in the residual heat into a cream.

Authentic touch. Take the pan off the heat before the egg goes in. Toss until glossy. Guanciale, or pancetta if that is what you can buy, should be rendered, not boiled.

How it is used

Eat it at once from the pan. It does not reheat well.

Field notes

  • Roman carbonara has no cream. The creaminess is egg, cheese, and pasta water.
  • The egg should thicken, not scramble. The pan has to be off the boil.

Ingredients

  • 4 ounces guanciale or pancetta, diced
  • 12 ounces spaghetti
  • 3 egg yolks and 1 whole egg
  • 3/4 cup finely grated pecorino Romano
  • 1 teaspoon coarse black pepper
  • Salt for the pasta water

Steps

  1. Render the guanciale until the fat is liquid and the pieces are golden. Turn off the heat.
  2. Boil the pasta. Mix the eggs, cheese, and pepper in a bowl.
  3. Drain the pasta, reserving water, and toss it with the guanciale off the heat.
  4. Stir in the egg mixture and a splash of pasta water until glossy.
  5. Serve immediately with more pepper.

The egg is cooked only by the hot pasta. Use fresh eggs and eat the dish at once.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Gricia is the same idea without egg.
  • A cream carbonara is a later, non-Roman version.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • spaghetti or rigatoni
  • pecorino Romano
  • black pepper
  • Nut-free

Cincinnati Chili Sauce

Fine beef, cinnamon, cocoa, and chile: savory, sweet-spiced, and thin.

15 min prep · 1 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 cups

History

Cincinnati chili is a sauce of finely ground beef and warm spices served over spaghetti. The usual account traces the style to Macedonian and Greek immigrant chili parlors, including Empress Chili, opened in 1922 by Tom and John Kiradjieff. It is not a German sauce, and it is not a Texas bowl of chili. Beans, if they appear, are a topping on a four-way or five-way, not the body of the sauce. Cheese and chopped onion are toppings too.

Cultural background

A two-way is spaghetti and chili. A three-way adds cheese. The sauce is thin because it has to coat noodles. Cinnamon, allspice or clove, and a little cocoa are the spice cabinet of those parlors.

Why it tastes this way

The beef is simmered until it breaks into a fine sauce, so it tastes savory without chunks. Chile is mild. Cinnamon and cocoa taste sweet and bitter, which is why people who expect only cumin think this sauce is spiced like a stew from the eastern Mediterranean. Tomato and a long simmer make it loose enough for spaghetti. Worcestershire, where it is used, adds anchovy salt.

How it is made

Ground beef simmers in water or stock with tomato, chile, cinnamon, cocoa, and other spices until it is a thin sauce.

Authentic touch. Simmer the beef until it is fine and saucy, not chunky. Keep beans out of the pot. Put them on top only if you are making a four-way. Do not call it German.

How it is used

Ladle it over spaghetti. Add cheddar and onion for a three-way. Beans stay on the side or on top.

Field notes

  • The 1922 Empress account is the usual origin story of the parlor style.
  • The sauce is Greek and Macedonian American cooking. It is not German heritage chili.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound ground beef
  • 1 onion, minced
  • 2 cups water
  • 1 cup crushed tomato
  • 2 tablespoons chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon cocoa powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cinnamon
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground allspice
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 tablespoon Worcestershire sauce
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Stir the raw beef into the water and break it up as it comes to a simmer.
  2. Add the onion, tomato, spices, cocoa, Worcestershire, and salt.
  3. Simmer gently about 1 hour, until the beef is fine and the sauce is loose.
  4. Thin with water if it gets thicker than pasta sauce.
  5. Serve over spaghetti. Add cheese, onion, or beans on top, not in the pot.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A three-way adds finely shredded cheddar.
  • A four-way adds beans or onion on top. A five-way adds both.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Meat
  • spaghetti
  • cheddar
  • oyster crackers
  • onion
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Gricia

Guanciale, pecorino, and black pepper: salty, porky, and sharp.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pasta portions

History

Gricia is the Roman pasta sauce of guanciale, pecorino, and black pepper, without tomato and without egg. It sits between cacio e pepe and carbonara. Amatrice’s tomato sauce is the one that adds tomato to this idea. The name’s origin is uncertain and is left out.

Cultural background

It is trattoria food, often rigatoni or spaghetti. The rendered fat has to meet cheese and pasta water or the bowl tastes greasy.

Why it tastes this way

Without tomato or egg, you taste pork fat, pecorino, and pepper. Pasta water is what makes those three look creamy. If the pan is too hot, the cheese clumps and the fat stays greasy.

How it is made

Guanciale renders, then pasta, pecorino, pepper, and pasta water are tossed off the heat into a creamy sauce.

Authentic touch. Toss off a hard boil and add pasta water until the cheese and fat turn into a sauce. Do not add cream to fix it.

How it is used

Serve immediately with more pecorino and pepper.

Field notes

  • Gricia has neither tomato nor egg. That is the whole distinction.
  • The cheese and the pork fat emulsify only if the pan is not screaming hot.

Ingredients

  • 4 ounces guanciale, diced
  • 12 ounces rigatoni
  • 1 cup finely grated pecorino Romano
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Render the guanciale until the fat runs and the pieces are crisp.
  2. Boil the rigatoni.
  3. Toss the pasta with the guanciale, fat, pepper, cheese, and a ladle of pasta water off a hard boil.
  4. Add water until the sauce looks creamy.
  5. Serve at once.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Add egg and it moves toward carbonara.
  • Add tomato and it moves toward amatriciana.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • rigatoni
  • pecorino Romano
  • black pepper
  • Nut-free

Koshari Sauce

Tomato, vinegar, garlic, and cumin: tangy, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

The tomato sauce on Egyptian koshari is a sharp, spiced sauce of tomato, garlic, vinegar, and cumin, ladled over the rice, lentils, and pasta. Koshari is Egypt’s famous street bowl. The sauce is cooked and often finished with a garlic-vinegar daqqa. It is not an Italian pasta sauce.

Cultural background

A bowl of koshari without the red sauce and the hot sauce on the side is incomplete. Cumin and vinegar are what make it taste like the street version.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato cooked with cumin, garlic, and vinegar tastes tangy and savory. The vinegar is why it cuts a heavy bowl of rice, lentils, and pasta. Cumin is the spice.

How it is made

Onion and garlic fry, tomato and cumin simmer, and vinegar is stirred in at the end.

Authentic touch. Add the vinegar near the end and taste. The sauce should be sharp, not only tomato-sweet.

How it is used

Ladle it over koshari: rice, lentils, and pasta.

Field notes

  • Koshari sauce is defined by vinegar and cumin as much as by tomato.
  • The dish stacks rice, lentils, and pasta under this sauce.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 1 can (14 ounces) crushed tomatoes
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon chili flakes

Steps

  1. Fry the onion in the oil until golden.
  2. Add the garlic, tomato paste, and cumin and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes, salt, and chile and simmer 15 minutes.
  4. Stir in the vinegar.
  5. Serve over rice, lentils, and pasta.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A separate garlic-vinegar splash is the hotter street finish.
  • More chile makes a sauce closer to the hot bottle beside the tomato one.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta

  • lentils
  • fried onions

Marinara

Tomato, garlic, and olive oil, cooked briefly: bright and savory.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Marinara is the quick southern Italian tomato sauce of garlic, olive oil, tomato, and often oregano. The name is associated with sailors and with a sauce that could be made fast. It is not the long ragù and it is not the French mother sauce tomate. In the United States the same word often means any red pizza or pasta sauce.

Cultural background

Neapolitan cooking uses it on pasta and as a base. Oregano is common. A handful of basil is the other usual finish. Meat is not part of it.

Why it tastes this way

Garlic in olive oil and a short tomato simmer taste bright because the tomato is not cooked for hours and there is no meat. Oregano or basil is the herb. A long ragù is a different sauce.

How it is made

Garlic softens in olive oil, tomato cooks briefly, and the sauce is seasoned and finished with a herb.

Authentic touch. Soften the garlic without browning it. Brown garlic makes the whole pot bitter. Stop while the tomato still tastes fresh.

How it is used

Toss it with pasta or use it as a pizza sauce. It should still taste of tomato.

Field notes

  • Marinara is a quick sauce. A four-hour simmer is a different tomato sauce.
  • American menus sometimes use the word for any red sauce.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1 can (28 ounces) whole tomatoes, crushed
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 6 basil leaves

Steps

  1. Warm the oil and garlic until the garlic smells sweet, not brown.
  2. Add the tomatoes and salt.
  3. Simmer 20 minutes until slightly thick.
  4. Stir in the oregano and basil.
  5. Toss with pasta and a little pasta water.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of chile makes it closer to arrabbiata, which is its own sauce.
  • A long Sunday meat sauce is a different recipe.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Dipping
  • Vegetables
  • spaghetti
  • pizza
  • fried eggplant
  • Vegetarian

Pesto alla Genovese

Raw basil, garlic, pine nuts, cheese, and olive oil: green, sharp, and rich.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Pesto alla Genovese is the basil sauce of Liguria. The name comes from pestare, to pound. The modern form, with basil, garlic, pine nuts, salt, Ligurian olive oil, and a mix of Parmesan and a hard Sardinian or local sheep cheese, was fixed in Genoese cooking in the nineteenth century. A mortar gives a creamier sauce than a blender.

Cultural background

It is a Ligurian sauce for trofie, trenette, and green beans with potato, not a generic herb paste. Other Italian pestos, such as Trapanese, are different recipes.

Why it tastes this way

Raw basil, garlic, pine nuts, cheese, and Ligurian olive oil are the flavor. Heat is not part of it. Pounding bruises the basil less than a long blend, so the sauce stays green and sweet instead of dark and bitter.

How it is made

Basil is pounded with salt, garlic, and pine nuts, then cheese and olive oil are worked in. Heat is not part of the sauce.

Authentic touch. Pound or pulse briefly and stop while it is still bright. Toss it with pasta off the boil. Boiling pesto turns it dull.

How it is used

Toss with pasta and a little pasta water, or stir into minestrone. Do not cook the pesto on a hard boil or the basil turns dull.

Field notes

  • Classic Genoese pesto is pounded, which bruises the basil less harshly than a long blend.
  • Pine nuts are traditional. Walnuts make a different Ligurian sauce, salsa di noci.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups packed fresh basil leaves
  • 1 small garlic clove
  • 2 tablespoons pine nuts
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup extra-virgin olive oil
  • 1/4 cup finely grated Parmesan
  • 2 tablespoons finely grated pecorino

Steps

  1. Pound the garlic and salt to a paste, then the pine nuts.
  2. Add the basil in handfuls and pound to a coarse green paste.
  3. Stir in the cheeses.
  4. Stream in the oil until the sauce is thick and spoonable.
  5. Toss with hot pasta and a splash of pasta water. Do not boil the pesto.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pesto alla Trapanese uses tomato and almonds and is a separate Sicilian sauce.
  • A blender works if you keep it cold and do not overmix the basil into a dark paste.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Vegetables
  • trofie
  • trenette with potatoes and green beans
  • minestrone
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free

Pesto alla Trapanese

Tomato, almond, basil, and garlic: fresh, nutty, and lightly sweet.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 2 cups

History

Pesto alla Trapanese is the Sicilian basil sauce of Trapani, made with tomato and almonds rather than the pine nuts of Genoa. It is a raw sauce for pasta, especially busiate. Almonds reflect Sicilian pantry cooking. It is not a cooked tomato sauce with nuts stirred in.

Cultural background

Western Sicily serves it in summer when tomatoes and basil are both good. Pecorino is usual. The texture should stay a little coarse.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato and almond, with basil, taste fresher and nuttier than Genoese pesto because the almond and raw tomato replace pine nuts. It is a summer sauce. The tomato should be ripe.

How it is made

Almonds, garlic, basil, and tomato are pounded or briefly pulsed with olive oil and cheese.

Authentic touch. Keep it coarse. A smooth puree tastes flatter. Toss it with hot pasta and a splash of pasta water, without cooking the sauce.

How it is used

Toss with busiate or other short pasta and a spoon of pasta water.

Field notes

  • Trapani’s pesto is a tomato and almond sauce, not a basil copy of Genoa.
  • It stays raw, so the tomato should be ripe.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup blanched almonds
  • 1 garlic clove
  • 1 cup basil leaves
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, seeded and chopped
  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 1/4 cup grated pecorino
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Pound or pulse the almonds and garlic.
  2. Add the basil and tomatoes and chop to a coarse sauce.
  3. Stir in the oil, cheese, and salt.
  4. Toss with hot pasta.
  5. Loosen with pasta water.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Genoese pesto has no tomato and uses pine nuts.
  • A fully blended smooth paste is less typical.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • busiate
  • ripe tomatoes
  • pecorino
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free

Pistou

Basil, garlic, olive oil, and cheese: bright and sharp, without nuts.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3/4 cup

History

Pistou is the Provençal basil sauce stirred into soupe au pistou. It is close to Genoese pesto and usually omits pine nuts. The word is related to the same pounding idea. Cheese and oil finish the pounded basil and garlic.

Cultural background

The soup is a summer vegetable soup. The pistou is added at the table so the basil stays raw. It is also tossed with pasta in home cooking.

Why it tastes this way

Basil, garlic, oil, and cheese, with no pine nuts, taste brighter and less rich than Genoese pesto. It is stirred into hot soup off the boil so the basil stays raw and green.

How it is made

Basil and garlic are pounded with salt, then cheese and olive oil are stirred in.

Authentic touch. Add it to the soup in the bowl, not during a long simmer. Boiling pistou dulls the basil.

How it is used

Stir a spoonful into hot vegetable soup off the boil, or toss it with pasta.

Field notes

  • Classic pistou has no pine nuts, which is the practical difference from Genoese pesto.
  • It is stirred into the soup at the end, not boiled for a long time.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups basil leaves
  • 1 garlic clove
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 1/4 cup grated Parmesan

Steps

  1. Pound the garlic and salt, then the basil.
  2. Stir in the cheese.
  3. Stir in the oil.
  4. Taste for salt.
  5. Add it to hot soup off the heat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Genoese pesto adds pine nuts and is the Ligurian sauce.
  • Tomato in the paste makes a different condiment.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • soupe au pistou
  • summer vegetables

Ragù alla Bolognese

Slow meat, milk, wine, and a little tomato: savory, soft, and rich.

25 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 6 cups

History

Ragù alla Bolognese is the meat sauce of Bologna, served with tagliatelle or used in lasagna. In 1982 the Accademia Italiana della Cucina deposited a recipe with the Bologna Chamber of Commerce: beef, pancetta, soffritto, wine, milk, and a modest amount of tomato. That registered recipe is a useful reference. It is not a heavily tomatoed Italian-American Sunday gravy.

Cultural background

In Bologna it is a meat sauce first. Tomato seasons it. Milk is part of the traditional pot, not a modern shortcut. Wide fresh egg pasta is the usual partner, not spaghetti.

Why it tastes this way

A slow meat cook with soffritto, wine, milk, and only a little tomato tastes savory and soft, not sharply tomatoed. Milk rounds the meat. The registered Bologna approach keeps tomato as a seasoning.

How it is made

A soffritto and ground or finely chopped meat brown slowly, then wine, milk, and tomato simmer until the sauce is thick and soft.

Authentic touch. Cook the meat gently after it loses its raw color. A hard crust dries it. Give it the long simmer, and toss it with tagliatelle, not a huge amount of sauce.

How it is used

Toss it with tagliatelle, or layer it in lasagna with béchamel.

Field notes

  • A 1982 registered Bolognese recipe includes milk and only a little tomato.
  • Tagliatelle, not spaghetti, is the local pasta.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 onion, diced
  • 1 carrot, diced
  • 1 celery stalk, diced
  • 4 ounces pancetta, minced
  • 1 pound ground beef
  • 1/2 cup dry white wine
  • 1 cup whole milk
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1 cup crushed tomato
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Cook the onion, carrot, celery, and pancetta in the butter and oil until soft.
  2. Add the beef and cook until it loses raw color. Do not rush it into a hard crust.
  3. Add the wine and let it nearly cook off. Add the milk and simmer until it is mostly absorbed.
  4. Add the tomato paste and crushed tomato. Simmer very low for about 2 hours, adding water if it dries.
  5. Salt at the end and toss with tagliatelle.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sunday gravy is the Italian-American long tomato sauce with meat pieces and is a different sauce.
  • Pork mixed with beef is common in home pots.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Meat
  • tagliatelle
  • lasagna
  • fresh egg pasta
  • Nut-free

Salsa di Noci

Walnut, bread, milk, and marjoram: earthy, creamy, and herbal.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Salsa di noci is the Ligurian walnut sauce for pansotti and other filled pasta. Walnuts are pounded with garlic, bread soaked in milk, and often marjoram or parsley, then loosened with oil and cheese. It is a pantry sauce of the same coast as basil pesto and is not a nut version of that pesto.

Cultural background

Ligurian cooking uses walnuts where other regions might use pine nuts. The bread and milk make it pale and creamy. It is served at room temperature on hot pasta.

Why it tastes this way

Walnuts, bread soaked in milk, and marjoram taste creamy and earthy. The bread and milk are the Ligurian way to loosen nuts into a sauce. It is not basil pesto with a nut swapped in.

How it is made

Soaked bread, walnuts, and garlic are blended with oil and cheese into a thick sauce.

Authentic touch. Soak the bread, squeeze it, and grind the walnuts fine. Thin the sauce with pasta water when you toss it, not with more oil alone.

How it is used

Toss with pansotti or another stuffed pasta. Thin with pasta water.

Field notes

  • This is Liguria’s other famous pasta sauce, beside basil pesto.
  • Milk-soaked bread is a traditional thickener, not a frugal afterthought.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup walnuts
  • 1 slice white bread, crust removed, soaked in 1/3 cup milk
  • 1 small garlic clove
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 2 tablespoons grated Parmesan
  • 1 teaspoon chopped marjoram
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Squeeze the bread. Blend it with the walnuts and garlic.
  2. Stream in the oil.
  3. Stir in the cheese, marjoram, and salt.
  4. Thin with pasta water when you toss it.
  5. Serve at room temperature on hot pasta.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little marjoram is the Ligurian herb. Basil makes it closer to pesto.
  • Skipping the bread makes a denser nut paste.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • pansotti
  • ravioli
  • marjoram
  • Vegetarian

Salsa Puttanesca

Tomato, anchovy, caper, olive, and garlic: salty, briny, and hot.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Sugo alla puttanesca is a southern Italian tomato sauce of anchovy, capers, olives, garlic, and chile. The name’s folk history is disputed and is left out here. The sauce was popular in Italian cooking by the mid-twentieth century and is built from pantry stores.

Cultural background

It is a fast pasta sauce from ingredients that keep: salted fish, olives, capers, dried chile, and tomato. Gaeta olives are traditional when you can get them.

Why it tastes this way

Anchovy, capers, and olives are preserved foods, so the sauce tastes salty and briny. Tomato and chile bind them. It was a pantry sauce, which is why it comes together quickly.

How it is made

Anchovy melts in oil with garlic and chile, then tomato, capers, and olives simmer briefly.

Authentic touch. Melt the anchovy into the oil until it dissolves. Taste before you salt. The pantry ingredients carry the salt.

How it is used

Toss with spaghetti. It is already salty, so taste before adding salt.

Field notes

  • The sauce is designed around pantry fish and pickles, which is why it comes together quickly.
  • Stories about the name are too unsettled to treat as history.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 4 anchovy fillets
  • 1/2 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 1 can (28 ounces) tomatoes, crushed
  • 1/3 cup chopped black olives
  • 2 tablespoons capers
  • Parsley

Steps

  1. Warm the oil and melt the anchovy with the garlic and chile.
  2. Add the tomatoes and simmer 12 minutes.
  3. Stir in the olives and capers.
  4. Toss with spaghetti.
  5. Finish with parsley. Add salt only if it needs it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Without anchovy it is a different, meatless olive-tomato sauce.
  • A hotter chile level is a household choice.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • spaghetti
  • Gaeta olives
  • parsley
  • Dairy-free
  • Gluten-free

Sauce Mornay

Béchamel enriched with Gruyère and Parmesan: cheesy, smooth, and savory.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Mornay is a daughter of béchamel finished with cheese, usually Gruyère and Parmesan. It is a nineteenth-century French restaurant sauce, named in the repertoire rather than invented as a folk recipe. It is the sauce under many French gratins.

Cultural background

French cooking uses it for eggs, fish, and vegetables that go under a broiler. It is the direct ancestor of the cheese sauce on a croque monsieur.

Why it tastes this way

It tastes of cheese because Gruyère and Parmesan melt into a milk sauce. The béchamel underneath keeps it smooth. The cheese goes in off the boil so it melts instead of turning grainy.

How it is made

A béchamel is taken off the boil and stirred with grated firm cheese until the cheese melts.

Authentic touch. Take the pan off the heat before the cheese. If the sauce boils after the cheese is in, it can split and taste oily.

How it is used

Spoon it over steamed vegetables, eggs, or fish and brown it briefly.

Field notes

  • Mornay is béchamel plus cheese, not a roux-free cheese melt.
  • The cheese should melt off the boil so the sauce stays smooth.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons flour
  • 2 cups warm milk
  • 1/2 cup grated Gruyère
  • 2 tablespoons grated Parmesan
  • Salt
  • Pinch of nutmeg

Steps

  1. Cook the butter and flour 1 minute, then whisk in the milk.
  2. Simmer until the béchamel coats a spoon.
  3. Take it off the heat and stir in the cheeses.
  4. Season with salt and nutmeg.
  5. Use it at once under a gratin.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little mustard makes a sharper cheese sauce.
  • Cheddar makes a different, British-leaning sauce.

  • Eggs
  • Seafood

Sauce Tomate

Tomato cooked with stock, aromatics, and a little roux: round and savory rather than bright.

15 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

Sauce tomate is the tomato mother sauce of the French system, distinct from quick Italian marinara. Classic versions in the Escoffier lineage use salt pork or bacon, a roux, stock, and a long simmer. Tomato had already entered French cooking in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries before it was fixed as a mother sauce.

Cultural background

French tomato sauce is a cooked sauce for meat, eggs, and filled pasta. It is thicker and more savory than a raw salsa and less herbal than many Italian sughi.

Why it tastes this way

French sauce tomate tastes cooked and savory because the tomato simmers with pork fat, stock, and a little flour. The long cook turns sharp tomato into a round sauce. That is different from a quick Italian marinara.

How it is made

A small roux and rendered pork cook with tomato and stock until the sauce is thick and sweet-savory.

Authentic touch. Simmer until the raw tomato edge is gone. If it still tastes like the can, it needs more time, not more salt.

How it is used

Serve with grilled meats, eggs, stuffed vegetables, or as a base under a cheese gratin.

Field notes

  • In the French mother-sauce list, tomato sauce sits beside the roux sauces and hollandaise.
  • A long simmer is the point: the sauce should taste cooked, not like fresh tomato water.

Ingredients

  • 2 ounces salt pork or bacon, diced
  • 1 small onion, diced
  • 1 tablespoon all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1 can (28 ounces) whole tomatoes, crushed by hand
  • 1 cup beef or chicken stock
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Render the pork and cook the onion in the fat until soft.
  2. Stir in the flour and tomato paste and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes, stock, bay leaf, and sugar.
  4. Simmer 40 minutes until thick.
  5. Remove the bay, season with salt, and blend if you want it smooth.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the pork and use oil and vegetable stock for a different, meatless sauce.
  • Italian marinara is a separate, quicker sauce and is not this mother sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Meat
  • Vegetables
  • grilled pork
  • eggs
  • stuffed cabbage
  • gnocchi
  • Nut-free

Sunday Gravy

Long-cooked tomato and pork: savory, sweet, and rich.

25 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 cups

History

Sunday gravy is the Italian-American tomato sauce simmered for hours with meatballs, sausage, and often braciole or pork ribs. The tomato becomes sweet. The meat sauces the pasta and is served beside it. It is not ragù alla Bolognese, which is a finer meat sauce with less tomato.

Cultural background

The Sunday pot is a family meal. The sauce is tomato-heavy on purpose. A short marinara will not taste like it.

Why it tastes this way

Hours of simmering turn tomato sweet and let pork fat and gelatin enrich it. The meat is partly the flavoring. That is why a meatless quick sauce tastes brighter and thinner.

How it is made

Pork and beef brown, then simmer in tomato with garlic and basil until the sauce is thick.

Authentic touch. Brown the meats, then simmer them in tomato low and slow for at least two hours. Do not rush it into a weeknight marinara.

How it is used

Toss pasta with the sauce and serve the meats on the side.

Field notes

  • Sunday gravy is Italian-American, tomato-heavy, and long-cooked.
  • The meats flavor the sauce and are also the second course.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 pound Italian sausage
  • 1 pound pork ribs or beef meatballs
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 2 cans (28 ounces) tomatoes, crushed
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 6 basil leaves

Steps

  1. Brown the meats in the oil and set them aside.
  2. Cook the onion and garlic.
  3. Add the tomatoes, salt, and bay and return the meats.
  4. Simmer very low for 2 hours.
  5. Add basil. Serve the sauce with pasta and the meats beside it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Bolognese is the Emilian meat sauce with less tomato.
  • Marinara is the quick meatless sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Meat
  • ziti
  • meatballs
  • sausage

Vodka Sauce

Tomato, cream, and a splash of vodka: smooth, rich, and lightly sharp.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3 cups

History

Penne alla vodka is a tomato-cream sauce with a small amount of vodka, popular in Italian-American restaurants in the late twentieth century. Several origin claims exist in Italy and the United States, and they disagree, so this page names no inventor. The vodka’s role is to sharpen the tomato and help carry flavor. It should cook off.

Cultural background

It is a restaurant pasta sauce, pink from tomato and cream. A heavy cream hand makes it dull. The tomato should still taste present.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato is the acid and the color. Cream rounds it. A little vodka, cooked down, lifts the tomato instead of leaving a boozy taste. Onion and chile flake are the usual savory notes.

How it is made

Onion, tomato, and vodka simmer, then cream finishes the sauce.

Authentic touch. Simmer the vodka for a minute so the alcohol cooks off, then add cream off a hard boil. The sauce should stay pink, not white.

How it is used

Toss it with penne.

Field notes

  • Origin stories disagree, so none is treated as fact.
  • The vodka should be cooked, not tasted as alcohol.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1/4 cup vodka
  • 2 cups tomato puree
  • 1/2 cup cream
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Pinch of chile flakes

Steps

  1. Cook the onion and garlic in the butter.
  2. Add the vodka and simmer 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomato, salt, and chile and simmer 12 minutes.
  4. Stir in the cream and heat gently.
  5. Toss with penne.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of chile is common.
  • Without the vodka it is a different pink sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • penne
  • basil
  • Parmesan
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

White Clam Sauce

Clam, garlic, white wine, and olive oil: briny and sharp.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 pasta portions

History

White clam sauce is the Italian-American pasta sauce of clams, garlic, olive oil, and often white wine, associated with coastal cities and with linguine. Red clam sauce adds tomato. The white version tastes of the clam liquor. It is a fast sauce.

Cultural background

Linguine alle vongole in Italy is the related dish. The American white sauce often uses canned clam juice when fresh littlenecks are out of season. The juice is the flavor.

Why it tastes this way

Clam liquor is salty and oceanic. Garlic and chile in olive oil perfume it. Wine sharpens it. A cream sauce would hide the brine, which is why the classic white sauce has little or no cream.

How it is made

Garlic and chile warm in oil, clam juice and wine reduce, and clams and parsley finish the pan.

Authentic touch. Add the clam juice and let it reduce a little. Put the clam meats in at the end so they stay tender. Toss with pasta water.

How it is used

Toss it with linguine.

Field notes

  • The juice is the sauce. The clam meats are the garnish.
  • Add the meats late so they do not turn rubbery.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 4 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1/2 teaspoon chile flakes
  • 1/2 cup dry white wine
  • 1 cup clam juice
  • 2 cans (6 ounces) chopped clams, drained, juice reserved
  • 2 tablespoons parsley
  • 12 ounces linguine

Steps

  1. Warm the oil, garlic, and chile.
  2. Add the wine and clam juice, including juice from the cans, and simmer 5 minutes.
  3. Boil the linguine.
  4. Stir in the clam meats and parsley.
  5. Toss with the pasta and a splash of pasta water.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red clam sauce adds tomato.
  • A splash of cream is a later restaurant habit.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Seafood
  • linguine
  • parsley
  • lemon
  • Dairy-free
