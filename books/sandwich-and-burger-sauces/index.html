Ají Amarillo Sauce

Yellow ají, onion, garlic, and oil: fruity, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Ají amarillo is the yellow chile at the center of much Peruvian cooking. A simple cooked sauce of the chile, onion, garlic, and oil is a base for stews and a table sauce. The chile is fruity rather than only hot. Pastes sold in jars are the practical way to cook it outside the Andes.

Cultural background

Peruvian kitchens use ají amarillo in causa, papa a la huancaína’s cousin sauces, and everyday saltados. This is the plain cooked chile sauce, not the cheese sauce huancaína.

Why it tastes this way

Ají amarillo tastes fruity as well as hot. Frying the paste with onion and garlic cooks off the raw edge and leaves that fruit in a savory sauce.

How it is made

Onion and garlic soften in oil, then ají paste or soaked dried ají is fried and loosened with water.

Authentic touch. Fry the paste in oil before you add water. Skipping the fry leaves a raw, dusty taste.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled chicken, potatoes, or rice, or stir a spoonful into a stew.

Field notes

  • Ají amarillo tastes fruity. Heat is only part of the point.
  • Jarred paste is a legitimate ingredient when fresh pods are unavailable.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/4 cup ají amarillo paste
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the garlic and ají paste and fry 2 minutes.
  3. Stir in the water and salt and simmer 8 minutes.
  4. Add the vinegar.
  5. Cool slightly and use as a spooning sauce.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Crema de ají blends the chile with mayonnaise and is milder.
  • Huancaína adds cheese and crackers and is a different sauce.

Ajvar

Roasted red pepper and eggplant: smoky, sweet, and silky.

30 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Ajvar is a Balkan roasted-pepper relish, especially associated with Serbia and North Macedonia. Red peppers and often eggplant are roasted, chopped, and cooked down with oil and vinegar in the autumn. Ljutenica and pinđur are related pepper relishes with more tomato or a different balance, and they are not the same jar.

Cultural background

Families roast large batches when peppers are cheap and keep the relish for winter. It is eaten with bread, grilled meat, and eggs.

Why it tastes this way

Roasted red pepper and eggplant taste smoky and sweet because the skins char and the flesh cooks down in oil. Vinegar keeps it from being only sweet. It is an autumn preserve, meant to taste of the roast.

How it is made

Peppers and eggplant are roasted, peeled, and simmered with oil and vinegar until thick.

Authentic touch. Char the peppers until the skins blacken, then peel them. Skipping the char is why some ajvar tastes like boiled pepper.

How it is used

Spread it on bread or serve it beside grilled meats and eggs.

Field notes

  • Autumn pepper roasting is part of making ajvar, not an optional garnish.
  • The name is used across several Balkan countries for closely related relishes.

Ingredients

  • 4 red bell peppers
  • 1 small eggplant
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 garlic clove, minced

Steps

  1. Roast the peppers and eggplant until the skins blacken. Steam them in a covered bowl, then peel and seed them.
  2. Chop the flesh fine.
  3. Simmer it with the oil, vinegar, salt, and garlic until thick, about 30 minutes.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Hot ajvar uses more chile.
  • Pinđur leans on eggplant and tomato and is a cousin, not a duplicate.

Alabama White Sauce

Mayonnaise, vinegar, and black pepper: tangy, peppery, and pale.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Alabama white sauce is credited to Bob Gibson of Decatur, who was serving barbecue in the 1920s. It is a mayonnaise and vinegar sauce, heavily peppered, used on smoked chicken. The attribution to Gibson’s barbecue in Decatur is the standard account in barbecue histories.

Cultural background

It is a north Alabama sauce, especially for chicken, and it now shows up on pork and on the table as a dip. It is not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise carries pepper and vinegar, so the sauce is pale, tangy, and peppery rather than red and sweet. It was built for smoked chicken, whose mild meat can take a sharp white sauce.

How it is made

Mayonnaise is loosened with vinegar and seasoned with pepper, salt, and a little sugar.

Authentic touch. Use plenty of black pepper and keep it cold. The pepper is the heat. Chile is optional.

How it is used

Toss it with smoked chicken, or serve it as a dip for smoked meats.

Field notes

  • The sauce is white because the base is mayonnaise, not because it is mild in flavor.
  • It was built for chicken in a state better known, from the outside, for pork and tomato sauces.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 1/3 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne

Steps

  1. Whisk the mayonnaise and vinegar until smooth.
  2. Stir in the pepper, salt, sugar, and cayenne.
  3. Rest it 15 minutes so the pepper blooms.
  4. Taste and add vinegar if you want it sharper.
  5. Refrigerate.

This is a mayonnaise sauce. Keep it cold.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Horseradish is a common addition and not required.
  • Use a thin homemade mayonnaise if you want more acid.

Amba

Pickled mango, turmeric, and fenugreek: sour, spicy, and savory.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Amba is the Iraqi pickled-mango condiment. The name comes from the word for mango, via Indian trade, and the sauce is tied to Iraqi cooking and to Iraqi Jewish communities. It is now also a standard sauce in Israel, especially with sabich. Turmeric, chile, and fenugreek scent the mango. This is a quicker cooked version, not a long traditional pickle.

Cultural background

It is eaten with falafel, sabich, and grilled meat. The color is yellow from turmeric. A commercial jar is fermented or brined longer than this pot.

Why it tastes this way

Unripe mango, vinegar, turmeric, and fenugreek taste sour, yellow, and slightly bitter. The mango traveled by trade. Fenugreek is the bitter note that marks amba.

How it is made

Green mango simmers with vinegar, turmeric, fenugreek, chile, and salt until soft and sharp.

Authentic touch. Keep the fenugreek modest. Too much tastes soapy. This simmer is a kitchen version, not a long pickle.

How it is used

Spoon it into a sandwich or next to grilled meat.

Field notes

  • The name means mango.
  • Iraqi amba and the sauce in an Israeli sabich are the same condiment’s path through migration.

Ingredients

  • 2 firm green mangoes, peeled and diced
  • 1/2 cup white vinegar
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground fenugreek
  • 1/2 teaspoon chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until the mango is soft, about 20 minutes.
  2. Mash part of it so the sauce is chunky, not smooth.
  3. Taste for salt and vinegar.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. This is not a long ferment. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A long brine is the traditional preserve. This simmer is a kitchen version.
  • Fenugreek is the bitter note. Leaving it out makes a simpler mango pickle.

American Burger Sauce

Mayonnaise, ketchup, pickle, and onion: creamy, sweet, and sharp.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

A creamy pink burger sauce of mayonnaise, ketchup, and chopped pickle is a standard American diner sauce, related to Thousand Island dressing. Chain restaurants sell their own versions. This is a home recipe for that style, not a copy of a trademarked sauce.

Cultural background

It lives on burgers and as a fry dip. The pickles and onion are what separate it from plain ketchup or plain mayonnaise.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise makes it creamy, ketchup makes it sweet and pink, and pickle and onion keep it sharp. That mix stands up to a salty grilled patty and a soft bun.

How it is made

Mayonnaise and ketchup are stirred with pickle, onion, and a little vinegar.

Authentic touch. Chop the pickle and onion very fine so the sauce spreads instead of falling off the bun in chunks.

How it is used

Spread it on burgers, or use it as a dip for fries and onion rings.

Field notes

  • The pink color is ketchup meeting mayonnaise, the same idea as salsa golf in Argentina, with a different history.
  • This page does not reproduce a chain’s secret formula.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup mayonnaise
  • 2 tablespoons ketchup
  • 2 tablespoons finely chopped dill pickle
  • 1 tablespoon minced onion
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon paprika
  • Pinch of salt

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients together.
  2. Rest 15 minutes so the onion softens.
  3. Taste for salt and vinegar.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Use within a few days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thousand Island adds more relish and sometimes egg.
  • A dash of hot sauce makes a spicier house sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Dipping
  • hamburgers

American Yellow Mustard

Vinegar and mustard seed, colored with turmeric: sharp and bright.

10 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Bright yellow prepared mustard became an American condiment in the early twentieth century. French’s yellow mustard, introduced at the 1904 St. Louis World’s Fair, is the commercial example most often cited. The color comes from turmeric. The heat is from white or yellow mustard seed, softened by vinegar.

Cultural background

It is the mustard of hot dogs and ballpark sausages, milder and brighter than Dijon or English powder mustard.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar tames mustard seed and turmeric dyes it yellow. The heat is the seed, softened by acid, which is why it is milder than a fresh English mustard mixed with water.

How it is made

Mustard powder, vinegar, water, and turmeric are stirred and left to mellow. A short rest is part of the recipe.

Authentic touch. Let it rest after cooking. Mustard tastes harsh the minute it is mixed and milder the next day.

How it is used

Spread it on hot dogs and ham sandwiches, or whisk it into a barbecue mop.

Field notes

  • The yellow color is turmeric, not a hotter chile.
  • Prepared yellow mustard’s American popularity is tied to the early 1900s, not to ancient Rome.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup yellow mustard powder
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1/4 cup white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • Pinch of sugar

Steps

  1. Whisk the mustard powder and water until smooth.
  2. Add the vinegar, turmeric, salt, and sugar.
  3. Warm it 3 minutes over low heat, whisking. Do not boil hard.
  4. Cool. It will thicken and mellow.
  5. Refrigerate at least a few hours before serving.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Dijon is a wine-mustard from France and is sharper.
  • Honey mustard sweetens this base.

Banana Ketchup

Banana, vinegar, and spice, colored red: sweet, tangy, and smooth.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 12 · about 2 cups

History

Banana ketchup is a Filipino condiment developed in the twentieth century, associated with food scientist Maria Orosa’s work on local ingredients during shortages of tomato products. Ripe banana, vinegar, sugar, and spices are cooked and traditionally colored red. It became a everyday sauce for fried chicken, omelets, and spaghetti in the Filipino style.

Cultural background

It is sweeter than tomato ketchup. Filipino spaghetti sauce often uses it. This home version can stay pale if you skip food coloring. The flavor does not depend on the dye.

Why it tastes this way

Ripe banana cooked with vinegar and spices tastes sweet and tangy. Tomato is scarce in the origin story of the condiment. The red color of bottles is added. The banana is the flavor.

How it is made

Ripe bananas, vinegar, sugar, and spices simmer and are blended smooth.

Authentic touch. Use very ripe bananas and blend the sauce smooth. Tomato paste here is only for color and a little acidity.

How it is used

Use it on fried meats, eggs, or as the sweet base of a Filipino-style spaghetti sauce.

Field notes

  • The red color of bottled banana ketchup is added. The banana itself is not red.
  • Maria Orosa’s food work in the 1940s is the history most often tied to the condiment.

Ingredients

  • 3 very ripe bananas
  • 1/2 cup vinegar
  • 1/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon garlic powder
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground clove
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients 10 minutes.
  2. Blend until smooth.
  3. Simmer 5 minutes more until thick.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate and use within two weeks.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of tomato paste makes it redder without commercial dye.
  • Tomato ketchup is a different fruit sauce.

Cape Malay Fruit Chutney

Dried fruit, vinegar, ginger, and chile: sweet, sour, and warm.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3 cups

History

Cape Malay chutneys and blatjangs grew from the cooking of Muslim communities at the Cape, with roots in Indonesian and South Asian preserves and local fruit. Dried fruit, vinegar, onion, ginger, and chile are typical. Mrs H.S. Ball’s chutney, first sold in the early twentieth century, became the famous commercial jar. This is a home fruit chutney in that Cape style, not a copy of a brand.

Cultural background

It is eaten with curry, bobotie, and cold meat. The texture should be soft and sticky. This is a refrigerator chutney, not a canning recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Dried fruit, vinegar, ginger, and chile taste sweet and sharp because Cape cooks adapted preserve methods to local fruit and spice. The long simmer concentrates both the fruit and the vinegar.

How it is made

Dried apricots, onion, vinegar, sugar, ginger, and chile simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook it until sticky. A thin syrup tastes like compote, not chutney. Refrigerate it. This is not a canning recipe.

How it is used

Serve it with curries, bobotie, or a cheese sandwich.

Field notes

  • Cape Malay chutney is part of a cooking tradition shaped at the Cape, not a direct copy of one Indian recipe.
  • The famous commercial fruit chutney is a brand. This is a home pot.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups dried apricots, chopped
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 cup water

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until the fruit is soft and the pot is thick, about 35 minutes.
  2. Stir often near the end so it does not catch.
  3. Cool.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Serve with curry or cold meat.

Refrigerate and use within a month. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Raisins or dates can join the apricots.
  • A hotter chile is a household choice.

Chakalaka

Tomato, baked beans, pepper, and curry spice: tangy, sweet, and hot.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 4 cups

History

Chakalaka is a South African vegetable relish associated with township cooking and with braais. It typically includes onion, pepper, tomato, carrot, baked beans, and curry spices. It does not have a single inventor on record. Commercial jars made it a national pantry item.

Cultural background

It is served cold or warm beside pap, braaied meat, and bread. The spice level is a household choice.

Why it tastes this way

Baked beans, tomato, pepper, and curry spices taste like a braai relish because the vegetables are cooked down with the spices, not left raw. Curry powder is the spice trail in this South African pot.

How it is made

Aromatics and vegetables fry with curry powder, tomato, and beans until thick.

Authentic touch. Simmer until thick enough to sit on pap or bread. A watery pot tastes stewed, not like chakalaka.

How it is used

Spoon it next to grilled meat, pap, or onto bread.

Field notes

  • Chakalaka is a relish, usually eaten beside starch and meat rather than as a glaze.
  • Baked beans are a usual ingredient, which surprises people who expect only fresh vegetables.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 1 carrot, grated
  • 1 tablespoon curry powder
  • 1 can (14 ounces) diced tomatoes
  • 1 can (14 ounces) baked beans
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Fry the onion, pepper, and carrot in the oil until soft.
  2. Stir in the curry powder and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes and beans.
  4. Simmer 20 minutes until thick.
  5. Salt and cool.

Refrigerate and eat within five days. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add cabbage or apple.
  • A hotter chakalaka uses fresh bird’s eye chile.

Chicago Giardiniera

Pickled vegetables in oil, with hot chile: sharp, crunchy, and hot.

20 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 12 · about 4 cups

History

Chicago giardiniera is the oil-packed hot relish on an Italian beef sandwich. Cauliflower, carrot, celery, and hot chiles sit in vinegar and then in oil, with oregano. Italian giardiniera is an older pickle of garden vegetables in vinegar. The Chicago sandwich version is the local one: hotter, and dressed with oil so it soaks the bread. Sport peppers are traditional. Serranos are a common stand-in when sport peppers are not in the jar.

Cultural background

The beef is salty and wet. The relish is the crunch and the heat. A mild vinegar pickle without oil will not taste like the sandwich.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar pickles the vegetables, so they taste sour and stay crisp. Oil afterwards coats them and carries the chile heat into the bread. Oregano is the herbal note. The chile, not black pepper, is the burn. A short boil of the brine, poured over raw vegetables, is why they stay firm instead of cooking into a soft stew.

How it is made

Vegetables are covered with a hot vinegar brine, cooled, and then dressed with olive oil and oregano.

Authentic touch. Pack the vegetables in the hot brine, cool them, then cover them with oil. Give them a day if you can. Serrano is a stand-in, not the same pod as a sport pepper.

How it is used

Spoon it on Italian beef, or on sausages. It is a relish, not a cooked sauce.

Field notes

  • The oil is what makes the Chicago sandwich relish different from a plain vinegar pickle.
  • It is Italian-American Chicago cooking, hotter than a mild garden giardiniera.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cauliflower florets
  • 1 carrot, sliced
  • 2 celery stalks, sliced
  • 4 serrano chiles, sliced
  • 2 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 1 1/2 cups white vinegar
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/2 cup olive oil

Steps

  1. Pack the cauliflower, carrot, celery, chiles, and garlic in a clean jar.
  2. Boil the vinegar, water, and salt.
  3. Pour the hot brine over the vegetables and cool to room temperature.
  4. Stir in the oregano and olive oil.
  5. Refrigerate at least a few hours, preferably a day, before serving.

Refrigerate. The vegetables want at least a few hours in the brine, and a day is better. Use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A vinegar-only jar is closer to older Italian giardiniera and milder on the bread.
  • Sport peppers, if you can buy them, are the Chicago chile.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Meat
  • Italian beef
  • sausages
  • crusty bread

Chip Shop Curry Sauce

Mild curry, onion, and a light thickener: savory, slightly sweet, and smooth.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 2 cups

History

British chip-shop curry sauce is a mild, thick spiced gravy sold on chips, especially in the north of England and in Scotland. It grew up beside fish and chips in the twentieth century, separate from Indian restaurant curries and from Chinese takeaway sauces. It is a chip-shop sauce, not a curry paste.

Cultural background

This guide does not name a single inventor. The sauce is not a national secret sauce, and it is not a tapenade. Mayonnaise is the body. Olives are the flavor.

Cultural background

The burger is plain and grilled. The sauce is cold, spooned on after the meat is cooked. Olive brine is why it tastes sharper than mayonnaise alone.

Why it tastes this way

Green olives are salted and soured in brine, so they taste briny rather than like fresh fruit. Mayonnaise is oil and egg, mild and thick, which lets that brine lead. A spoon of the brine itself thins the sauce and makes it saltier. There is no chile and no tomato. That bareness is the regional sauce.

How it is made

Green olives are chopped and folded into mayonnaise with a spoon of their brine.

Authentic touch. Chop the olives. Do not blend them into a smooth paste. A little brine is enough. Too much turns the mayonnaise thin and watery.

How it is used

Spoon it on a hot hamburger. It is not cooked.

Field notes

  • The olive burger is a western Michigan sandwich, not a national chain sauce.
  • The olives stay in pieces. A puree is closer to a tapenade.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup mayonnaise
  • 1/2 cup chopped green olives
  • 1 tablespoon olive brine
  • Pinch of black pepper

Steps

  1. Chop the olives into small pieces.
  2. Stir them into the mayonnaise.
  3. Add the brine and pepper.
  4. Taste before you add any salt. The olives are already salty.
  5. Spoon it on cooked burgers.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Salted vegetables are simmered briefly in a vinegar sauce thickened with mustard and a little flour.

Zhug

Cilantro, hot chile, garlic, and cardamom: bright, hot, and aromatic.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 10 · about 1 cup

History

Zhug, also spelled skhug, is the Yemeni cilantro and chile paste. Green zhug is herbs and green chile. Red zhug uses hotter red chile. Cardamom, cumin, and garlic are typical. Yemeni Jews brought it into wider Israeli cooking in the twentieth century. It is a condiment, not a cooked salsa.

Cultural background

It is spooned onto falafel, grilled meat, and breakfast eggs. A little is enough. The cardamom is the spice that keeps it from being a generic green hot sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Cilantro and hot chile taste bright. Cardamom and cumin are why it does not taste like a generic green hot sauce. It is uncooked, so the herbs stay raw.

How it is made

Cilantro, chile, garlic, and spices are pounded or blended with olive oil and salt.

Authentic touch. Pound or blend it the day you eat it, and use cardamom, not only chile.

How it is used

Serve a spoonful with grilled meat, eggs, or falafel.

Field notes

  • Cardamom is a usual spice in zhug, which is a quick way to tell it from many other green hot sauces.
  • Green and red zhug are both Yemeni. The color follows the chile.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cilantro leaves
  • 3 green chiles
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Blend everything to a coarse paste.
  2. Taste for salt.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red zhug uses dried or fresh red chile and less herb.
  • Sahawiq is the tomato-chile relish and a different Yemeni sauce.
