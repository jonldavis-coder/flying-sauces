Island Hopping by Boat

Caribbean and Pacific sauces that moved by sea.

These sauces belong to islands. Sour orange and garlic in Cuba, allspice and scotch bonnet in Jamaica, herb pastes in Haiti and Puerto Rico, pepper sauce in Trinidad, coconut and chile farther into the Pacific.

The boat is the vehicle because that is how these flavors moved. New Zealand’s feijoa chutney is the last Pacific stop in the guide, a home preserve, not a canoe-era sauce.

Mojo Criollo

Sour orange, garlic, cumin, and oil: sharp, garlicky, and savory.

15 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 cup

History

Mojo criollo is the Cuban garlic and sour-orange sauce, also called Cuban mojo. It goes on yuca and is the marinade for roast pork. Sour orange, naranja agria, is the traditional acid. Lime and orange together are the usual stand-in when sour oranges are scarce. It is not the Spanish word for a gravy in general, and it is not the mojito drink.

Cultural background

Lechón and Christmas pork in Cuban cooking are tied to mojo. The boiled yuca on the table gets the same sauce, often heated so the garlic softens.

Why it tastes this way

Sour orange and a lot of garlic are the taste. Sour orange is sharper than sweet orange. Cumin and oil round it. The sauce is both a pork marinade and a spooning sauce for yuca.

How it is made

Garlic and cumin are pounded, mixed with sour orange juice, and combined with oil. A short warm-up blooms the garlic for the table version.

Authentic touch. If you cannot buy sour oranges, use equal parts lime and orange juice. Warm the oil and pour it over the garlic so the garlic softens without burning.

How it is used

Marinate pork in it, and spoon more over boiled yuca.

Field notes

  • Mojo the sauce and mojito the drink share a word history around moisture and sauce, and they are not the same recipe.
  • Sour orange is the acid that makes the marinade taste right.

Ingredients

  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/3 cup sour orange juice, or half lime and half orange
  • 1/3 cup olive oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon oregano

Steps

  1. Mash the garlic with the salt and cumin.
  2. Stir in the juice and oregano.
  3. Warm the oil, pour it over the garlic, and stir.
  4. Use it as a pork marinade for several hours.
  5. Spoon more over boiled yuca at the table.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Puerto Rican mojito isleño adds tomato, pepper, and olives for fish.
  • Equal parts lime and sweet orange approximate sour orange.

Jerk Marinade

Scotch bonnet, allspice, thyme, and scallion: hot, aromatic, and smoky-sweet.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Jerk is the Jamaican method of seasoning and smoke-grilling meat, historically associated with the Maroons. Allspice, called pimento in Jamaica, and scotch bonnet chile define it, along with thyme and scallion. The wet marinade is one way the seasoning is applied. A dry rub is the other.

Cultural background

Roadside jerk pits cook pork and chicken over pimento wood. Home cooks use the same seasoning under a grill. Soy sauce appears in many modern marinades and is not the oldest layer of the technique.

Why it tastes this way

Scotch bonnet, allspice, thyme, and scallion are the taste. Allspice, called pimento in Jamaica, is the spice that makes it jerk rather than a generic chile paste. The wet paste seasons the surface before smoke.

How it is made

Scotch bonnet, scallion, thyme, allspice, and acid are blended into a wet paste that coats the meat before grilling.

Authentic touch. Cut slits in the meat so the paste gets under the surface. Reserve some paste before it touches raw meat if you want a table sauce.

How it is used

Rub it on chicken or pork and grill it. Serve with more sauce only if that portion never touched raw meat.

Field notes

  • Allspice berry, not a mix of spices, is the pimento in jerk.
  • Scotch bonnet is traditional. A milder chile makes a different dish.

Ingredients

  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • 2 scotch bonnet chiles
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 tablespoon ground allspice
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar

Steps

  1. Blend all the ingredients to a coarse paste.
  2. Cut slits in chicken or pork and rub the paste in.
  3. Refrigerate at least 2 hours.
  4. Grill until the meat is cooked through.
  5. Boil any leftover marinade before you consider serving it.

Do not eat marinade that has touched raw meat unless you cook it through.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dry jerk rub skips the liquid and uses the same spices.
  • Reserve a cup of marinade before it touches raw meat if you want a table sauce.

Haitian Epis

Bell pepper, garlic, thyme, and scallion: savory and herbal.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Epis is the Haitian seasoning base of pepper, garlic, scallion, thyme, and often parsley or culantro, blended and used to start stews and marinades. It plays a role like a sofrito. Cooks keep it on hand. The blend is not one fixed recipe.

Cultural background

Rice, beans, chicken, and griot often start with epis fried in oil. That fry is when the raw blend becomes the flavor of the pot.

Why it tastes this way

A concentrated herb and allium paste tastes savory once it fries in oil. Bell pepper sweetens it. Thyme is the herbal note. Using it raw as a table sauce is not the usual path.

How it is made

Pepper, garlic, herbs, and scallion are blended, then fried as the base of a dish.

Authentic touch. Blend it, then fry a spoonful in oil at the start of a stew until it smells sweet. That fried spoonful seasons the whole pot.

How it is used

Use it to start rice and stews or as a marinade that you then cook.

Field notes

  • Epis is a cooking base, like a sofrito.
  • Frying it is what turns the raw blend into the stew’s flavor.

Ingredients

  • 1 green bell pepper
  • 1 onion
  • 6 scallions
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 cup parsley
  • 6 sprigs thyme
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend everything except a spare spoon of oil, adding a splash of water if needed.
  2. Store it cold.
  3. When you cook, fry a few tablespoons in oil until fragrant.
  4. Build the stew on that base.
  5. Use the jar within a few days.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Trinidadian green seasoning is a close cousin.
  • A Scotch bonnet in the blend makes it hot.

Puerto Rican Sofrito

Ají dulce, pepper, cilantro, and culantro: sweet, herbal, and savory.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Puerto Rican sofrito is a blended base of onion, garlic, sweet pepper, ají dulce, cilantro, and culantro, often with tomato and oil. It starts beans, rice, and stews. Spanish sofrito is a cooked tomato-onion base and is not the same blend. Ají dulce is aromatic and not very hot.

Cultural background

A freezer jar of sofrito is how many kitchens start dinner. Culantro, recao, tastes stronger than cilantro. Both appear in many blends. Frying the raw blend is the step that makes the pot taste right.

Why it tastes this way

Sweet peppers and ají dulce taste fragrant rather than hot. Culantro and cilantro are herbal. Fried in oil with tomato, the blend tastes savory and sweet, which is the base under rice and beans.

How it is made

Peppers, onion, garlic, and herbs are blended, then fried as the start of a dish.

Authentic touch. Blend it raw, then fry a few spoonfuls in oil until the raw smell is gone before you add rice or beans. If you cannot find ají dulce, a bit of bell pepper is milder and not the same.

How it is used

Use it as the base of rice, beans, and stews.

Field notes

  • Ají dulce is fragrant, not a habanero’s heat.
  • Puerto Rican sofrito and Spanish sofrito share a name and not a recipe.

Ingredients

  • 1 green bell pepper
  • 1 onion
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 cup cilantro
  • 1/2 cup culantro if available, or more cilantro
  • 1 tomato
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend the vegetables, herbs, and salt.
  2. Store cold.
  3. Fry a portion in oil until fragrant before building a rice or bean pot.
  4. Use the jar within a few days.
  5. Taste the finished dish for salt.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cuban sofrito leans on more tomato and different peppers.
  • A spoon of tomato sauce in the fry is common.

Pairings and uses

  • Stews
  • Rice
  • arroz con gandules
  • habichuelas
  • stews

  • Gluten-free

Trinidadian Pepper Sauce

Scotch bonnet or Congo pepper, vinegar, and garlic: very hot and sharp.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 20 · about 1 cup

History

Trinidadian pepper sauce is a vinegar chile sauce built on local hot peppers, often Congo pepper or scotch bonnet, with garlic and sometimes culantro or mustard. Bottled pepper sauces from the island are famous. This is a fresh cooked home version of that style, not a brand formula.

Cultural background

It is on the table for pelau, grilled meat, and doubles in small amounts. The pepper is the ingredient. A mild sauce misses the point.

Why it tastes this way

Caribbean hot peppers taste fruity and extremely hot. Vinegar keeps the sauce thin and sharp so a few drops season a plate. Garlic rounds the fruit.

How it is made

Hot peppers, garlic, and vinegar simmer and are blended with salt.

Authentic touch. Simmer the peppers and garlic in vinegar, then blend. Use it by the drop. Leave the seeds in if you want the full heat.

How it is used

Shake a little onto rice, meat, or doubles.

Field notes

  • Congo pepper and scotch bonnet are the heat.
  • The sauce is thin and used sparingly.

Ingredients

  • 8 scotch bonnet or habanero peppers
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 3/4 cup white vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Simmer the peppers, garlic, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend smooth.
  3. Cool.
  4. Bottle and refrigerate.
  5. Use a few drops at a time.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Culantro, if you can buy it, is a common herbal note.
  • A mustard version is another local style.

Sauce Chien

Lime, scallion, garlic, chile, and oil: sharp, herbal, and hot.

15 min prep · 2 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Sauce chien is the table sauce of Martinique and Guadeloupe for grilled fish. Scallion, garlic, chile, lime, and oil are chopped together, often scalded with hot water so the alliums soften. The name’s folk etymologies are uncertain and are left out. The sauce is a fresh relish.

Cultural background

It is spooned over grilled fish the way a salsa is. The hot water step is what separates many family versions from a fully raw chop. Thyme or parsley may join the scallion.

Why it tastes this way

Lime and raw chile taste bright and hot. Scalding the scallion and garlic tames their raw bite without cooking them into a jam. Oil carries the flavor onto the fish.

How it is made

Scallion, garlic, and chile are chopped, softened with hot water, and dressed with lime and oil.

Authentic touch. Chop the aromatics, cover them with boiling water for a minute, drain, then dress with lime, salt, and oil.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled fish.

Field notes

  • A splash of boiling water is a traditional softening step.
  • It is a French Caribbean fish sauce, not a French mother sauce.

Ingredients

  • 4 scallions, minced
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 habanero or bird’s eye chile, minced
  • 2 tablespoons parsley
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/4 cup boiling water
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Put the scallion, garlic, chile, parsley, and salt in a bowl.
  2. Pour on the boiling water and rest 5 minutes. Drain most of it.
  3. Stir in the lime and oil.
  4. Taste.
  5. Serve with grilled fish.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A fully raw version is sharper.
  • Parsley is a frequent addition.

Wasakaka

Lime, garlic, and oregano: sharp, herbal, and savory.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Wasakaka is the Dominican garlic, lime, and oregano sauce served with roast chicken, especially pollo al carbón. Parsley or cilantro often joins it. It is a fresh finishing sauce, related in spirit to mojo and not the same as either Cuban mojo or chimichurri.

Cultural background

Roast chicken stalls spoon it over the carved bird. Oregano is the herb that makes it taste Dominican in this sauce. Lime should be plentiful.

Why it tastes this way

Raw garlic and lime taste sharp against roasted chicken skin and mild meat. Oregano, often with a little oil, perfumes the juice. It is not cooked down, so it stays bright.

How it is made

Garlic, oregano, lime, and oil are stirred with salt and herbs.

Authentic touch. Stir it together just before serving and spoon it over hot carved chicken. Let the garlic be noticeable.

How it is used

Spoon it over roast or grilled chicken.

Field notes

  • It is a finishing sauce for chicken, not a long marinade.
  • Oregano and lime are the core.

Ingredients

  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley
  • 3 tablespoons oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir all the ingredients.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Taste for lime and salt.
  4. Spoon over hot chicken.
  5. Use it the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A sliced chile makes it hotter.
  • Sour orange instead of lime is a sharper acid.

Pairings and uses

  • tostones

Samoan Miti

Coconut cream, onion, and lime: cool, rich, and sharp.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Miti is a Samoan coconut sauce, typically coconut cream with onion and salt, often with lime or lemon, served with cooked taro, breadfruit, or palusami. Related coconut sauces appear elsewhere in Polynesia. This is the fresh coconut-cream version, not a cooked curry.

Cultural background

It is a cool sauce next to hot starches from the umu or the pot. The onion is chopped fine so each bite of taro picks it up.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut cream is rich and cool. Onion and lime cut it so the sauce tastes bright against hot taro or fish. It is not cooked, so the coconut stays sweet instead of oily.

How it is made

Coconut cream is stirred with minced onion, lime, and salt. It is not cooked.

Authentic touch. Salt it and serve it cool. Heat is for the taro, not for the miti.

How it is used

Spoon it over hot taro, breadfruit, or grilled fish.

Field notes

  • Miti means the coconut sauce, not a spice paste.
  • It is meant to be cool against hot taro.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup coconut cream
  • 2 tablespoons minced onion
  • 1 tablespoon lime juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir the coconut cream, onion, lime, and salt.
  2. Rest 10 minutes.
  3. Taste for salt.
  4. Serve cool.
  5. Use the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A green-onion-only version is common.
  • Heating the coconut cream makes a different, thicker sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Vegetables
  • boiled taro
  • breadfruit
  • grilled fish

Hawaiian Chili Pepper Water

Chile, garlic, and water: clean, hot, and savory.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 16 · about 1 cup

History

Chili pepper water is a Hawaiian table condiment of hot chiles steeped in water with garlic, sometimes ginger or vinegar. Small local chiles became part of the islands’ cooking after the plant’s global spread. A bottle of it sits on diner counters beside soy sauce. This is a simple infused version.

Cultural background

It is shaken onto saimin, loco moco, grilled fish, and rice. It is closer to a chile vinegar or a light infusion than to a cooked salsa.

Why it tastes this way

Small hot chiles steeped in water with garlic taste clean and hot, like a table vinegar. It is thin so it can be shaken onto noodles and rice. A cooked ketchup would be a different condiment.

How it is made

Smashed chiles and garlic sit in hot water and a little vinegar until the water tastes hot, then the bottle is refrigerated.

Authentic touch. Let it sit overnight. The water tastes mild the hour you mix it and hotter the next day.

How it is used

Shake it over noodles, rice plates, and grilled fish.

Field notes

  • It is meant to be thin enough to sprinkle, like a table vinegar.
  • The islands’ diner counters helped make it a daily sauce, not a special-occasion one.

Ingredients

  • 6 Hawaiian or other small hot chiles, smashed
  • 2 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 1 cup just-boiled water
  • 1 tablespoon white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Put the chiles and garlic in a clean jar.
  2. Pour on the hot water, vinegar, and salt.
  3. Cool, then refrigerate overnight.
  4. Taste the next day and add a pinch more salt if needed.
  5. Use within two weeks.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some recipes add a knob of ginger.
  • A fully fermented hot sauce is a different project.

Feijoa Chutney

Feijoa, onion, vinegar, and ginger: fragrant, sweet, and sharp.

20 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3 cups

History

Feijoa, a South American fruit grown widely in New Zealand gardens, became a home-kitchen chutney fruit there in the twentieth century. The chutney follows the British cooked-chutney pattern of fruit, onion, vinegar, sugar, and spice, with feijoa’s perfume in place of mango or apple. It is a New Zealand household preserve, not an older Māori sauce.

Cultural background

Jars of it are made when the fruit drops in autumn and are eaten with cold meat and cheese. This version is a refrigerator chutney, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Feijoa’s perfume is the fruit. Vinegar, ginger, and sugar are the British-style chutney method that New Zealand home cooks used on a local glut. The result is fragrant, sweet, and sharp.

How it is made

Chopped feijoa, onion, vinegar, sugar, and ginger simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook it until a spoon leaves a trail. A thin chutney tastes like stewed fruit, not a preserve.

How it is used

Serve it with cold lamb, cheese, or a meat sandwich.

Field notes

  • Feijoa is also called pineapple guava, and it is not native to New Zealand.
  • The chutney style is the British cooked chutney adapted to a local glut of fruit.

Ingredients

  • 4 cups chopped feijoas
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 3/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground allspice

Steps

  1. Combine all the ingredients in a wide pot.
  2. Simmer, stirring, until thick, about 40 minutes.
  3. The chutney should hold a trail when you pull a spoon through it.
  4. Cool and jar.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within a month. This is not a canning recipe. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Apple can replace part of the fruit if the feijoa season is short.
  • A hotter chutney adds a sliced chile.
