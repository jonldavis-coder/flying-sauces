The Mother Sauces & Their Children

The five French mother sauces and the named sauces built from them, plus a few classics from other kitchens.

In the French system taught since the nineteenth century, five mother sauces anchor a repertoire: béchamel, velouté, espagnole, sauce tomate, and hollandaise. Mayonnaise, béarnaise, and a vinaigrette sit beside them as emulsions.

A mother sauce is a method, not a claim that other cultures descend from Paris. Mole, pesto, and nuoc cham are in this book because they are classics of their own kitchens, not because they are French daughters.

Béchamel

Milk, butter, and a white roux: smooth, lightly nutty, and savory.

5 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Béchamel is one of the five French mother sauces in the repertoire associated with Auguste Escoffier. The name is tied to Louis de Béchamel, a financier at the court of Louis XIV, though the sauce of flour-thickened milk is older than that dedication. By the nineteenth century it was a standard base for gratins and for daughter sauces such as Mornay.

Cultural background

In French cooking it is a pantry technique as much as a sauce: the white sauce that carries cheese, nutmeg, or onion. Italian besciamella and Greek béchamel in pastitsio are close relatives with their own seasoning.

Why it tastes this way

The mild, milky taste is the liquid, not a spice. A white roux only cooks long enough to lose raw flour, so the sauce stays pale and sweet from milk instead of tasting toasted or meaty like a brown sauce.

How it is made

A white roux of butter and flour is whisked with warm milk and simmered until it coats a spoon.

Authentic touch. Add the milk warm and whisk constantly. Cold milk and a hot roux lump, and those lumps taste of raw starch.

How it is used

Use it under a gratin, in lasagna, or as the base of a cheese sauce for cauliflower, croque monsieur, or creamed spinach.

Field notes

  • Escoffier’s codification made béchamel a mother sauce, not a single restaurant recipe.
  • The Italian besciamella in lasagna alla bolognese is the same technique with a lighter hand on the nutmeg.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 2 cups whole milk, warmed
  • 1/4 teaspoon fine salt
  • Pinch of white pepper
  • Pinch of nutmeg

Steps

  1. Melt the butter in a saucepan over medium heat.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook 1 to 2 minutes without browning.
  3. Whisk in the warm milk a little at a time until smooth.
  4. Simmer, whisking, until the sauce coats a spoon, about 8 minutes.
  5. Season with salt, white pepper, and nutmeg.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mornay stirs in Gruyère and Parmesan.
  • Soubise blends in a sweated onion puree.
  • A pinch of nutmeg is traditional; a bay leaf in the milk is optional.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Vegetables
  • Eggs
  • lasagna
  • cauliflower gratin
  • croque monsieur
  • creamed spinach
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Velouté

Light stock and a blond roux: silky, savory, and pale.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Velouté is the stock-based white mother sauce of the French repertoire. Carême and later Escoffier distinguished it from béchamel by the liquid: chicken, veal, or fish stock instead of milk. Sauce suprême, a cream-finished chicken velouté, is the best-known daughter.

Cultural background

French sauce cookery treats velouté as a blank savory canvas. The stock decides the sauce: fish velouté for poached fish, chicken velouté for vegetables and poultry.

Why it tastes this way

It tastes of the stock you choose because stock, not milk, is the liquid. A blond roux keeps the flavor light. Chicken stock tastes of poultry. Fish stock tastes of the sea. They are not interchangeable.

How it is made

A blond roux is loosened with hot stock and simmered until nappé, then strained.

Authentic touch. Simmer and skim. The long, gentle cook is what removes the raw-flour taste without browning the sauce.

How it is used

Spoon it over poached chicken, steamed vegetables, or fish. Reduce it further with cream for sauce suprême.

Field notes

  • The name means velvety, which describes the texture once the starch has cooked out.
  • A fish velouté and a chicken velouté are the same method and should not be swapped casually.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 2 1/2 cups hot chicken stock
  • Salt
  • White pepper

Steps

  1. Melt the butter and stir in the flour. Cook the roux to a pale blond, about 2 minutes.
  2. Whisk in the hot stock until smooth.
  3. Simmer 15 minutes, skimming, until the sauce coats a spoon.
  4. Strain, then season with salt and white pepper.
  5. Keep it warm over the lowest heat, with a film of stock or butter on top if you are not using it at once.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sauce suprême finishes chicken velouté with cream and a little butter.
  • Sauce vin blanc adds a white-wine reduction and fish stock.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Seafood
  • Vegetables
  • poached chicken
  • steamed sole
  • asparagus
  • veal blanquette
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Espagnole

Brown stock, browned roux, and tomato: deep, roasted, and savory.

20 min prep · 1 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Espagnole is the brown mother sauce. The name is French for Spanish, a label that stuck in nineteenth-century French kitchens and does not mean the sauce is from Spain. Escoffier built it from brown stock, a brown roux, mirepoix, and tomato, then used it as the path to demi-glace.

Cultural background

It belongs to the professional French sauce pot: a base kept warm and reduced, not usually a table sauce by itself. Home cooks meet it mostly through demi-glace and pan sauces that borrow its flavor.

Why it tastes this way

The dark, roasted taste comes from a brown roux and browned vegetables, then brown stock. Tomato is a seasoning here, not the main flavor, which is why it does not taste like marinara.

How it is made

A brown roux thickens brown stock, with a browned mirepoix and tomato, then the sauce is skimmed and strained.

Authentic touch. Let the roux reach a true brown and a toasted smell before the stock goes in. A pale roux will not give you this sauce.

How it is used

Use it as the base of demi-glace or a pan sauce for steak. It is too plain and thick to be the whole point of a plate.

Field notes

  • The Spanish name is a French kitchen label, not a Spanish recipe.
  • A properly cooked brown roux thickens less than a white roux and tastes more toasted.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter or neutral oil
  • 1 small onion, diced
  • 1 small carrot, diced
  • 1 celery stalk, diced
  • 2 tablespoons all-purpose flour
  • 3 cups hot brown beef stock
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 1 bay leaf
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Brown the onion, carrot, and celery in the fat.
  2. Stir in the flour and cook until the roux is brown and smells toasted, 4 to 6 minutes.
  3. Whisk in the stock and tomato paste. Add the bay leaf.
  4. Simmer 45 minutes, skimming the surface.
  5. Strain, season, and use as a base or reduce further.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Demi-glace reduces espagnole with more brown stock.
  • Sauce Robert adds mustard and onion to a brown sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • pan sauces for steak
  • braised beef
  • demi-glace
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Sauce Tomate

Tomato cooked with stock, aromatics, and a little roux: round and savory rather than bright.

15 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

Sauce tomate is the tomato mother sauce of the French system, distinct from quick Italian marinara. Classic versions in the Escoffier lineage use salt pork or bacon, a roux, stock, and a long simmer. Tomato had already entered French cooking in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries before it was fixed as a mother sauce.

Cultural background

French tomato sauce is a cooked sauce for meat, eggs, and filled pasta. It is thicker and more savory than a raw salsa and less herbal than many Italian sughi.

Why it tastes this way

French sauce tomate tastes cooked and savory because the tomato simmers with pork fat, stock, and a little flour. The long cook turns sharp tomato into a round sauce. That is different from a quick Italian marinara.

How it is made

A small roux and rendered pork cook with tomato and stock until the sauce is thick and sweet-savory.

Authentic touch. Simmer until the raw tomato edge is gone. If it still tastes like the can, it needs more time, not more salt.

How it is used

Serve with grilled meats, eggs, stuffed vegetables, or as a base under a cheese gratin.

Field notes

  • In the French mother-sauce list, tomato sauce sits beside the roux sauces and hollandaise.
  • A long simmer is the point: the sauce should taste cooked, not like fresh tomato water.

Ingredients

  • 2 ounces salt pork or bacon, diced
  • 1 small onion, diced
  • 1 tablespoon all-purpose flour
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1 can (28 ounces) whole tomatoes, crushed by hand
  • 1 cup beef or chicken stock
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Render the pork and cook the onion in the fat until soft.
  2. Stir in the flour and tomato paste and cook 1 minute.
  3. Add the tomatoes, stock, bay leaf, and sugar.
  4. Simmer 40 minutes until thick.
  5. Remove the bay, season with salt, and blend if you want it smooth.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skip the pork and use oil and vegetable stock for a different, meatless sauce.
  • Italian marinara is a separate, quicker sauce and is not this mother sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Pasta
  • Meat
  • Vegetables
  • grilled pork
  • eggs
  • stuffed cabbage
  • gnocchi
  • Nut-free

Hollandaise

Butter emulsified with egg yolk and lemon: rich, tart, and warm.

10 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Hollandaise is the emulsion mother sauce: egg yolks holding butter, sharpened with lemon or a vinegar reduction. The name points at Holland and at butter, and the sauce was established in French cookbooks well before Escoffier listed it among the mothers. It is the parent of béarnaise and of sauce mousseline.

Cultural background

It is the classic sauce for eggs Benedict in American brunch cooking and for asparagus and poached fish in French cooking. It is made to order because it does not hold well.

Why it tastes this way

The flavor is butter, held by egg yolk and sharpened with lemon. Warmth keeps the emulsion glossy. Too much heat scrambles the yolk and the sauce tastes eggy and grainy instead of smooth.

How it is made

Yolks and a little water or reduction are warmed while melted butter is whisked in to form a stable emulsion.

Authentic touch. Whisk the butter in off a hard boil. If it tightens, a teaspoon of warm water brings it back. Do not try to save a scrambled yolk.

How it is used

Spoon it over poached eggs, asparagus, or poached fish as soon as it is made.

Field notes

  • Hollandaise is an emulsion, like mayonnaise, but the fat is warm butter rather than oil.
  • If it gets too hot, the yolk scrambles and the emulsion breaks.

Ingredients

  • 3 egg yolks
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon cold water
  • 1/2 cup unsalted butter, melted and warm
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • Pinch of cayenne

Steps

  1. Whisk the yolks, lemon juice, and water in a heatproof bowl.
  2. Set the bowl over barely simmering water and whisk until the yolks thicken slightly.
  3. Take the bowl off the heat and whisk in the warm butter in a thin stream.
  4. Season with salt and cayenne.
  5. Serve at once. If it tightens, whisk in a teaspoon of warm water.

The yolks stay under a simmer. Use the sauce the day it is made and keep it warm, not hot. Pasteurized yolks are the safer choice if you need to hold it.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Béarnaise uses a tarragon and shallot reduction.
  • Sauce mousseline folds in whipped cream.
  • Maltaise uses orange juice instead of lemon.

Pairings and uses

  • Eggs
  • Vegetables
  • Seafood
  • eggs Benedict
  • asparagus
  • poached salmon
  • artichokes
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Mayonnaise

Oil emulsified with egg yolk and acid: thick, neutral, and rich.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Mayonnaise is a cold emulsion of oil, egg yolk, and vinegar or lemon. French cookbooks of the early nineteenth century already treat it as a standard sauce. A story that ties the name to Mahón in Menorca after a French victory in 1756 is often repeated and not settled, so this page keeps the documented part: it is a French sauce technique with a disputed name origin.

Cultural background

From French tables it became a global sandwich and salad sauce. Belgian frites are often eaten with mayonnaise. Japanese Kewpie mayonnaise is a later, seasoned style with a different recipe.

Why it tastes this way

The neutral richness is oil suspended in yolk and acid. Mustard helps the emulsion and adds a small bite. The oil you choose is the flavor: a strong olive oil tastes loud, a neutral oil tastes clean.

How it is made

Oil is whisked drop by drop into yolk, mustard, and acid until a thick emulsion forms, then the rest of the oil can go in faster.

Authentic touch. Start the oil drop by drop. Once the sauce thickens, you can stream it. Dumping the oil in at the start is how mayonnaise breaks.

How it is used

Spread it on sandwiches, serve it with cold seafood or fries, or use it as the base of rémoulade and aïoli-style garlic mayonnaise.

Field notes

  • Mustard helps the emulsion and is why many home recipes include a spoon of Dijon.
  • The Menorca naming story is popular and not proven, so it is not treated here as fact.

Ingredients

  • 1 egg yolk
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice or white wine vinegar
  • 1 cup neutral oil or light olive oil
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Whisk the yolk, mustard, lemon juice, and salt until smooth.
  2. Whisk in the oil a few drops at a time until the mixture thickens.
  3. Add the remaining oil in a thin stream, whisking steadily.
  4. Adjust the salt and acid. Whisk in a teaspoon of water if it is stiffer than you want.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a few days.

This uses raw yolk. Refrigerate it and use it within a few days, or use a pasteurized yolk.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Aïoli, in the egg-added modern sense, is garlic mayonnaise.
  • Rémoulade stirs in herbs, capers, and cornichons.
  • Japanese-style mayonnaise is seasoned with vinegar, sugar, and often MSG, and is a separate product.

Pairings and uses

  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Dipping
  • Seafood
  • Salads
  • frites
  • cold lobster
  • egg salad
  • sandwiches
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Béarnaise

Warm butter emulsion with shallot, vinegar, and tarragon: herbal and sharp.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Béarnaise is a daughter of hollandaise flavored with a reduction of shallot, vinegar, and tarragon. It was popular in Paris restaurants in the nineteenth century. The name refers to the Béarn region, though the sauce as served with steak is a Paris restaurant sauce more than a farmhouse one.

Cultural background

It is the classic French steak sauce beside peppercorn and bordelaise. The tarragon is not optional in the traditional version.

Why it tastes this way

It tastes herbal and sharp because a vinegar, shallot, and tarragon reduction seasons the same butter emulsion as hollandaise. Tarragon, not lemon, is the perfume.

How it is made

Shallot, pepper, and tarragon stems reduce with vinegar, then that reduction flavors an egg-and-butter emulsion.

Authentic touch. Strain the reduction so the shallot does not stay in threads, then stir chopped tarragon leaves in at the end so they stay green.

How it is used

Serve with grilled steak, roasted potatoes, or grilled fish. It should be warm, not hot.

Field notes

  • Béarnaise is hollandaise plus a tarragon reduction, not a separate emulsion method.
  • The Béarn name outran the sauce’s actual regional origin.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons white wine vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons dry white wine
  • 1 shallot, minced
  • 2 sprigs tarragon, stems and leaves separated
  • 3 egg yolks
  • 1/2 cup unsalted butter, melted
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the vinegar, wine, shallot, and tarragon stems until about a tablespoon remains. Strain and cool slightly.
  2. Whisk the yolks with the reduction over gentle heat until thick.
  3. Whisk in the warm butter in a stream.
  4. Chop the tarragon leaves and stir them in. Season with salt.
  5. Hold the sauce warm, not hot, and serve the same day.

Keep the yolks below a simmer. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sauce Choron replaces some of the tarragon with tomato.
  • Sauce Paloise uses mint instead of tarragon.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Grilling
  • Vegetables
  • grilled steak
  • frites
  • grilled asparagus
  • sole
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free

Beurre Blanc

White wine, shallot, and butter: sharp, creamy, and pale.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 3/4 cup

History

Beurre blanc is the butter sauce of the Loire, especially associated with Nantes and with fish from the river and the Atlantic. It is an emulsion of cold butter into a sharp reduction of shallot and white wine or vinegar. It does not use a roux or, in the classic version, egg.

Cultural background

Loire cooking pairs it with pike and other river fish. Restaurant kitchens later put it on almost any poached or grilled fish.

Why it tastes this way

The sauce tastes of white wine and shallot because that reduction is the only liquid. Cold butter emulsifies into it if the pan stays below a boil. Boiling drives the butter out and the sauce tastes greasy.

How it is made

A shallot and acid reduction is taken off the boil and mounted with cold butter, whisked in piece by piece.

Authentic touch. Whisk in cold butter a few cubes at a time, off a boil. A boil separates it.

How it is used

Spoon it over poached or grilled fish, shellfish, or asparagus.

Field notes

  • Classic beurre blanc has no egg yolk. The butter itself forms the emulsion if the pan is not too hot.
  • It is a Loire sauce that became an international fish-restaurant sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 shallot, minced
  • 1/4 cup white wine vinegar
  • 1/4 cup dry white wine
  • 8 tablespoons cold unsalted butter, cubed
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the shallot, vinegar, and wine until only about 2 tablespoons of liquid remain.
  2. Drop the heat to low. Whisk in the butter a few cubes at a time.
  3. Do not let the sauce boil once the butter goes in.
  4. Season with salt and serve at once.
  5. If it separates, whisk in a spoon of cold water off the heat.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Beurre rouge uses red wine instead of white.
  • A splash of cream makes the emulsion more stable and less classic.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Vegetables
  • pike
  • sole
  • scallops
  • asparagus

Vinaigrette

Vinegar, mustard, and oil: sharp, salty, and fresh.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1/2 cup

History

A vinaigrette is oil and vinegar, seasoned and often held together for a minute by mustard. French cooking made the ratio and the mustard emulsion a standard. It is a temporary emulsion unless mustard or another emulsifier is doing real work.

Cultural background

It is the default sauce for green salad in France, tossed at the last moment so the leaves stay crisp. Countless regional oils and vinegars change the flavor without changing the method.

Why it tastes this way

Sharpness is the vinegar, body is the oil, and mustard is what holds them together for a minute. Without mustard it separates, which is normal for a temporary emulsion. The vinegar you pick is the main flavor.

How it is made

Salt dissolves in vinegar, mustard joins them, and oil is whisked in.

Authentic touch. Dissolve the salt in the vinegar before the oil goes in. Salt will not dissolve well in oil.

How it is used

Dress green salads, lentil salads, and warm vegetables just before serving.

Field notes

  • Without mustard, a vinaigrette separates within minutes. That is normal.
  • The usual French starting ratio is about three parts oil to one part vinegar, adjusted to the vinegar’s strength.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon Dijon mustard
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil or olive oil
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir the vinegar, mustard, and salt until the salt dissolves.
  2. Whisk in the oil until the sauce looks lightly creamy.
  3. Add pepper.
  4. Toss with salad immediately.
  5. Whisk again if it separates.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A shallot and herbs make a sauce ravigote-like dressing.
  • Walnut oil or red wine vinegar changes the character without a new method.

Pairings and uses

  • green salad

Demi-Glace

Brown stock reduced with espagnole: glossy, concentrated, and savory.

15 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Demi-glace, in the Escoffier system, is espagnole combined with brown stock and reduced by about half until it is glossy and nappé. The name means half-glaze. A restaurant pan sauce that reduces a purchased stock is related in flavor and is not the full classic preparation.

Cultural background

It is a kitchen staple of French sauce work, used by the spoonful to finish meat sauces. It is not usually eaten by itself.

Why it tastes this way

The deep, glossy taste is reduction: water leaves, and browned stock concentrates. A little tomato and a brown roux add body. It tastes meaty because the liquid is brown stock, not because of a spice mix.

How it is made

Espagnole and brown stock simmer together until the sauce is reduced, shiny, and thick enough to coat a spoon. This home version builds a shorter brown sauce and reduces it.

Authentic touch. Reduce until it coats a spoon. If it tastes thin, it is not done. Salt only at the end, because reduction concentrates salt.

How it is used

Whisk a spoonful into pan juices for steak, short ribs, or a roast. A little goes a long way.

Field notes

  • Half-glaze refers to the reduction, not to a glaze made of sugar.
  • A boxed stock reduced with butter is a useful shortcut and a different sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 carrot, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1/2 cup dry red wine
  • 4 cups unsalted beef stock
  • 1 tablespoon flour mixed with 1 tablespoon soft butter
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Brown the onion and carrot in the oil. Stir in the tomato paste and cook until it darkens.
  2. Add the wine and reduce by half.
  3. Add the stock and simmer until reduced to about 1 1/2 cups.
  4. Whisk in bits of the butter-flour paste and simmer until the sauce coats a spoon.
  5. Strain and season. The result is a home demi-glace, shorter than the classical reduction.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wine reduction before the demi-glace goes in makes a simple pan sauce.
  • A fully classical demi-glace starts from a proper espagnole and a long brown stock.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Roasts
  • steak
  • short ribs
  • roasted game
  • mushroom pan sauce
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Pesto alla Genovese

Raw basil, garlic, pine nuts, cheese, and olive oil: green, sharp, and rich.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Pesto alla Genovese is the basil sauce of Liguria. The name comes from pestare, to pound. The modern form, with basil, garlic, pine nuts, salt, Ligurian olive oil, and a mix of Parmesan and a hard Sardinian or local sheep cheese, was fixed in Genoese cooking in the nineteenth century. A mortar gives a creamier sauce than a blender.

Cultural background

It is a Ligurian sauce for trofie, trenette, and green beans with potato, not a generic herb paste. Other Italian pestos, such as Trapanese, are different recipes.

Why it tastes this way

Raw basil, garlic, pine nuts, cheese, and Ligurian olive oil are the flavor. Heat is not part of it. Pounding bruises the basil less than a long blend, so the sauce stays green and sweet instead of dark and bitter.

How it is made

Basil is pounded with salt, garlic, and pine nuts, then cheese and olive oil are worked in. Heat is not part of the sauce.

Authentic touch. Pound or pulse briefly and stop while it is still bright. Toss it with pasta off the boil. Boiling pesto turns it dull.

How it is used

Toss with pasta and a little pasta water, or stir into minestrone. Do not cook the pesto on a hard boil or the basil turns dull.

Field notes

  • Classic Genoese pesto is pounded, which bruises the basil less harshly than a long blend.
  • Pine nuts are traditional. Walnuts make a different Ligurian sauce, salsa di noci.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups packed fresh basil leaves
  • 1 small garlic clove
  • 2 tablespoons pine nuts
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup extra-virgin olive oil
  • 1/4 cup finely grated Parmesan
  • 2 tablespoons finely grated pecorino

Steps

  1. Pound the garlic and salt to a paste, then the pine nuts.
  2. Add the basil in handfuls and pound to a coarse green paste.
  3. Stir in the cheeses.
  4. Stream in the oil until the sauce is thick and spoonable.
  5. Toss with hot pasta and a splash of pasta water. Do not boil the pesto.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pesto alla Trapanese uses tomato and almonds and is a separate Sicilian sauce.
  • A blender works if you keep it cold and do not overmix the basil into a dark paste.

  • Vegetables
  • trofie
  • trenette with potatoes and green beans
  • minestrone

Mole Poblano

Dried chiles, nuts, seeds, spices, and a little chocolate: deep, mildly hot, and complex.

45 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 6 cups

History

Mole poblano is the thick chile sauce of Puebla, served with turkey or chicken. It uses several dried chiles, a fried puree, nuts and seeds, spices, and a small amount of chocolate as a seasoning, not as a dessert. Picturesque stories about a convent inventing it for a visiting bishop are legends, not reliable history, and they are left off this page.

Cultural background

In Puebla and in Mexican celebrations more broadly, mole poblano is feast food. It is one mole among many. Oaxacan mole negro, mole verde, and pipián are different sauces.

Why it tastes this way

The layered taste is fried chiles, nuts, seeds, and spices, with a little chocolate as one seasoning among many. Puebla’s dried chiles and the fry in fat are why it tastes toasted and deep rather than like a chocolate drink.

How it is made

Dried chiles are fried and soaked, then blended with toasted nuts, seeds, spices, tomato or tomatillo, and chocolate, and the puree is fried in fat and thinned with stock.

Authentic touch. Fry the blended puree in fat until it darkens. That fry, not extra chocolate, is what makes mole taste cooked.

How it is used

Serve with poached or roasted turkey or chicken, sesame seeds on top, and rice or tortillas.

Field notes

  • Chocolate in mole poblano is one ingredient among many, used the way a spice is used.
  • Convent-invention tales are widely told and not treated here as fact.

Ingredients

  • 4 dried ancho chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 3 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 dried pasilla chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 tablespoons lard or neutral oil
  • 1/4 cup almonds
  • 2 tablespoons peanuts
  • 2 tablespoons sesame seeds
  • 1/4 cup raisins
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 2 tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 cups chicken stock, plus more to thin
  • 1 ounce Mexican chocolate, chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon cinnamon
  • Salt

Steps

  1. Toast the chiles in a dry pan until fragrant, soak them in hot water 20 minutes, and drain.
  2. Fry the nuts, sesame, and raisins in the fat and remove them. Fry the onion and tomato.
  3. Blend the chiles, fried ingredients, cinnamon, and 1 cup stock until smooth.
  4. Fry the puree in the remaining fat, stirring, for about 10 minutes.
  5. Add the rest of the stock and the chocolate. Simmer 40 minutes, stirring, until thick. Season with salt and thin with stock if needed.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mole negro of Oaxaca uses chilhuacle chiles and a darker, smokier method.
  • The chocolate is a seasoning. Adding a large amount turns it into a different sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Rice
  • Stews
  • turkey
  • chicken
  • rice
  • warm tortillas

Nước Chấm

Fish sauce, lime, sugar, garlic, and chile: salty, sour, sweet, and hot.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3/4 cup

History

Nước chấm is the everyday Vietnamese dipping sauce built on nước mắm, fermented fish sauce. The balance of fish sauce, lime or vinegar, sugar, water, garlic, and chile is adjusted by region and by the dish. It is a composed sauce, not the fish sauce itself.

Cultural background

It is on the table for grilled pork, spring rolls, noodles, and rice plates across Vietnam. Northern versions are often a little less sweet than some southern ones. Families keep their own ratio.

Why it tastes this way

It tastes loud because it seasons mild rice and herbs. Fish sauce is the salt, lime is the acid, sugar softens both, and chile is the heat. Water is there so it is a dip, not a straight splash of fish sauce.

How it is made

Sugar dissolves in lime juice and water, then fish sauce, garlic, and chile are stirred in. Nothing is cooked.

Authentic touch. Dissolve the sugar in warm water first. Taste it on a piece of the food it will season, not from the spoon alone. It should be a little strong.

How it is used

Use it as a dip for grilled meats, summer rolls, and fried rolls, or spoon it over rice noodles.

Field notes

  • Nước mắm is the ingredient. Nước chấm is the dipping sauce made from it.
  • The sauce should taste a little too strong on its own, because it seasons mild rice and herbs.

Ingredients

  • 3 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 3 tablespoons lime juice
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/2 cup warm water
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1 bird’s eye chile, thinly sliced

Steps

  1. Stir the sugar into the warm water until it dissolves. Cool it.
  2. Stir in the fish sauce and lime juice.
  3. Add the garlic and chile.
  4. Taste and adjust the lime, sugar, or fish sauce.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A carrot and daikon quick-pickle in the bowl is common.
  • Substituting soy for fish sauce makes a different sauce for people who do not eat fish.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Noodles
  • Rice
  • Grilling
  • grilled pork
  • summer rolls
  • bún
  • fried spring rolls
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
