Tex-Mex & the Borderlands

New Mexico chile, salsa, and a Central Texas mop.

Chile was grown in the Americas long before Spanish settlement. New Mexico’s red and green chile sauces are the borderland expression of that crop in this guide. Mexican salsas and mole are the related sauces across the border and are labeled Mexican.

Central Texas barbecue mop is a different history: German and Czech meat markets, salt, pepper, and a thin vinegar basting sauce. It is in this book so it is not mistaken for a salsa.

New Mexico Red Chile Sauce

Dried New Mexico chile: earthy, mildly hot, and smooth.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

New Mexico red chile sauce is made from dried chile pods grown in the state, simmered and blended. It is the red half of the local question, red or green. It is not a bean chili con carne. The chile is the sauce.

Cultural background

It covers enchiladas, eggs, and burritos. Heat depends on the pod. Toasting and a short simmer bring out sweetness. A heavy cumin hand is more Texas chili than this sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Sun-dried New Mexico chiles taste earthy and sweet. The sauce is the chile itself, loosened with water and garlic. Cumin is optional and easy to overdo.

How it is made

Dried pods are stemmed, simmered, and blended with garlic and salt.

Authentic touch. Use dried New Mexico pods if you can. Toast lightly, simmer, and blend. Keep cumin out or use a pinch.

How it is used

Ladle it over enchiladas, eggs, or burritos.

Field notes

  • Red or green is a local choice, not a minor garnish.
  • The sauce can be just chile, water, garlic, and salt.

Ingredients

  • 8 dried New Mexico chiles
  • 3 cups water
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stem and seed the chiles. Toast them briefly.
  2. Simmer in the water 15 minutes.
  3. Blend with garlic and salt.
  4. Simmer 5 minutes more.
  5. Serve.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green chile sauce is the other New Mexico sauce.
  • Texas chili con carne is a meat stew.

New Mexico Green Chile Sauce

Roasted green chile and onion: smoky, vegetal, and hot.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

New Mexico green chile sauce is roasted long green chiles, peeled and simmered with onion and garlic. Hatch and other New Mexico varieties are the local crop. The roast is seasonal. Frozen roasted chile is how cooks keep the flavor.

Cultural background

It is stew, enchilada sauce, and a blanket for burgers. The skin must be charred and peeled. Unpeeled skin tastes bitter and papery.

Why it tastes this way

Flame-roasting adds smoke and sweetness. The chile’s own heat is the spice. A long simmer with onion makes a sauce rather than a bowl of chopped peppers.

How it is made

Roasted, peeled green chiles simmer with onion, garlic, and a little stock.

Authentic touch. Char the chiles, steam them, and peel every strip of skin. Then simmer the flesh with onion.

How it is used

Spoon it over eggs, pork, or enchiladas.

Field notes

  • Peeling the charred skin is not optional.
  • The flavor is the chile, so the variety matters.

Ingredients

  • 8 roasted New Mexico green chiles, peeled and chopped
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 cup water
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Cook the onion and garlic in the oil.
  2. Add the chiles, water, and salt.
  3. Simmer 15 minutes.
  4. Mash some of the chile for body.
  5. Serve.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red chile is the dried-pod sauce.
  • A flour thickener makes it more like a gravy.

Salsa Roja

Roasted tomato, chile, and garlic: smoky, savory, and hot.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Salsa roja is a cooked Mexican red sauce of tomato or tomatillo’s red cousins, dried or fresh chile, and garlic. Roasting or boiling the vegetables, then frying the puree, is a common path. It is a table salsa and a taco sauce, not a long Italian tomato sauce.

Cultural background

Taquerías and home kitchens both keep a red salsa. The chile chosen, árbol, guajillo, or jalapeño, decides the heat. The fry after blending is what makes many red salsas taste cooked rather than raw.

Why it tastes this way

Charring tomato and chile adds smoke. Frying the blended salsa in a little fat cooks off the raw taste and concentrates it. Watery boiled tomato without that fry tastes thin.

How it is made

Tomato, chile, onion, and garlic are charred, blended, and fried briefly.

Authentic touch. Roast or char the vegetables, blend, then fry the puree for a few minutes until it darkens.

How it is used

Spoon it on tacos, eggs, or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • The fry after blending is a usual step, not a mistake.
  • Red salsa is a category. The chile changes the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 4 ripe tomatoes
  • 2 jalapeños
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1/4 onion
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil

Steps

  1. Char the tomatoes, jalapeños, garlic, and onion in a dry pan or under a broiler.
  2. Blend with salt until coarse.
  3. Fry the puree in the oil 5 minutes.
  4. Taste for salt.
  5. Serve warm or cool.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A boiled salsa is milder and less smoky.
  • Árbol chile makes it hotter.

Salsa Verde

Tomatillo, chile, and cilantro: tart, bright, and hot.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Mexican salsa verde is built on tomatillos, not on Italian parsley. Tomatillos are husked, boiled or roasted, and blended with chile, onion, and often cilantro. The sauce for enchiladas verdes and for tacos is this family.

Cultural background

The tart taste is the tomatillo itself. Cilantro is common and not universal. Roasting makes it sweeter and smokier. Boiling makes it brighter.

Why it tastes this way

Tomatillos are naturally tart, which is why salsa verde tastes sour even before lime. Chile brings heat. A fry or a simmer softens the raw edge.

How it is made

Tomatillos and chile are cooked, then blended with onion, garlic, and cilantro.

Authentic touch. Husk and rinse the tomatillos. The sticky coat is bitter if you leave it on. Then boil or roast them until they soften.

How it is used

Use it on enchiladas, chicken, or tacos.

Field notes

  • The green is tomatillo, not parsley.
  • Rinse off the sticky husk residue before cooking.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound tomatillos, husked and rinsed
  • 2 jalapeños
  • 1/4 onion
  • 1 garlic clove
  • 1/2 cup cilantro
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer the tomatillos and jalapeños in water until soft, about 10 minutes.
  2. Blend with onion, garlic, cilantro, and salt.
  3. Fry the salsa 3 minutes if you want it rounder.
  4. Taste.
  5. Serve.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Roasted tomatillo salsa is smokier.
  • Italian salsa verde is parsley and is a different sauce.

Guacamole

Ripe avocado, lime, onion, and chile: rich, fresh, and bright.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Guacamole is mashed avocado seasoned with lime or salt, onion, chile, and often cilantro and tomato. Avocado sauces are old in Mesoamerican cooking. The word comes from Nahuatl ahuacamolli, avocado sauce. It is a dip and a sauce for grilled meat and tacos.

Cultural background

Ripe fruit is the body. Lime slows darkening and adds brightness. A mortar gives a coarser, more traditional texture than a blender.

Why it tastes this way

Avocado fat tastes rich and mild, so lime, salt, onion, and chile are what make it savory. Over-blending whips in air and makes it fluffy and pale instead of dense.

How it is made

Avocado is mashed with salt, lime, onion, chile, and cilantro.

Authentic touch. Mash ripe avocados with a fork, leaving some texture. Fold in onion, chile, cilantro, and lime. Do not use a long blend.

How it is used

Eat it with tortillas, tacos, or grilled meat the day it is made.

Field notes

  • The name is from Nahuatl for avocado sauce.
  • Lime and an airtight press slow browning. They do not stop it forever.

Ingredients

  • 3 ripe avocados
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 lime, juiced
  • 2 tablespoons minced onion
  • 1 jalapeño, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro

Steps

  1. Mash the avocados with salt and lime, leaving lumps.
  2. Fold in the onion, jalapeño, and cilantro.
  3. Taste.
  4. Press plastic against the surface if it has to wait.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Tomatillo in the mash is a greener, tarter version.
  • A completely smooth blender guacamole is a different texture.

Pico de Gallo

Raw tomato, onion, chile, and lime: fresh, sharp, and juicy.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 3 cups

History

Pico de gallo is a chopped raw salsa of tomato, white onion, chile, cilantro, and lime. It is not cooked. The name’s folk stories are not solid enough to repeat. The method is a fresh relish, eaten the day it is made.

Cultural background

It sits beside grilled meat, beans, and tacos. Because nothing is cooked, ripe tomato and a sharp onion matter. Lime and salt draw out the juice that becomes the sauce.

Why it tastes this way

No cook means the tomato stays bright and the onion stays sharp. Lime juice salts the mixture and pulls out liquid, so it tastes juicy rather than like dry dice.

How it is made

Tomato, onion, chile, cilantro, lime, and salt are chopped and rested.

Authentic touch. Salt it and let it sit 15 minutes. Chop the onion fine. Serve it the same day. It does not keep like a cooked salsa.

How it is used

Eat it with tacos, grilled meat, or beans.

Field notes

  • It is raw. Cooking it makes a different salsa.
  • The juice after a short rest is part of the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 3 ripe tomatoes, diced
  • 1/2 white onion, finely diced
  • 1 jalapeño, minced
  • 1/2 cup cilantro, chopped
  • 1 lime, juiced
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Combine everything.
  2. Rest 15 minutes.
  3. Taste for salt and lime.
  4. Drain nothing. The juice stays.
  5. Serve the same day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Serrano is hotter than jalapeño.
  • A mango pico is a different, sweeter relish.

Chipotle in Adobo

Smoked jalapeño, vinegar, and tomato: smoky, sour, and hot.

10 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 10 · about 2 cups

History

Chipotle is a smoked ripe jalapeño. Packed in adobo, it sits in a sauce of vinegar, tomato, garlic, and spices. Mexican adobo here means that chile sauce, not Filipino vinegar-soy adobo. Canned chipotles are the pantry form of this preserve.

Cultural background

A spoon of the sauce seasons mayo, beans, and braises. The smoke is from the chile, so liquid smoke is unnecessary if the chipotles are real.

Why it tastes this way

Smoke-drying the jalapeño is why chipotle tastes smoky rather than green and fresh. Vinegar and tomato make a tart sauce around the fruit. The seeds and veins carry extra heat.

How it is made

Smoked chiles simmer in vinegar, tomato, garlic, and spices until soft.

Authentic touch. Simmer dried chipotles or use smoked jalapeños until they are soft in the vinegar-tomato sauce. Taste before adding salt.

How it is used

Use the chiles and the sauce in marinades, beans, or a spoon on tacos.

Field notes

  • Chipotle means a smoked chile, usually jalapeño.
  • Mexican adobo and Filipino adobo are not the same word in the kitchen.

Ingredients

  • 8 dried chipotle chiles or canned smoked jalapeños
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/4 cup vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil

Steps

  1. Simmer the chiles in water 15 minutes if they are dry.
  2. Blend half of them with vinegar, tomato, garlic, oregano, salt, and oil.
  3. Return the puree and whole chiles to the pot and simmer 10 minutes.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Filipino adobo is a different sauce.
  • Morita chiles are a fruitier smoked jalapeño.

Pairings and uses

  • Marinades
  • Tacos and tortillas
  • Stews
  • Meat
  • beans
  • pork
  • mayonnaise for a chipotle crema

Texas Mop Sauce

Vinegar, black pepper, and beef drippings: thin, peppery, and savory.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Central Texas barbecue often serves brisket with salt, pepper, and smoke, and uses a thin mop of vinegar, pepper, and drippings rather than a sweet tomato glaze. The mop bastes the meat. It is not a Kansas City sauce.

Cultural background

The pepper crust on the meat is the seasoning. The mop adds moisture and a sharp note. A thick sweet sauce would cover the beef.

Why it tastes this way

Black pepper and vinegar taste sharp. Beef drippings, if you have them from the cook, add savor. There is little sugar, so it does not taste like a glaze.

How it is made

Vinegar, water, pepper, and a spoon of drippings or oil are combined.

Authentic touch. Mop the meat during a long cook. Keep the sauce thin. Use drippings from the beef if the cook produced them.

How it is used

Baste brisket or serve a little on the side.

Field notes

  • Central Texas flavor is smoke, salt, and pepper. The mop is thin on purpose.
  • A sweet red sauce is a different region.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cider vinegar
  • 1 cup water
  • 2 tablespoons black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons beef drippings or oil
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne

Steps

  1. Warm everything until the salt dissolves.
  2. Do not thicken it.
  3. Mop meat during cooking.
  4. Keep the rest warm.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A tomato mop is more East Texas than Central Texas.
  • Salt and pepper on the meat matter more than the mop.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue
  • Meat
  • brisket
  • sausage
  • white bread
