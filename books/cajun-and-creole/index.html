Cajun & Creole Louisiana

Dark roux, Creole tomato, and a spicy rémoulade.

Creole cooking in New Orleans drew on French and Spanish colonial kitchens, West African cooking carried by enslaved people, Caribbean links, and Native American ingredients such as filé. Cajun cooking grew among Acadian exiles in rural southwest Louisiana. The usual distinction is city tomato and butter against a darker rural roux. It is a useful distinction, not a wall.

This book holds the Louisiana sauces in the guide: Creole sauce, Louisiana rémoulade, cayenne hot sauce, and Cajun brown gravy. Gumbo is a stew and is not rewritten here as a sauce.

Creole Sauce

Tomato, onion, celery, and bell pepper: savory, sweet, and lightly herbal.

15 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Creole sauce is the tomato sauce of Louisiana built on onion, celery, and bell pepper, cooked with garlic and thyme and served with shrimp. The three vegetables are the local aromatic base. It is a tomato sauce, distinct from a brown roux étouffée.

Cultural background

Shrimp Creole is the dish, eaten with rice. The vegetables should cook until soft before the tomato goes in. Cayenne heats it. The sauce should taste of pepper and celery, not only tomato.

Why it tastes this way

Bell pepper, celery, and onion cooked in oil taste sweet and savory. Tomato and thyme make a round sauce. A short cook keeps it brighter than a long Sunday gravy.

How it is made

The aromatic vegetables soften, then tomato, garlic, and thyme simmer.

Authentic touch. Cook the onion, celery, and pepper until soft before you add tomato. That base is the flavor.

How it is used

Serve it with shrimp and rice.

Field notes

  • Onion, celery, and green pepper are the Louisiana aromatic trio in this sauce.
  • It is tomato-based, unlike a brown étouffée.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 1 celery stalk, chopped
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 can (28 ounces) tomatoes, crushed
  • 1 teaspoon thyme
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Cook the onion, pepper, and celery in the oil until soft.
  2. Add the garlic, tomatoes, thyme, cayenne, and salt.
  3. Simmer 25 minutes.
  4. Add cooked shrimp at the end if you are making the dish.
  5. Serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A roux makes it closer to étouffée.
  • More cayenne is a household choice.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Rice
  • Stews
  • shrimp
  • rice
  • Dairy-free
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Louisiana Rémoulade

Mayonnaise, Creole mustard, and paprika: spicy, tangy, and pink.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Louisiana rémoulade is a spicy mayonnaise sauce for cold shrimp and fried seafood, seasoned with mustard, paprika, and often horseradish and hot sauce. It is sharper and redder than French rémoulade, which is herbs and capers. Creole mustard is the local grainy mustard.

Cultural background

Shrimp rémoulade is a New Orleans starter. The sauce should taste of mustard and paprika, not only of mayonnaise. A chill lets it thicken.

Why it tastes this way

Creole mustard is sharp. Paprika and cayenne add color and heat. Lemon and a little horseradish keep it bright. The French herb rémoulade is a cousin with a quieter spice list.

How it is made

Mayonnaise, mustard, paprika, and lemon are stirred together.

Authentic touch. Use a grainy Creole or whole-grain mustard and paprika. Chill the sauce before you dress cold shrimp.

How it is used

Spoon it over cold shrimp or use it as a fried-seafood dip.

Field notes

  • New Orleans rémoulade is a spiced mayonnaise, not the French herb sauce.
  • Paprika is the color.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise
  • 2 tablespoons whole-grain mustard
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1 teaspoon prepared horseradish
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon hot sauce
  • 1 scallion, minced
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir everything together.
  2. Chill 30 minutes.
  3. Taste for mustard and heat.
  4. Serve with shrimp.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • French rémoulade is herbier and less red.
  • Horseradish makes it sharper.

Pairings and uses

  • Seafood
  • Sandwiches and burgers
  • Dipping
  • cold shrimp
  • fried fish
  • lettuce
  • Vegetarian
  • Nut-free

Louisiana-Style Hot Sauce

Cayenne and vinegar: thin, sharp, and hot.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 20 · about 1 cup

History

Louisiana hot sauce is aged or simmered cayenne in vinegar and salt. The McIlhenny family on Avery Island began selling Tabasco sauce, made from tabasco peppers, in 1868, and that bottle defined the style for many eaters. This recipe is a weeknight cayenne-vinegar sauce in the Louisiana style. It is not the aged barrel formula of any brand.

Cultural background

It is on the table for oysters, greens, eggs, and beans. Thinness is correct. A thick chile paste is a different family.

Why it tastes this way

Cayenne and vinegar, simmered and strained, taste thin, sharp, and hot. Thickness is not the goal. The vinegar is why it seasons oysters and greens without covering them.

How it is made

Cayenne, garlic, vinegar, and salt simmer, then the sauce is blended and strained.

Authentic touch. Strain it if you want it to pour. Leave it a day in the refrigerator. The flavor settles.

How it is used

Shake it on oysters, greens, eggs, or rice and beans.

Field notes

  • The style is thin and vinegar-forward. Thickness is not a flaw to correct.
  • A brand name on a bottle is not the name of the whole style.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup fresh red cayenne peppers, stemmed
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 3/4 cup distilled white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Simmer the peppers, garlic, vinegar, salt, and water 10 minutes.
  2. Blend until smooth.
  3. Strain if you want it pourable from a bottle.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate. The flavor settles after a day.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fermented cayenne mash is the commercial path and takes months.
  • Crystal-style sauces lean on cayenne. Tabasco-style sauces use tabasco peppers. Both are Louisiana vinegar sauces.

Pairings and uses

  • Dipping
  • Seafood
  • Eggs
  • Rice
  • raw or grilled oysters
  • greens
  • scrambled eggs
  • red beans
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Cajun Brown Gravy

Dark roux, onion, and stock: toasted, savory, and peppery.

10 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 6 · about 4 cups

History

Cajun rice and gravy is a southwest Louisiana pot of meat in a dark roux gravy. The roux is the French technique cooked much darker than a béchamel, until it smells of toasted nuts. Onion, celery, and bell pepper season it. This is the gravy, not a whole gumbo, and it is not the tomato Creole sauce of New Orleans.

Cultural background

Cajun cooking grew among Acadian exiles in rural southwest Louisiana. A dark roux gravy over rice is everyday food there. Creole city cooking more often uses tomato and butter. The two are related and not the same pot.

Why it tastes this way

The longer the flour cooks in fat, the less it thickens and the more it tastes toasted. That is why this gravy is nutty and looser than a pale French sauce. Stock and the pepper trinity make it savory. Cayenne is a small heat, not the body.

How it is made

A dark roux is loosened with stock, seasoned with onion, celery, and pepper, and simmered.

Authentic touch. Stir the roux constantly and take it to a brown the color of peanut butter or darker. If it smells sharp, it has burned and you should start again.

How it is used

Serve it over rice, with or without the meat that cooked in it.

Field notes

  • A dark roux is a French method pushed further in Cajun pots.
  • Color is flavor. Pale roux will not taste like this gravy.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup oil
  • 1/2 cup flour
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 celery stalk, chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, chopped
  • 4 cups chicken or pork stock
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir the oil and flour over medium heat until the roux is brown and smells toasted, about 15 to 25 minutes.
  2. Add the onion, celery, and pepper and cook until soft.
  3. Whisk in the stock.
  4. Simmer 15 minutes.
  5. Add cayenne and salt. Serve over rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Creole sauce is the tomato city cousin.
  • A gumbo uses a similar roux and is a stew, not this gravy.

Pairings and uses

  • Meat
  • Rice
  • Stews
  • rice
  • pork
  • chicken
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free
