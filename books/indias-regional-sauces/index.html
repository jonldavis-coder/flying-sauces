Sambar

Toor dal, tamarind, and sambar powder: sour, savory, and warm.

15 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 cups

History

Sambar is the lentil and vegetable stew of South India, soured with tamarind and spiced with a sambar powder that includes chile, coriander, and often fenugreek. It is eaten with rice and with idli and dosa. Stories about a Maratha cook inventing it in Thanjavur are local legend and are not repeated here as fact.

Cultural background

A South Indian meal can put sambar on rice at lunch and next to breakfast dosas. Vegetables change with the market. The tamarind sourness is the constant.

Why it tastes this way

Toor dal tastes savory, tamarind tastes sour, and sambar powder heats it. The sourness is why it seasons rice. A dal without tamarind is a different stew.

How it is made

Toor dal is boiled, then simmered with tamarind, vegetables, and sambar powder, and finished with a mustard and curry-leaf tadka.

Authentic touch. Simmer the tamarind in. Taste for sour. Finish with a hot tadka of mustard seed and curry leaf.

How it is used

Ladle it over rice or serve it beside idli and dosa.

Field notes

  • Tamarind is what makes sambar sour. A dal without it is a different stew.
  • The origin tale about a royal cook is legend, not a sourced fact on this page.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup toor dal
  • 4 cups water
  • 1 tablespoon tamarind paste
  • 1 tablespoon sambar powder
  • 1 cup diced carrot and onion
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 teaspoon mustard seeds
  • 8 curry leaves

Steps

  1. Boil the dal in the water until soft, about 30 minutes.
  2. Stir in the tamarind, sambar powder, vegetables, and salt. Simmer 12 minutes.
  3. Heat the oil, pop the mustard seeds, and fry the curry leaves.
  4. Pour the tadka into the sambar.
  5. Serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • The vegetable changes the pot. Drumstick and okra are classic.
  • Rasam is the thinner pepper-tamarind cousin.

Rasam

Tamarind, pepper, cumin, and garlic: thin, sour, and hot.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Rasam is the thin, sour South Indian broth of tamarind, black pepper, cumin, and often tomato and garlic, poured over rice or drunk from a cup. It is thinner than sambar and does not rely on a large amount of dal. Pepper rasam is one of the older styles.

Cultural background

It is everyday food and also food for someone who wants something light. The tadka of mustard, cumin, and curry leaf finishes it.

Why it tastes this way

Tamarind and black pepper in a thin broth taste sour and hot. It is thin so it soaks into rice. Thickness would make it a stew, not rasam.

How it is made

Tamarind water, tomato, and crushed pepper simmer, then a hot spice tempering is poured in.

Authentic touch. Keep it thin. Crush the pepper so the heat is in the broth. Add the tempering at the end.

How it is used

Pour it over rice, or serve it in a cup beside the meal.

Field notes

  • Rasam is supposed to be thin. If it is stew-thick, it has become something else.
  • Black pepper, not only chile, is a traditional heat.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons tamarind paste
  • 4 cups water
  • 1 tomato, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon crushed black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 2 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon mustard seeds
  • 6 curry leaves

Steps

  1. Simmer the tamarind, water, tomato, pepper, cumin, garlic, and salt 12 minutes.
  2. Heat the oil, pop the mustard, and fry the curry leaves.
  3. Pour the tempering into the rasam.
  4. Taste. It should be sour and peppery.
  5. Serve over rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Tomato rasam leans on tomato. Pepper rasam leans on black pepper.
  • A little cooked dal water makes a heartier pot.

Kasundi

Mustard, green mango or vinegar, and chile: sharp, hot, and pungent.

15 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 12 · about 1 cup

History

Kasundi is the Bengali mustard sauce, traditionally a fermented mustard relish made when mustard seed is plentiful. Household ferments vary and can be tricky to keep safe. This is a cooked mustard-chile sauce in the kasundi flavor, not a claim to reproduce a long home ferment.

Cultural background

It is eaten with rice and fish, with cutlets, and as a sharp table sauce. Mustard heat, not only chile, is the burn.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard seed tastes hot in a different way from chile. Vinegar sharpens it. This cooked paste echoes kasundi. A true ferment is longer and is not what this pot claims to be.

How it is made

Mustard seeds are soaked and blended with vinegar, chile, and turmeric, then cooked briefly.

Authentic touch. Soak the seeds, blend them, and fry the paste briefly. Rest it. Mustard mellows.

How it is used

Serve a spoonful with fried fish, cutlets, or rice.

Field notes

  • Kasundi is a mustard sauce from Bengal, hotter and grainier than Dijon.
  • This recipe is a cooked version and says so, rather than a pretend ferment.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup brown mustard seeds, soaked 2 hours
  • 4 dried red chiles, soaked
  • 1/4 cup vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Drain the seeds and chiles and blend with vinegar, turmeric, and salt.
  2. Fry the paste in the oil over low heat for 6 minutes, stirring.
  3. Cool.
  4. It should be sharp and thick.
  5. Refrigerate.

Refrigerate and use within two weeks. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A traditional ferment takes longer and needs careful salting. This cooked paste is the weeknight version.
  • Green mango, in season, is a classic sour note.

Pairings and uses

Coconut Chutney

Coconut, green chile, and curry leaf: fresh, mild, and aromatic.

15 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 6 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Coconut chutney is the white chutney of South Indian breakfasts, served with dosa and idli. Fresh coconut is ground with green chile, ginger, and salt, then finished with a tadka of mustard seeds and curry leaves. Roasted chana dal is a common addition and is left out here.

Cultural background

A dosa plate without coconut chutney and sambar is incomplete in many homes. The tempering is poured on at the end so the curry leaves stay fragrant.

Why it tastes this way

Fresh coconut tastes sweet and mild. Green chile heats it, and a hot tempering of mustard seed and curry leaf perfumes the top. Roasted dal is left out here, so the coconut stays the main flavor.

How it is made

Coconut, chile, and ginger are blended with water, then hot oil, mustard seed, and curry leaf are poured over.

Authentic touch. Pour the hot tadka over the chutney just before serving. Stale curry leaves taste flat.

How it is used

Serve it with dosa or idli.

Field notes

  • The tadka of curry leaf and mustard seed is part of the chutney, not a garnish you can skip casually.
  • Fresh coconut, not sweetened coconut flakes, is the ingredient.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup grated fresh coconut
  • 1 green chile
  • 1/2 inch ginger
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 teaspoon oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon mustard seeds
  • 6 curry leaves

Steps

  1. Blend the coconut, chile, ginger, salt, and water until coarse.
  2. Heat the oil, pop the mustard seeds, and fry the curry leaves a few seconds.
  3. Pour the oil over the chutney.
  4. Stir.
  5. Serve the same day with dosa.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of yogurt makes a different, dairy chutney.
  • Roasted dal thickens some family recipes.

Mint-Coriander Chutney

Cilantro, mint, green chile, and lemon: fresh, hot, and herbal.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Green chutney of cilantro, mint, chile, and lime or lemon is a standard Indian table sauce, served with snacks, grilled meats, and rice. Recipes differ by region: some add roasted cumin, some coconut, some yogurt. This is the unsweetened herb chutney, not tamarind chutney.

Cultural background

It is the bright sauce next to samosas, kebabs, and chaat, often paired with a sweet tamarind chutney. Both are expected. Neither replaces the other.

Why it tastes this way

Raw cilantro, mint, green chile, and lemon taste fresh because nothing is cooked. It is the bright sauce next to a sweet tamarind chutney. Cooking it would make a different, duller sauce.

How it is made

Herbs, chile, lemon, and salt are blended with a little water. A brief rest dulls the raw garlic if you use it.

Authentic touch. Use stems as well as leaves if they are tender, and salt it enough. A timid green chutney disappears next to fried snacks.

How it is used

Serve with grilled meat, pakoras, or dosas, or spread it in a sandwich.

Field notes

  • Sweet tamarind chutney and this green chutney are a pair in chaat, not substitutes.
  • The chutney is uncooked. Browning it would make a different sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro leaves
  • 1/2 cup mint leaves
  • 1 or 2 green chiles
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons water

Steps

  1. Blend all the ingredients to a coarse sauce.
  2. Add water only if the blades will not turn.
  3. Taste for salt and lemon.
  4. Serve the same day.
  5. A darker top is oxidation. Stir it under.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Coconut chutney is the South Indian breakfast sauce and a different recipe.
  • Yogurt makes a milder dip.

Tamarind-Date Chutney

Tamarind, date, and jaggery: sour, sweet, and dark.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Sweet tamarind chutney, often called saunth, is the dark dip served with chaat across North India. Tamarind pulp, dates or jaggery, and cumin simmer into a pourable sauce. It is the sweet partner to green herb chutney, not a substitute for it.

Cultural background

Chaat stalls set both chutneys on the counter. The tamarind one should be sour first and sweet second.

Why it tastes this way

Tamarind is sour, dates and jaggery are sweet, and cumin makes it savory. That sour-sweet taste is why it sits next to fried snacks and a hot green chutney.

How it is made

Tamarind, dates, jaggery, and cumin simmer and are strained or blended smooth.

Authentic touch. Simmer and blend until pourable. Taste for sour first. If it is only sweet, add tamarind.

How it is used

Drizzle it on chaat, samosas, or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • This is the sweet chaat chutney. The green one is the herbal chile sauce.
  • Tamarind is the sour. Dates and jaggery are the sweet.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons tamarind paste
  • 6 dates, pitted
  • 2 tablespoons jaggery or brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon chili powder

Steps

  1. Simmer everything 15 minutes.
  2. Blend until smooth and strain if needed.
  3. It should be pourable and sour-sweet.
  4. Cool.
  5. Refrigerate and use within a week.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Jaggery instead of dates is a simpler sweet-sour pot.
  • A pinch of black salt is the chaat finish.

Mango Chutney

Ripe or green mango, vinegar, ginger, and sugar: sweet, sour, and warm.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 12 · about 3 cups

History

Cooked mango chutney is an Indian preserve that British cooks adopted in the colonial period and sweetened into the jar now common with cheese and cold meat. Major Grey’s is a brand style, not a single historic recipe. This is a home cooked mango chutney with ginger and chile, for the refrigerator, not a canning formula.

Cultural background

In India, chutneys range from fresh to cooked. This cooked one is the style that traveled. It should stay chunky.

Why it tastes this way

Mango cooked with vinegar, ginger, and sugar tastes sweet and sour because the fruit concentrates and the vinegar preserves the sharpness. Green mango is sharper. Ripe mango is rounder.

How it is made

Mango, onion, vinegar, sugar, ginger, and chile simmer until thick.

Authentic touch. Cook until thick and chunky. This is a refrigerator chutney, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

How it is used

Serve it with dal, rice, cheese, or cold meat.

Field notes

  • The very sweet British jar and a sharper Indian home chutney are related and not identical.
  • This is a refrigerator chutney, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.

Ingredients

  • 3 mangoes, peeled and diced
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 3/4 cup vinegar
  • 1/2 cup sugar
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Simmer all the ingredients until the mango is soft and the pot is thick, about 35 minutes.
  2. Stir near the end.
  3. Cool.
  4. Refrigerate.
  5. Serve with rice or cold meat.

Refrigerate and use within a month. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Green mango makes a sharper chutney.
  • A fresh mint-mango relish is a different, uncooked sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Barbecue

Cucumber Raita

Yogurt, cucumber, and cumin: cool, salty, and lightly spiced.

15 min prep · 2 min cook · Serves 6 · about 2 cups

History

Raita is the Indian yogurt sauce served to cool a spicy meal. Cucumber raita is the most common. Roasted cumin and salt are the usual seasoning. Boondi raita, with fried chickpea drops, is a variation. The sauce is cold and is not cooked.

Cultural background

It sits beside biryani, kebabs, and any hot curry. The yogurt should be thick enough to spoon, not a thin lassi.

Why it tastes this way

Yogurt tastes cool and tangy next to a hot meal. Roasted cumin is the spice, not raw garlic. Cucumber water would thin it, so the cucumber is squeezed.

How it is made

Cucumber is salted, squeezed, and folded into yogurt with roasted cumin.

Authentic touch. Toast and crush the cumin. Salt the cucumber and squeeze it dry.

How it is used

Serve it cold with biryani or grilled meat.

Field notes

  • Raita is a cooling sauce. It is supposed to be mild next to a hot curry.
  • Roasted cumin is the usual spice, not a pile of raw garlic.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups plain yogurt
  • 1 cucumber, grated and squeezed
  • 1/2 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons chopped cilantro

Steps

  1. Toast the cumin in a dry pan until fragrant and crush it.
  2. Stir the yogurt, cucumber, cumin, salt, and cilantro.
  3. Rest 10 minutes.
  4. Taste for salt.
  5. Serve cold.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mint raita swaps most of the cucumber for herbs.
  • Boondi raita adds fried chickpea flour drops and is not gluten-free in the same way.

Pairings and uses

  • Rice
  • Grilling
  • Vegetables

Kadhi

Yogurt, gram flour, and turmeric: tangy, savory, and lightly spiced.

10 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Kadhi is the yogurt and chickpea-flour sauce of Gujarat, Punjab, and other regions, simmered with turmeric and finished with a cumin and curry-leaf tempering. Pakoras are often simmered in Punjabi kadhi. Gujarati kadhi is often thinner and a little sweet. This is a plain savory version.

Cultural background

It is eaten with rice. The gram flour keeps the yogurt from splitting if you whisk it well and cook it gently after the boil starts.

Why it tastes this way

Yogurt and chickpea flour taste tangy and savory. Turmeric colors it. The flour keeps the yogurt from splitting and thickens the sauce for rice. A tadka of cumin and curry leaf is the perfume.

How it is made

Yogurt and besan are whisked with turmeric and water, simmered, and finished with a tadka.

Authentic touch. Whisk the flour and yogurt smooth before any heat. Simmer until the raw flour taste is gone.

How it is used

Ladle it over rice.

Field notes

  • Besan, chickpea flour, is what stabilizes the yogurt.
  • The tempering is poured on at the end for the smell of cumin and curry leaf.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup plain yogurt
  • 2 tablespoons besan (chickpea flour)
  • 3 cups water
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 teaspoon cumin seeds
  • 6 curry leaves

Steps

  1. Whisk the yogurt, besan, water, turmeric, and salt until smooth.
  2. Simmer 15 minutes, stirring, until it no longer tastes raw.
  3. Heat the oil, pop the cumin, and fry the curry leaves.
  4. Pour the tadka into the kadhi.
  5. Serve with rice.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Gujarati kadhi often includes a little sugar and ginger.
  • Pakoras turned in the sauce are the Punjabi meal.

Pairings and uses

  • Rice
  • Vegetables
  • steamed rice
  • roti
  • pickle
  • Vegetarian

Vindaloo

Vinegar, garlic, and hot chile: sharp, hot, and savory.

20 min prep · 1 hr 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Goan vindaloo comes from the Portuguese carne de vinha d’alhos, meat in wine and garlic, adapted in Goa with vinegar, dried chiles, and local spices. The British curry-house vindaloo that is only a very hot tomato gravy is a different dish. This is the Goan vinegar and chile stew sauce.

Cultural background

Pork is the classic meat because of Goa’s Catholic cooking. The vinegar is the point. It should taste sour as well as hot.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar, garlic, and dried chile taste sour and hot because Goan cooks adapted a Portuguese wine-and-garlic marinade to vinegar and local chiles. A tomato gravy with extra chili is a different, British restaurant dish.

How it is made

A paste of chile, garlic, ginger, spices, and vinegar marinates pork, then the meat simmers in that sauce.

Authentic touch. Use enough vinegar that the sauce still tastes sour when the meat is tender. The sour is the point.

How it is used

Serve the pork and sauce with rice.

Field notes

  • Vindaloo is a Goan reading of a Portuguese wine-and-garlic marinade.
  • Vinegar is as important as the chile.

Ingredients

  • 6 dried red chiles, soaked
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon ginger
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 1 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1/4 cup vinegar
  • 1 pound pork shoulder, cubed
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Blend the chiles, garlic, ginger, cumin, turmeric, and vinegar into a paste.
  2. Coat the pork and rest 30 minutes.
  3. Fry the onion in the oil, add the pork and paste, and brown.
  4. Add the salt and water and simmer until the pork is tender, about 50 minutes.
  5. The sauce should be thick and sour.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Potato in the pot is common in some Goan homes.
  • The British restaurant version that is only extra chili in a tomato gravy is not this sauce.

Pairings and uses

  • Curries
  • Meat
  • Rice
  • pork
  • rice
  • plain yogurt on the side
  • Dairy-free
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Makhani Sauce

Tomato, butter, and cream, with mild spice: rich, sweet, and savory.

15 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 cups

History

Makhani, the butter sauce, is the tomato and butter sauce of butter chicken and dal makhani. Butter chicken is widely associated with the kitchens of Moti Mahal in Delhi in the mid-twentieth century. Accounts differ on which cook first simmered tandoori chicken in a tomato-butter gravy, so this page does not pick one person. The sauce is tomato, butter, and cream, lightly spiced.

Cultural background

It is restaurant food that became home food. The tomato should cook until sweet. Cream and butter go in late. It is milder than vindaloo or chettinad.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato cooked until sweet, finished with butter and cream, tastes rich and mild. The spices are light. Butter at the end is why it tastes like butter sauce rather than a dry tomato fry.

How it is made

Tomato, ginger, and garlic simmer with mild spice, then butter and cream finish the sauce.

Authentic touch. Cook the tomato a long time before the cream. Add butter and cream off a hard boil so they do not split.

How it is used

Simmer grilled chicken or paneer in it and serve with naan or rice.

Field notes

  • The Delhi restaurant origin is mid-twentieth century, and the individual credit is disputed.
  • Butter and cream are the finish. They are not fried at the start.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons butter
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • 2 cups tomato puree
  • 1 teaspoon garam masala
  • 1/2 teaspoon chili powder
  • 1/2 cup cream
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Cook the onion, ginger, and garlic in the butter until soft.
  2. Add the tomato paste, puree, garam masala, and chili and simmer 25 minutes.
  3. Blend until smooth.
  4. Stir in the cream, salt, and sugar and heat gently.
  5. Add cooked chicken or paneer and serve.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Dal makhani uses the same butter-tomato idea on lentils.
  • Chicken tikka masala is a British restaurant cousin and a separate page.

Pairings and uses

  • Curries
  • Rice
  • Meat
  • Grilling
  • tandoori chicken
  • paneer
  • naan

Mirchi ka Salan

Chile, peanut, sesame, and tamarind: nutty, tangy, and warm.

20 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 4 cups

History

Mirchi ka salan is the Hyderabadi chile curry served with biryani. Long chiles simmer in a sauce of peanut, sesame, coconut, and tamarind. It is a side curry, not a chutney. The chiles are often mild and are the vegetable, while smaller hot chiles can raise the heat.

Cultural background

A Hyderabadi biryani spread includes this gravy so the meal is not only rice and meat. The nut paste is the body.

Why it tastes this way

Peanut, sesame, and coconut make a nutty sauce. Tamarind makes it tangy. The long chiles are the vegetable, cooked mild, which is why it can sit beside biryani without overwhelming it.

How it is made

A paste of peanut, sesame, and coconut is fried with spice, then tamarind and whole chiles simmer in it.

Authentic touch. Toast and grind the nuts and seeds. Simmer the chiles until they are tender in that paste.

How it is used

Serve it beside biryani.

Field notes

  • The chiles are cooked as the vegetable of the curry.
  • It is a biryani accompaniment in Hyderabad, not a breakfast chutney.

Ingredients

  • 8 long mild green chiles, slit
  • 3 tablespoons peanuts
  • 1 tablespoon sesame seeds
  • 2 tablespoons grated coconut
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon turmeric
  • 1 tablespoon tamarind paste
  • 2 cups water
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Toast and grind the peanuts, sesame, and coconut.
  2. Fry the paste in the oil with the turmeric.
  3. Add the tamarind, water, salt, and chiles.
  4. Simmer until the chiles are tender and the sauce is thick, about 20 minutes.
  5. Serve with biryani.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mild banana chiles keep it a side. Hot chiles make it a hotter curry.
  • Coconut is traditional in many pots.

Cafreal Marinade

Cilantro, green chile, ginger, and spices: fresh, hot, and herbal.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 4 · about 1 cup

History

Cafreal is the Goan green marinade for chicken, associated with Portuguese-Goan cooking. A paste of cilantro, green chile, ginger, garlic, and spices coats the chicken before it is fried or grilled. It is not a tomato curry. Recheado is the red spice paste and a different Goan marinade.

Cultural background

Beach shacks and home kitchens both serve chicken cafreal. The color is green from the herb, not from spinach.

Why it tastes this way

A large amount of cilantro and green chile tastes fresh and hot. The paste cooks on the chicken, so the herb becomes a crust instead of a gravy. It is not a tomato curry.

How it is made

Cilantro and spices are blended into a paste that coats chicken before cooking.

Authentic touch. Coat the chicken thickly and let it sit. Grill or fry until the paste browns and the meat is cooked through.

How it is used

Marinate chicken, then grill or pan-fry it until cooked through.

Field notes

  • Cafreal is green because of cilantro, not food coloring.
  • It is a marinade that becomes the crust, not a gravy you pour on after.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cilantro
  • 4 green chiles
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon ginger
  • 1 teaspoon cumin
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon lime juice
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Blend everything into a paste.
  2. Coat chicken pieces and refrigerate at least 1 hour.
  3. Grill or pan-fry until the chicken is cooked through.
  4. Scrape the browned paste back over the meat.
  5. Serve with lime.

Cook the chicken through. Do not eat the raw marinade.

Refrigerate and use within a few days unless the recipe gives a different time. Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Recheado is the red vinegar paste for fish.
  • A spoon of lime in the paste brightens it.
