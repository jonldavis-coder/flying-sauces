Sauce 101

A short course in the techniques behind the recipes: mother sauces, emulsions, reductions, and thickeners.

What this guide means by sauce

A sauce is a seasoned liquid, puree, or emulsion served with food to add fat, acid, salt, heat, or aroma. Some are simmered for hours. Some are stirred in a bowl in the time it takes to set the table. A dry spice rub is not a sauce. A soup eaten on its own is not a sauce, even when a stew is thick enough to spoon over rice.

This field guide includes table sauces, cooking sauces, pastes that are sauces in concentrated form, and a few famous dips that are eaten the way a sauce is eaten. Each entry is a real, named sauce. Regional cousins are listed as variations unless cooks treat them as a different named sauce.

The five French mother sauces

Antoine Carême described a set of grand sauces in the early nineteenth century. Auguste Escoffier’s Le Guide Culinaire, at the turn of the twentieth century, taught the version cooks still memorize: five mother sauces, plus daughter sauces built from them by adding a flavor.

Béchamel is milk thickened with a white roux of butter and flour. Mornay adds cheese. Soubise adds onion.

Velouté is a light stock, usually chicken, fish, or veal, thickened with a blond roux. Sauce suprême reduces a chicken velouté with cream. Sauce allemande, later called sauce parisienne, finishes a veal velouté with egg yolk and cream.

Espagnole is brown stock thickened with a brown roux and flavored with tomato and a dark mirepoix. Demi-glace is espagnole reduced with more brown stock until it naps a spoon. Bordelaise and sauce Robert start from that brown-sauce family.

Sauce tomate, in the French mother-sauce sense, is a cooked tomato sauce, classically with salt pork, stock, and a roux or a long reduction. It is not the same recipe as Italian marinara, even though both are tomato sauces.

Hollandaise is egg yolk emulsified with butter and sharpened with lemon or vinegar. Béarnaise keeps the emulsion and flavors it with a shallot and tarragon reduction. Sauce Choron adds tomato. These are emulsions, and they are fragile: too much heat and the yolk scrambles.

Emulsions

An emulsion suspends fat in water, or water in fat, with help from an emulsifier. Egg yolk does this with lecithin. Mustard does it too. A vinaigrette without mustard is a temporary emulsion: it separates, and you shake it.

Mayonnaise is oil whipped into egg yolk with acid. Hollandaise is warm butter whipped into yolk. Aïoli, in the Provençal sense, is garlic pounded with olive oil. Many modern aïolis add egg. Toum is the Levantine garlic-and-oil emulsion, usually without egg, held together by the garlic itself.

If a warm emulsion breaks, stop heating it. Whisk a spoonful of warm water in a clean bowl and slowly whisk the broken sauce into it. If a mayonnaise breaks, start a new yolk or a teaspoon of mustard and whisk the broken sauce in drop by drop. These are repairs, not guarantees.

Reductions

A reduction is a liquid cooked until water leaves and flavor concentrates. A gastrique is caramelized sugar loosened with vinegar, the base of sauces such as bigarade. A pan sauce starts when you deglaze the browned bits in a pan with wine or stock, then reduce and finish with butter.

Glazes such as char siu, teriyaki, and unagi tare are reductions of soy, sugar, and mirin or honey. They burn quickly once the sugar concentrates. Brush them late, or thin them with a splash of water.

Thickeners

A roux is flour cooked in fat. White roux barely colors and thickens béchamel. Blond roux goes a little further for velouté. Brown roux cooks until it smells like toasted nuts and thickens gumbo and espagnole with less power and more flavor.

A slurry is starch whisked into cold water, then stirred into a simmering liquid. Cornstarch, potato starch, and arrowroot are common. It turns clear and glossy, which is why it suits many East Asian sauces.

Beurre manié is equal parts soft butter and flour kneaded together and whisked into a hot sauce at the end. A liaison is egg yolk and cream tempered with hot sauce and returned to the pot below a boil. Purees of nut, bean, pepper, or tomato thicken without starch. So does an emulsion of butter or oil.

Nappé is the French word for a sauce that coats the back of a spoon. It is a thickness, not a virtue by itself. Some of the best sauces in this guide are thin on purpose: North Carolina vinegar sauce, nuoc cham, a salsa, a vinaigrette.

Heat, base type, and diet on these pages

Heat describes chili, not black pepper or mustard. “No chili heat” means the classic recipe is not a chili sauce. Mustard and horseradish can still feel hot.

Base type is the main family used for browsing: emulsion, mother sauce, salsa, chutney, curry, barbecue, and the rest. A sauce can belong to more than one family in the kitchen. The page picks one so the filters stay usable.

Diet tags are about the recipe as written. Soy sauce made with wheat is not tagged gluten-free. Fish sauce is not vegetarian. A peanut sauce is not nut-free. If a commercial product you buy differs, read that label. These tags are not a medical diet.

Fermented staples

Soy sauce, fish sauce, miso, gochujang, doubanjiang, and oyster sauce are made by fermentation that takes weeks or months, often in a factory or a dedicated workshop. This guide does not pretend a half-hour recipe is that ferment.

Where a page gives a home version of Worcestershire, hoisin, oyster sauce, or kecap manis, the history says so. The recipe is a kitchen reconstruction of the flavor, built from the fermented staple or from anchovy, molasses, and vinegar. It is not the industrial product.

Food safety, briefly

Cook meat and poultry to temperatures you trust. Do not keep garlic-in-oil, or a fresh garlic emulsion, at room temperature. Refrigerate mayonnaise, hollandaise, and other egg sauces and use them the day you make them, or use pasteurized eggs if you need a longer hold.

Bitter cassava can contain cyanogenic compounds until it is properly processed. Recipes here that call for cassareep or tucupi mean the commercial product. They are not instructions for processing raw bitter cassava.

Chutneys and relishes in this guide are refrigerator sauces. They are not home-canning recipes and they are not shelf-stable.
